# California Counts: The Guard Union
## What 28,000 correctional officers, $120,000 average salaries, a $14 billion CDCR budget, $50 million in political contributions over three decades, and a union that has opposed nearly every major criminal justice reform reveal about the organized labor force that has most consistently shaped California's incarceration policy

California's prisons are staffed by approximately 28,000 correctional officers represented by the California Correctional Peace Officers Association, one of the most politically influential unions in a state that has no shortage of politically influential unions. CCPOA represents the officers who lock and unlock cells, manage yards, escort inmates to medical appointments, and work mandatory overtime when a colleague calls in sick. They are also, in aggregate, among the highest-paid correctional officers in the nation, with average salaries in the $100,000 to $120,000 range before overtime. And their union has spent an estimated $50 million on California elections over three decades, making CCPOA one of the largest political spenders in state history, with a consistent focus on opposing criminal justice reforms that would reduce the incarcerated population and, therefore, the staffing requirements that justify CCPOA's bargaining power.

---

## The Workforce: 28,000 Officers, The Highest Salaries in the Nation

California's Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation employs approximately 50,000 total staff, of whom roughly 28,000 are correctional officers represented by CCPOA. The remaining employees include parole agents, counselors, medical staff, administrative personnel, and executives. The correctional officer classification is the core of CDCR's workforce, and it is expensive.

The average salary for a California correctional officer in 2025 is approximately $100,000 to $120,000 per year, not counting overtime. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports the national median for correctional officers as approximately $49,610 per year. California's officers earn roughly twice the national median in base salary. Including overtime, which is substantial and will be addressed in the next California Counts installment, many California correctional officers earn $150,000 to $200,000 or more in total annual compensation. Transparent California, which publishes state and local government pay data, shows dozens of CDCR employees with total compensation exceeding $200,000 annually, primarily driven by overtime in the higher-paying positions.

Total personnel costs for CDCR are approximately $6 billion to $7 billion per year, representing roughly 45 to 50 percent of the department's $14 billion annual budget. Salaries and benefits for CCPOA-represented employees account for the majority of that personnel cost. The CCPOA contract, negotiated with the Department of Human Resources and approved by the Legislature, determines base pay scales, overtime rules, work schedules, disciplinary procedures, and a range of other conditions that have significant fiscal consequences for the state.

## The Budget: $14 Billion, 8 Percent of the General Fund

CDCR's total budget for fiscal year 2025-26 is approximately $14 billion to $16 billion, making it the third- or fourth-largest department in California state government by expenditure, behind only Medi-Cal, K-12 education, and the UC/CSU systems. As a share of the General Fund, CDCR consumes approximately 8 percent of available state tax revenues, a share that has declined from a peak of approximately 9.4 percent in 2007 as the state budget has grown and the prison population has declined.

The $14 billion CDCR budget is frequently cited in comparative context: California spends more on its prison system than most states spend on their entire state budgets. The per-incarcerated-person cost has reached approximately $132,860 per year, the highest in the nation by a substantial margin. The national average for state prison systems is approximately $45,000 per person per year. California's cost is roughly three times the national average, driven by high salaries, extensive overtime, expensive medical care, federal court mandates, and pension obligations for retired officers.

The size and cost of California's corrections system is not separable from CCPOA's political influence. The union's power in Sacramento has historically supported staffing ratios, classification expansions, and salary increases that collectively produce the personnel cost structure that drives CDCR's budget. That influence has operated not primarily through formal lobbying (though CCPOA does maintain a substantial lobbying presence) but through electoral political spending that has shaped which governors, legislators, and ballot measure outcomes have prevailed.

## The Political Machine: $50 Million Over Three Decades

CCPOA has contributed an estimated $50 million or more to California political campaigns and ballot measure committees over the three decades since it became a major political spender in the early 1990s. The spending has been consistently directed at outcomes that protect or expand the correctional officer workforce. CCPOA opposed Proposition 36 (2012), the initiative that would have softened Three Strikes; it eventually passed 69 to 31 percent, over CCPOA opposition. CCPOA opposed Proposition 47 (2014), which reclassified drug and property felonies as misdemeanors; it passed 59 to 41 percent, over CCPOA opposition. CCPOA opposed Proposition 57 (2016), which expanded parole consideration for nonviolent offenders; it passed 64 to 36 percent, over CCPOA opposition.

The union's record on ballot measures is not one of consistent success: California voters approved all three of those decarceration initiatives despite CCPOA's opposition and spending. But the political spending has not been primarily about defeating ballot measures. It has been about electing governors and legislators who are sympathetic to CCPOA's priorities on salaries, staffing, and working conditions.

CCPOA's most visible electoral investment was its support for Governor Gray Davis in 2002, when CCPOA contributed approximately $3 million to Davis's reelection campaign. Shortly before the election, Davis signed a contract that gave CCPOA members a 37 percent pay increase over five years, one of the most generous labor contracts in California history and a significant driver of the correctional officer salary structure that persists today. The Davis-CCPOA relationship became a case study in campaign finance and public employee contract negotiations, cited in academic literature on the political economy of public sector unions.

The union also contributes to legislators' campaigns and maintains a political action committee that grades legislators on criminal justice issues from CCPOA's perspective. The grading system creates electoral accountability for legislators who support sentencing reform: a poor CCPOA grade can become a campaign advertisement.

## The Reform Paradox

California has enacted more significant criminal justice reform since 2011 than any other large state: AB 109 Realignment, Proposition 47, Proposition 57, COVID emergency releases, Senate Bill 483's retroactive resentencing, and various other measures have collectively reduced the state prison population from 173,643 at peak to approximately 90,600 in 2025. CCPOA opposed most of these reforms. Most of them passed anyway, through the initiative process that bypasses the legislature or through a legislative coalition that included Democratic supermajorities.

The paradox is that the decarceration has not produced the workforce reduction that CCPOA's political opponents predicted. CDCR employed approximately 60,000 staff at peak incarceration. With the population nearly halved, it employs approximately 50,000 staff, a reduction of approximately 17 percent against a population reduction of 48 percent. Prisons that should be running at 50 to 60 percent occupancy still require full staffing because mandatory minimum staffing ratios, embedded in the labor contract and state regulation, require a certain number of officers per housing unit regardless of how many people are in the unit. The result is that each remaining incarcerated person is more expensive to supervise because the fixed staffing costs are spread over a smaller population.

CCPOA has participated in and sometimes supported the transition to a smaller, more expensive system, because the outcome, fewer prisoners but the same officers, is not an unfavorable one from the union's perspective. The members still have jobs. Their wages have continued to increase. The per-person cost, which is primarily a function of labor expense, has risen 121 percent since 2012.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that CCPOA has been one of the most consequential opponents of criminal justice reform in California history, contributing tens of millions of dollars to campaigns and candidates who supported incarceration-expanding policies and opposed nearly every significant decarceration initiative. The union's support for Gray Davis in 2002 produced a 37 percent salary increase that permanently elevated CDCR's personnel cost structure. The mandatory minimum staffing ratios embedded in CCPOA contracts ensure that the cost of a California correctional officer's compensation follows the department even as the incarcerated population declines. California's prisons are among the most expensive in the world partly because the political economy of corrections in California has consistently favored the interests of those who staff the prisons over the interests of taxpayers who fund them or the people who are incarcerated in them. A union that spends $50 million over three decades opposing reforms that would reduce its members' workload is not acting as a neutral labor organization: it is acting as a political force for a particular carceral policy.

The generous read is that correctional officers perform dangerous, psychologically demanding work in a high-stress environment that has measurable impacts on mental health and longevity. High salaries reflect genuine labor market realities: if California paid its correctional officers at the national median, recruitment and retention in the high-cost-of-living markets where most California prisons are located would be severely compromised, and the staffing crisis that already costs the state $600 million to $800 million per year in overtime would be worse. CCPOA's political spending, while substantial, has not prevented California from enacting the most ambitious decarceration agenda in the nation. The voters who approved Propositions 47 and 57 made their choices despite CCPOA's opposition and with full knowledge of it. And the union's focus on salaries, safety conditions, and staffing ratios is what labor unions do: representing the interests of their members within the political system. That CCPOA has been good at it is a feature of effective labor organization, not evidence of corruption.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CCPOA is a private association and is not required to publish detailed financial statements of its total revenues, assets, or expenditures beyond what is required under California labor law. Campaign finance records published by the Secretary of State's office track individual contributions to specific campaigns and committees, but assembling a comprehensive 30-year total requires significant aggregation work that is not available in a single public source. The total of $50 million is a widely cited estimate from journalism and academic research, not a figure from a single audited database. Similarly, the relationship between CCPOA's political spending and specific legislative outcomes is causal in direction but difficult to quantify precisely. The data shows spending and outcomes. It does not show the counterfactual.
