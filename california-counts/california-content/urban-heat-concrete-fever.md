# California Counts: The Concrete Fever
## What a 15-degree temperature difference between neighborhoods, 200 excess heat deaths per year, a tree canopy that tracks household income, and the urban heat island effect that turns asphalt into a public health emergency reveal about the climate risk California's poorest neighborhoods face without air conditioning or shade

The urban heat island effect, the phenomenon by which developed areas are significantly hotter than surrounding natural landscapes due to heat absorption by concrete, asphalt, dark roofs, and reduced vegetation, adds approximately 5 to 15 degrees Fahrenheit to peak temperatures in California's densest neighborhoods. In Los Angeles, the difference between the hottest urban neighborhoods (Pacoima, Boyle Heights, South Los Angeles) and nearby vegetated areas can exceed 15 degrees during heat events. In the Central Valley, where summer temperatures routinely exceed 105 degrees, urban heat islands push temperatures in the most developed areas to 110 to 115 degrees.

Heat is the deadliest weather phenomenon in California, killing more people than floods, earthquakes, and wildfires combined. The California Department of Public Health estimates approximately 200 excess deaths per year attributable to extreme heat, though the actual number is likely higher because heat-related deaths are systematically undercounted (cardiac arrest triggered by heat exposure is often recorded as cardiac arrest, not as heat-related). During extreme heat events (the September 2022 heat dome produced temperatures exceeding 110 degrees across much of inland California for a week), excess mortality spikes sharply: the 2022 event produced an estimated 400 to 600 excess deaths.

## The Equity Geography

The urban heat island maps directly onto the income and race maps of California's cities. Neighborhoods with the highest temperatures are disproportionately low-income communities and communities of color. Research by UCLA and UC Berkeley has documented that historically redlined neighborhoods (designated as "hazardous" for lending by the Home Owners' Loan Corporation in the 1930s) are an average of 5 to 7 degrees hotter today than neighborhoods rated "best" in the same cities. The mechanism is built-environment investment: redlined neighborhoods received less public and private investment in trees, parks, green space, and building quality, and more investment in freeways, industrial uses, and impervious surfaces. The heat island is the thermal signature of structural racism: decades of disinvestment produced neighborhoods with less shade, less vegetation, more asphalt, and more heat.

Tree canopy coverage is the most visible indicator of the disparity. Affluent neighborhoods in Los Angeles have tree canopy coverage of 25 to 40 percent. Low-income neighborhoods have 5 to 15 percent. The difference in canopy directly translates to a difference in temperature (mature trees reduce local temperatures by 5 to 10 degrees through shade and evapotranspiration) and a difference in health outcomes (heat stroke, heat exhaustion, cardiovascular stress, kidney disease, and preterm birth are all elevated in high-heat neighborhoods).

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that urban heat is a climate crisis hiding inside an equity crisis, and California's response is grossly insufficient. The state's Extreme Heat Action Plan (2022) identified the problem and proposed investments in tree planting, cool roofs, cool pavements, cooling centers, and heat warning systems. The investments are modest: approximately $200 million per year across all programs, in a state where the cost of achieving equitable tree canopy coverage alone would exceed $2 billion. Cooling centers (air-conditioned public buildings designated for use during heat events) serve a tiny fraction of the at-risk population and are useless for people who cannot reach them (the elderly, the disabled, the unhoused). The fundamental adaptation (residential air conditioning) is unaffordable for low-income renters and is absent from approximately 30 percent of homes in the Central Valley and 50 percent of homes in coastal communities that historically did not need it but increasingly do.

The generous read is that California is the first state to develop a comprehensive extreme heat response framework, including the designation of heat as an occupational hazard (Cal/OSHA heat illness prevention standards require shade, water, and rest for outdoor workers), the Extreme Heat Action Plan, and targeted investments in urban greening in disadvantaged communities. The CalEnviroScreen tool identifies the communities most vulnerable to heat and directs investment to them. Tree planting programs (CAL FIRE urban forestry, local tree equity initiatives) have planted millions of trees in underserved neighborhoods. The problem is decades of disinvestment that cannot be reversed in years, but the framework for reversal exists and investment is increasing.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Department of Public Health tracks heat-related emergency department visits and hospitalizations but does not publish neighborhood-level heat mortality data, heat-related chronic disease burden, or the intersection of heat exposure with housing quality (air conditioning prevalence, insulation, ventilation). The question of how many Californians live in heat-vulnerable housing (no AC, poor insulation, inadequate shade) in heat-vulnerable neighborhoods is not answered by any published state data.
