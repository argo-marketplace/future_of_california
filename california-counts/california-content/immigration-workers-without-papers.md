# California Counts: The Workers Without Papers
## What 1.8 million undocumented residents, $3.7 billion in annual state tax contributions, a $130 billion GDP contribution, and a Medi-Cal expansion that covers them reveal about the workforce California depends on and cannot legally acknowledge

California is home to approximately 1.8 million undocumented immigrants, the largest undocumented population of any state and roughly 16 percent of the estimated 11 million undocumented residents nationwide. The undocumented population has been roughly stable since 2010, declining from a peak of approximately 2.5 million in 2007 as net migration from Mexico decreased and enforcement increased. The population is predominantly Mexican-born (approximately 60 percent), with growing shares from Central America, Asia, and other regions.

The economic contribution is substantial and well-documented. The Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy estimates that undocumented immigrants in California pay approximately $3.7 billion per year in state and local taxes (income tax, sales tax, property tax paid through rent). The California Budget and Policy Center estimates that undocumented workers generate approximately $130 billion in annual GDP through their labor in agriculture, construction, food service, domestic work, and manufacturing. Approximately 10 percent of California's workforce, roughly 1.8 million workers, is undocumented, a share that rises to 50 to 70 percent in agriculture, 20 to 30 percent in construction, and 15 to 20 percent in food service and hospitality.

## The Policy Landscape

California has adopted the most inclusive state-level policies for undocumented residents in the nation. AB 60 (2013) made California the tenth state to allow undocumented residents to obtain driver's licenses; approximately 1 million AB 60 licenses have been issued. SB 4 (2017), the "California Values Act," limits state and local law enforcement cooperation with federal immigration authorities. Health4All, implemented in phases from 2016 to 2024, extended full-scope Medi-Cal coverage to undocumented residents of all ages, making California the first state to provide comprehensive health insurance to undocumented adults. The Medi-Cal expansion covers an estimated 700,000 to 800,000 undocumented residents at an annual cost to the state of approximately $3 billion to $4 billion (the federal government does not reimburse Medi-Cal costs for undocumented residents, making the state share 100 percent rather than the approximately 50 percent federal match for other Medi-Cal populations).

The CalEITC, California's Earned Income Tax Credit, is available to undocumented workers who file taxes using Individual Taxpayer Identification Numbers (ITINs), providing refundable tax credits to approximately 400,000 undocumented tax filers. In-state tuition at UC, CSU, and community colleges is available to undocumented students under AB 540 (2001), and the California Dream Act (2011) extended state financial aid eligibility to undocumented students.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's inclusive policies for undocumented residents, however humane, exist because the state's economy depends on a labor force whose legal vulnerability suppresses its wages and whose federal status makes it unable to access the labor protections (collective bargaining, OSHA enforcement, minimum wage recovery through litigation) that documented workers take for granted. The $3.7 billion in state tax contributions and the $130 billion in GDP are generated by workers who cannot vote, who fear reporting workplace injuries, who accept wages below market because the alternative is deportation, and whose children grow up in households where a traffic stop can lead to family separation. California's policies mitigate the cruelty of federal immigration law. They do not resolve the fundamental contradiction: the state's economy requires workers whom the federal government classifies as criminals.

The generous read is that California has made a policy choice that no other large state has made: to treat undocumented residents as members of the community rather than as problems to be deported. The Medi-Cal expansion provides healthcare to 800,000 people who would otherwise seek care in emergency departments at higher cost. The driver's license program improves road safety by ensuring that undocumented drivers are tested, licensed, and insured. The in-state tuition and financial aid policies allow talented students to contribute to the state's economy rather than being excluded from higher education by an accident of birth. These policies cost money. They also produce a society that is more inclusive, more productive, and more consistent with the values California claims to hold.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The undocumented population estimate is derived from Census Bureau data (American Community Survey) and the "residual method" that estimates the undocumented population by subtracting the legally present foreign-born population from the total foreign-born population. The methodology produces estimates with a margin of error of approximately 10 to 15 percent. Individual-level data on undocumented residents is not collected by state agencies (which generally do not ask about immigration status), making demographic, economic, and health analyses dependent on survey data with significant nonresponse bias (undocumented residents are less likely to participate in surveys due to fear of identification).
