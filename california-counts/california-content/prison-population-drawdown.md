# California Counts: The Billion-Dollar Drawdown
## What happens when a state shrinks its prison population by half and the budget barely moves

On October 20, 2006, California's state prison system held 173,643 people. It was the peak, the high-water mark of a three-decade incarceration expansion that had grown the prison population nearly ninefold from 19,623 in 1977. Every year the number climbed. Every election cycle brought new sentencing enhancements, new mandatory minimums, new ballot propositions with tough-on-crime branding. California built 23 prisons between 1984 and 2005 and still ran out of room.

By December 2025, the population had fallen to 90,600. A decline of 48 percent from peak. The imprisonment rate dropped from 483 per 100,000 adults to 294. Five prisons have closed. The state is discussing closing more. And yet CDCR's budget, the Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation, has grown from $8.1 billion in FY2006-07 to $13.6 billion proposed for FY2025-26. California is spending 68 percent more to incarcerate 48 percent fewer people. The per-person cost has reached $132,860 per year, roughly double the national median and more than the annual tuition at Stanford. The drawdown is real. The savings are not.

---

## The Arc: From 19,000 to 173,000 and Back

The shape of California's prison population over the past five decades is an asymmetric mountain. The ascent took 29 years. The descent, still underway, has taken 19 and counting.

The climb began with the Uniform Determinate Sentencing Act of 1976, which replaced indeterminate sentences with fixed terms and shifted power from parole boards to prosecutors. What followed was a generation of sentencing escalation: mandatory minimums, Three Strikes (1994), a succession of ballot propositions that lengthened terms for violent and drug offenses. The prison population doubled from 1984 to 1990, then doubled again by 2000. By 2006, California's prisons held roughly 200 percent of their design capacity. Some gymnasiums held triple-stacked bunks. The state was shipping inmates to private prisons in Arizona, Mississippi, and Oklahoma.

The descent came in stages, each triggered by a different mechanism.

| Year | Event | Population impact |
|---|---|---|
| 2011 | Brown v. Plata Supreme Court ruling; AB 109 Realignment | Shifted roughly 25,000 non-violent offenders to county jails |
| 2014 | Proposition 47 (reclassified drug and property felonies as misdemeanors) | Reduced prison population by roughly 5,200 |
| 2016 | Proposition 57 (parole consideration for nonviolent felons) | Estimated reduction of 2,600 in first year; 11,500 projected by FY2020-21 |
| 2020-21 | COVID emergency releases and reduced admissions | Population fell 23 percent from March 2020 to February 2021 |
| 2024 | Proposition 36 (toughened penalties for repeat theft and fentanyl) | Projected to add roughly 3,300 by FY2025-26 |

The single largest driver was Realignment, which accounted for an estimated 55 percent of the total decline. Brown v. Plata, the 2011 Supreme Court case in which Justice Kennedy wrote that California's prison overcrowding "falls below the standard of decency," ordered the state to reduce its population to 137.5 percent of design capacity. AB 109 was the legislative response: non-violent, non-serious, non-sexual offenders with sentences over one year would serve time in county jails instead of state prisons. The shift was immediate. In the first eight months, new prison admissions fell 41 percent.

The question that hangs over every number in this dataset is whether the decline represents a durable structural change or a political cycle that Proposition 36 has already begun to reverse.

## The Cost Paradox: Fewer People, More Money

The most striking finding in the CDCR data is the divergence between population and spending. Between FY2010-11 and FY2024-25, the prison population fell by roughly 40 percent. Over the same period, CDCR expenditures rose from $9.7 billion to $14.3 billion, an increase of 47 percent.

The cost per incarcerated person tells the story most precisely. In 2012, the annual cost was roughly $60,000. By 2024, it had reached $132,860, an increase of 121 percent. Ninety-one percent of per-prisoner spending comes from fixed costs: salaries, benefits, pension contributions, and facility maintenance. When the population falls, those fixed costs do not fall with it. A prison designed for 3,000 people and holding 1,800 still needs a warden, a medical staff, a boiler, and a fence. The marginal cost of each additional prisoner is low. The marginal savings from each departure are equally low.

Employee compensation is the dominant factor. CDCR is the largest department employer in California state government, with over 65,000 authorized positions. Average employee compensation rose 43 percent between 2010 and 2019, from $110,000 to $158,000 per year, nearly triple the rate of inflation. The correctional officers' union, CCPOA, negotiated successive contracts that included 3 percent annual raises and benefits costing roughly $1 billion over three years. California's correctional officers are the highest-paid in the nation, with a 2026 median salary of approximately $91,470.

The other major driver is healthcare. Medical care per person has more than doubled in the past decade, driven in part by the federal receivership that has governed prison healthcare since 2006. The state spent $3.1 billion on prison medical care in FY2025-26. Temporary medical staff alone cost $1.1 billion over five years.

CDCR's share of the General Fund has actually declined, from 9.4 percent in 2007 to 6.7 percent in 2024. In an era of expanding state budgets, corrections has been a shrinking share of a growing pie. But the absolute numbers remain enormous. California spends more on its prison system than 42 states spend on their entire state budgets.

## Who Is Inside: The Changing Composition

The prison population of 2025 is not the prison population of 2006. The reforms that drove the drawdown selectively removed non-violent and lower-level offenders, leaving behind a population that is older, serving longer sentences, and convicted of more serious offenses.

In 2019, 46 percent of the prison population was incarcerated for homicide or assault. By 2025, that figure had risen to 52 percent. Sex crimes account for 18 percent. Drug offenses, once a major driver of incarceration, now account for just 3 percent, and the drug incarceration rate is 35 percent lower than it was in 1990. Robbery and burglary, which represented 22 percent of the population in 2019, have fallen to 16 percent. The prison system that Proposition 47 and Realignment built is, by design, a system that holds violent offenders and little else.

The racial composition remains profoundly disproportionate. Latinos make up 47 percent of the prison population but 36 percent of the state's adult population. Black Californians make up 27 percent of the prison population but 6 percent of the state's adults, an overrepresentation of 4.5 times their share. The Black-to-white imprisonment disparity stands at 8.9 to 1, meaning a Black Californian is nearly nine times as likely to be in state prison as a white Californian. That ratio has actually increased slightly since 2000, from 8.6 to 1, even as the absolute number of Black men in state prisons dropped 25 percent between 2010 and 2019.

The aging of the population is the most consequential demographic shift for the budget. In 1994, 4 percent of the prison population was aged 50 or older. By 2019, the figure was 25 percent. By 2025, it had reached 30 percent. On average, these individuals have served over 18 years of continuous incarceration. They are the human residue of the sentencing laws of the 1980s and 1990s: long sentences imposed decades ago, now producing elderly prisoners who require exponentially more expensive medical care.

## The Closures: What Happens to a Prison Town

California has closed five state prisons since 2021, with a sixth scheduled for fall 2026. Each closure saves the state approximately $150 million per year. Each closure also removes the economic anchor from a community that was often built around it.

| Prison | Location | Closed |
|---|---|---|
| Deuel Vocational Institution | Tracy | September 2021 |
| California Correctional Center | Susanville | June 2023 |
| Chuckawalla Valley State Prison | Blythe | 2024 |
| California City Correctional Facility | California City | 2024 |
| California Rehabilitation Center | Norco | Scheduled fall 2026 |

Susanville is the case study. The California Correctional Center and adjacent High Desert State Prison together accounted for more than 45 percent of local employment. CCC's roughly 1,080 staff earned salaries that in some cases exceeded $90,000, in a county where the median household income was roughly half that. When the closure was announced, Susanville filed a CEQA lawsuit to block it. A Lassen County judge issued a temporary restraining order, which was dissolved in September 2022. The state provided a $1 million Rapid Response grant and connected the community to the $500 million Community Economic Resilience Fund.

The economic logic of prison closures is straightforward: a prison running at 50 percent occupancy with a $150 million annual operating cost is an inefficient use of state funds. The political logic is less clean. Rural California built its economic base around prisons in the same decades that mining, timber, and agriculture were declining. The communities that hosted prisons did so willingly, often competing for the privilege, because the jobs were real and the paychecks were steady. Closing a prison is not just a budget line. It is a decision about which communities the state will sustain and which it will not.

## Recidivism: The Outcome That Matters Most

The CDCR recidivism report tracks the three-year conviction rate for each annual release cohort. The most recent data, published April 2025, covers the 34,215 individuals released in FY2019-20. Of that cohort, 39.1 percent were reconvicted within three years: 22.1 percent for felonies and 17.0 percent for misdemeanors. The remaining 60.9 percent completed the three-year follow-up without a new conviction.

The trend is encouraging. The three-year conviction rate has fallen from 44.6 percent for the FY2015-16 cohort to 39.1 percent for FY2019-20, a decline of 5.5 percentage points over five release years.

Program participation correlates with lower recidivism, though causation is difficult to establish from the data alone. Men who participated in the Male Community Reentry Program had a 26.5 percent reconviction rate. Women in the Female Community Reentry Program had a rate of 18.3 percent. Individuals released through resentencing had a reconviction rate of just 14 percent, compared to 42 percent for the general population.

The COVID early-release cohort offers a natural experiment. Of the approximately 14,800 people released early during the pandemic, roughly one-third, about 4,600, had returned to prison as of January 2025. That return rate is broadly consistent with the overall recidivism trend, which complicates both the "they should never have been released" and the "everyone can be safely released" narratives.

## The Federal Court Shadow

Two federal lawsuits have shaped California's prison system for longer than most of its current inmates have been incarcerated.

Plata v. Newsom, filed in 2001 over medical care, led to the appointment of a federal Receiver in 2006, effectively removing prison healthcare from CDCR's control. As of March 2026, the Receiver has delegated medical operations at 31 of 34 institutions back to the state. The receivership appears to be approaching its conclusion after nearly 20 years, though the final delegations have not been completed.

Coleman v. Newsom, filed in 1990 over mental health care, has moved in the opposite direction. In June 2024, the federal court found CDCR in contempt and imposed $112 million in fines for failing to meet mental health staffing ratios. In September 2025, the court established a new mental health receivership, appointing Colette S. Peters, former director of the U.S. Bureau of Prisons, to take over the system. The vacancy rate among mental health workers was nearly 30 percent as of April 2024. Fifty-six percent of mental health appointments were not completed as scheduled. The Receiver estimates compliance will cost $41 million annually and take five to seven years.

The Coleman case has now been active for 35 years. The state has paid over $112 million in contempt fines for failing to staff the positions it authorized but could not fill, a pattern that connects directly to the workforce vacancy data examined in a previous California Counts installment.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has executed one of the largest deincarceration experiments in American history and has almost nothing fiscal to show for it. The prison population is down 48 percent from peak. The budget is up 68 percent. The per-person cost has more than doubled. The state closes prisons to save money but still spends $13.6 billion a year on a system that holds fewer people than it did in 1990. The cost paradox is a structural feature, not a temporary distortion: fixed costs, union contracts, federal court mandates, and an aging population guarantee that spending per capita will continue to rise even as the population continues to fall. Meanwhile, Proposition 36 has already begun to reverse the drawdown, with 900 people sent to state prison on Prop 36 charges and prosecutors filing nearly 40,000 Prop 36 criminal charges in 2025. Only 17 percent of Prop 36 felony drug cases resulted in treatment referrals, despite the measure's branding as a treatment-mandated alternative. The pendulum is swinging back, and the fiscal consequence of reincarceration at $132,860 per person per year is not abstract.

The generous read is that the decline has been accompanied by measurable improvements. The three-year reconviction rate has dropped from 44.6 to 39.1 percent. Community reentry programs show recidivism rates 12 to 25 percentage points below the baseline. Resentenced individuals reoffend at one-third the rate of the general release population. The racial composition of the prison system, while still deeply disproportionate, reflects a declining absolute number of Black men incarcerated. Five prison closures represent a genuine contraction of the carceral footprint, not just a shuffle of bodies between institutions. And the share of corrections in the General Fund has fallen from 9.4 to 6.7 percent, freeing fiscal space for education, healthcare, and infrastructure. The drawdown has not produced proportional savings because the remaining population is harder to serve, older, sicker, and serving longer sentences for more serious offenses. That is not a failure of reform. That is the natural consequence of a system that has already released the people who were easiest to release.

Both readings are present in the same data. The cost paradox and the outcome improvements are not contradictory. They are the two consequences of a single policy trajectory, and neither cancels the other.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The CDCR population data counts bodies in beds. It does not count the roughly 80,000 people in county jails, many of whom were shifted there by Realignment and are serving sentences that would previously have been served in state prison. The drawdown in state prison population was partially accomplished by moving people to a different level of government, not by reducing incarceration overall. County jail populations rose 15 percent in the first three years after Realignment, and by 2014 more than 1,700 county jail inmates were serving sentences of five years or longer, a category that did not exist before AB 109.

The data also does not capture the experience of incarceration. A system that spends $132,860 per person per year and still cannot complete 56 percent of its mental health appointments is not a system that is spending too much money. It is a system that is spending money badly, or spending it on the wrong things, or both. The per-capita figure tells you the cost of warehousing a human being. It does not tell you the cost of rehabilitating one.

And the data says nothing about deterrence. Whether the drawdown has affected crime rates is the question that Proposition 36's supporters and opponents argue most fiercely, and the utilization data cannot resolve it. Crime rates declined roughly 10 percent statewide in 2025, a period during which both the drawdown and Proposition 36 were in effect simultaneously. The data shows the correlation. It does not show the cause.

---

California spent three decades building the largest state prison system in American history and is now two decades into unbuilding it. The population has been cut nearly in half. Five prisons have closed. The recidivism rate is falling. And the budget has grown by two-thirds. The drawdown is a genuine policy achievement that has not produced the fiscal dividend its architects promised, because the system that remains is more expensive per person than the system that was dismantled. The question now is whether Proposition 36 marks a new chapter of the story or a return to an old one. At $132,860 per person per year, the answer is not cheap either way.
