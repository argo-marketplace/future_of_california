# California Counts: The One Percent Budget
## What 50 percent of income tax from 1 percent of filers, $45 billion budget swings, capital gains as the volatility engine, and a 13.3 percent top rate reveal about the tax base that makes California's budget a bet on the stock market

California's personal income tax generates approximately $115 billion per year, roughly 65 percent of the state's general fund revenue. The top 1 percent of income tax filers, approximately 180,000 returns reporting adjusted gross income above approximately $900,000, pay approximately 50 percent of that total, or roughly $57 billion. The top 5 percent pay approximately 70 percent. The bottom 60 percent of filers pay approximately 2 percent. California's budget is funded, to a degree matched by no other large state, by a small number of very wealthy taxpayers whose income is heavily composed of capital gains, stock options, and business profits, the most volatile forms of income in the tax base.

The volatility is the defining feature. In fiscal year 2021-22, capital gains realizations in California exceeded $260 billion, generating approximately $20 billion in income tax revenue above trend. In fiscal year 2022-23, capital gains fell to approximately $120 billion as stock markets declined, reducing revenue by approximately $15 billion. The swing, $35 billion in a single year from a single income component, exceeded the entire general fund budget of 42 individual states. California's budget does not have a revenue problem or a spending problem. It has a volatility problem that produces alternating cycles of surplus and deficit, each of which generates policy responses (spending increases during surpluses, service cuts during deficits) that amplify the underlying instability.

## The Progressive Structure

California's income tax is the most progressive major state tax in the nation. The rate structure includes 10 brackets, ranging from 1 percent on the first $10,412 of taxable income (single filer) to 12.3 percent on income above $698,271, plus an additional 1 percent Mental Health Services Act surcharge on income above $1 million, producing an effective top marginal rate of 13.3 percent. Combined with the federal top rate of 37 percent and the 3.8 percent Net Investment Income Tax, a California millionaire faces a combined marginal rate of approximately 54 percent on ordinary income and 37 percent on long-term capital gains.

The progressivity is deliberate and popular. The Mental Health Services Act surcharge was approved by voters as Proposition 63 in 2004. Proposition 30 (2012) and Proposition 55 (2016) temporarily and then extended higher rates on high earners. The state's reliance on progressive income taxation rather than broader consumption taxes (California's sales tax, while high at 7.25 percent base rate, is narrower than most states' because it exempts services, which constitute an increasing share of economic activity) reflects both political choice and constitutional constraint: Proposition 13 limits property tax revenue, forcing the state to rely more heavily on income tax.

The concentration has consequences. When the stock market rises, California runs surpluses. When it falls, California runs deficits. The Rainy Day Fund, established by Proposition 2 in 2014, requires deposits during years when capital gains revenue exceeds a threshold, building a reserve for downturns. The fund held approximately $22 billion at its peak in 2022. The 2023-24 budget deficit of approximately $32 billion drew the fund down to approximately $12 billion. The reserve is valuable but structurally insufficient: it cannot absorb a multi-year downturn because the magnitude of revenue decline ($35 billion or more per year in a severe market correction) exceeds the fund's capacity.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has built a $310 billion budget on a tax base that is functionally a stock market derivative. The state's fiscal health depends on the investment returns of 180,000 taxpayers whose income is generated by asset markets the state cannot control. A sustained bear market, a tech sector contraction, or an acceleration of high-income out-migration would produce a fiscal crisis that the Rainy Day Fund cannot absorb and that would require either massive service cuts or emergency tax increases. The 13.3 percent top rate, already the highest in the nation, leaves limited room for rate increases without accelerating the out-migration of the taxpayers who generate the revenue. The structural dependence on capital gains income, which is discretionary (taxpayers can defer realizations) and mobile (taxpayers can relocate to zero-income-tax states), makes California's budget more fragile than its GDP suggests.

The generous read is that California's progressive tax structure produces the revenue necessary to fund the most comprehensive public services of any large state: Medi-Cal for 15 million people, K-12 spending above the national average, the UC and CSU systems, extensive environmental regulation, and social programs that other states do not offer. The volatility is real but manageable: the Rainy Day Fund, the ability to borrow through revenue anticipation notes, and the sheer size of the economy ($3.6 trillion GDP) provide buffers that smaller states lack. The concentration of revenue in high earners is the natural consequence of a progressive tax system in the most economically productive state in the nation. And the $115 billion in income tax revenue, however volatile, is $115 billion that funds the institutions that make California's economy productive in the first place.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Franchise Tax Board publishes income tax statistics by income bracket, filing status, and county. It does not publish taxpayer-level data that would allow analysis of individual mobility patterns. The $29 billion in AGI lost to out-migration (cited in the migration post) is an aggregate figure; the breakdown by income bracket, while available at a high level, does not reveal whether the departing high earners are replaced by new high earners arriving from other states or from income growth among existing residents. The stability of the top 1 percent's contribution depends on churn: if the same 180,000 filers contribute 50 percent of revenue year after year, the base is stable. If the composition changes as filers leave and are replaced by new filers, the stability depends on replacement rates that are not publicly tracked.
