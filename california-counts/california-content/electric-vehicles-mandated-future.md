# California Counts: The Mandated Future
## What 1.9 million registered EVs, a 2035 ban on new gas car sales, 100,000 public chargers, and a $2,900 average annual fueling savings reveal about the electric vehicle transition California mandated before the grid could deliver it

California has approximately 1.9 million registered zero-emission vehicles as of early 2026, more than any other state and approximately 40 percent of all EVs registered in the United States. The state's Advanced Clean Cars II regulation, adopted by the California Air Resources Board in August 2022, requires that 100 percent of new passenger vehicles sold in California be zero-emission by 2035. The interim targets require 35 percent ZEV sales by 2026 and 68 percent by 2030. In 2024, ZEVs constituted approximately 25 percent of new car sales in California, up from 19 percent in 2023 and 6 percent in 2020.

The ZEV mandate is the most aggressive vehicle electrification policy in the nation, and approximately 17 other states have adopted California's standards under Section 177 of the Clean Air Act. The mandate drives approximately one-third of the U.S. EV market through regulatory force. The Clean Vehicle Rebate Project (CVRP) and its successor, the Clean Cars 4 All program, have distributed approximately $1.2 billion in consumer incentives since 2010, with income-capped rebates of $2,000 to $7,500 per vehicle.

## The Charging Gap

California has approximately 100,000 public charging ports (Level 2 and DC fast chargers combined), the most of any state. The California Energy Commission's AB 2127 assessment determined that the state will need approximately 1.2 million public and shared chargers by 2030 to support the projected EV fleet. The current buildout rate, approximately 15,000 to 20,000 new ports per year, is insufficient to meet the 2030 target. The gap between charging infrastructure and vehicle sales is particularly acute in disadvantaged communities, rural areas, and multi-unit housing (apartments and condominiums), where approximately 40 percent of Californians live without access to home charging.

The grid capacity question looms behind the charging infrastructure question. The California Independent System Operator (CAISO) projects that full vehicle electrification will add approximately 25,000 to 50,000 megawatts of peak demand to a grid that currently peaks at approximately 52,000 MW. Managed charging (shifting EV charging to off-peak hours) can reduce the peak impact significantly, but the infrastructure to support managed charging, including time-of-use rate structures, vehicle-grid integration, and smart charging equipment, is not yet deployed at scale.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California mandated an electric vehicle transition before building the infrastructure to support it. The 2035 ban on new gas car sales assumes a charging network, grid capacity, and vehicle affordability that do not currently exist and may not exist on the timeline the regulation requires. The average new EV costs approximately $55,000 (though prices are declining), placing it out of reach for the median California household income of $85,000. The mandate is regressive in implementation: affluent homeowners with garages and solar panels capture the savings (approximately $2,900 per year in avoided fuel costs), while renters in multi-unit housing cannot charge at home and face longer trips to public chargers that cost $0.40 to $0.60 per kilowatt-hour, eroding the cost advantage. California is mandating that its residents buy a product that works well for some and poorly for others.

The generous read is that regulatory mandates create markets, and markets drive innovation, cost reduction, and infrastructure buildout. The ZEV mandate has already reduced average EV prices by approximately 30 percent since 2020, driven $12 billion in private charging infrastructure investment, and created a domestic EV manufacturing sector (with Rivian, Lucid, and Tesla all manufacturing in or near California). The 2035 target is ten years away. Battery costs are declining at approximately 15 percent per year. Vehicle-to-grid technology is emerging. The mandate gives automakers, utilities, and infrastructure developers a guaranteed market, which is the single most powerful tool for accelerating the transition. Without the mandate, the transition stalls at early-adopter penetration. With it, the transition becomes inevitable, and inevitability attracts capital.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Energy Commission publishes ZEV registration data, charger deployment data, and grid demand projections. It does not publish comprehensive data on charging accessibility by housing type, income level, or geography. The question of whether renters, rural residents, and low-income households can participate in the EV transition at the same rate as homeowners is an equity question that the aggregate registration numbers do not answer.
