# California Counts: The Water Beneath
## What 2 million acre-feet of annual overdraft, 30 feet of land subsidence, a 2014 law that gave California its first groundwater regulations, and a 2040 compliance deadline reveal about the aquifer the Central Valley has been mining for a century

California pumps approximately 13 million to 15 million acre-feet of groundwater per year, roughly 40 percent of the state's total water supply in normal years and as much as 60 percent in drought years. For over a century, California was the only western state that did not regulate groundwater extraction. A landowner could drill a well and pump without limit, without a permit, and without reporting how much water was taken. The legal doctrine of correlative rights gave overlying landowners equal rights to the groundwater beneath their property, but no mechanism existed to determine how much water the aquifer contained, how much was being extracted, or whether extraction exceeded natural recharge.

The result was predictable. The Central Valley's aquifer system, one of the most productive in the world, has been in a state of chronic overdraft for decades. The Department of Water Resources estimates the average annual overdraft at approximately 1.8 million to 2.1 million acre-feet, meaning that extraction consistently exceeds recharge by roughly the volume of water used by 4 million households. During the 2012-2016 drought, the overdraft accelerated to an estimated 6 million to 8 million acre-feet per year as farmers compensated for reduced surface water deliveries by drilling deeper wells. The total volume of groundwater extracted from the Central Valley since intensive irrigation began in the early 20th century has been estimated by NASA's GRACE satellite program at approximately 40 to 50 cubic kilometers, roughly the volume of Lake Mead at capacity.

## The Sinking Valley

The most visible consequence of groundwater overdraft is land subsidence: the physical sinking of the ground surface as the clay layers in the aquifer system compress when water is removed. The Central Valley has experienced some of the most extreme subsidence ever recorded. Areas near Corcoran in the Tulare Basin have sunk more than 28 feet since the 1920s. In the San Joaquin Valley, subsidence rates during the 2012-2016 drought reached 11 inches per year in the most affected areas.

Subsidence is not merely a geological curiosity. It damages infrastructure. The Delta-Mendota Canal, a federal water conveyance facility that delivers water to farms and cities on the west side of the San Joaquin Valley, has lost approximately 3 to 4 feet of capacity due to subsidence along its alignment. The Friant-Kern Canal, which delivers water from Millerton Lake to the southern San Joaquin Valley, has lost approximately 60 percent of its capacity in the most subsided reach, reducing deliveries to communities and farms that depend on it. Repairing these canals, essentially re-grading and re-lining sections that have sunk, costs hundreds of millions of dollars. The Friant-Kern Canal repair alone is estimated at $500 million. And the repairs are treating a symptom: if pumping continues, the ground will continue to sink.

Subsidence is also largely irreversible. When clay layers compact under reduced water pressure, they lose their capacity to store water. The aquifer's storage volume is permanently reduced. Each acre-foot of overdraft does not just borrow water from the future; it shrinks the container from which future water can be drawn. The Central Valley is not just mining its groundwater. It is destroying the aquifer's capacity to hold groundwater.

## SGMA: California's Belated Regulation

The Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA), signed by Governor Brown in September 2014, was California's first comprehensive groundwater regulation. The law requires the formation of local Groundwater Sustainability Agencies (GSAs) to develop and implement Groundwater Sustainability Plans (GSPs) for the state's most overdrafted basins. The plans must achieve "sustainability" by 2040 for critically overdrafted basins and by 2042 for other high- and medium-priority basins. Sustainability is defined as the elimination of six "undesirable results": chronic lowering of groundwater levels, reduction of groundwater storage, seawater intrusion, degradation of water quality, land subsidence, and depletion of interconnected surface water.

The law is simultaneously the most significant water policy reform in California in a generation and an exercise in structured procrastination. SGMA gave critically overdrafted basins until January 2020 to submit their sustainability plans and until 2040 to actually achieve sustainability. The 20-year implementation timeline means that the pumping that caused the crisis can continue, at reduced but still substantial levels, for decades after the law's passage.

DWR reviewed the initial GSPs submitted in 2020 and 2022 and found significant deficiencies. Several plans in the Tulare Basin and San Joaquin Valley were assessed as "incomplete" or "inadequate," requiring revision. The most contentious issue is the volume of land that must be fallowed, retired from irrigated agriculture, to bring extraction into balance with recharge. Estimates range from 500,000 to 1 million acres of currently irrigated Central Valley farmland that must be taken out of production to achieve groundwater sustainability, representing approximately $5 billion to $10 billion in annual agricultural output and the livelihoods of thousands of farm families and tens of thousands of farmworkers.

## The Farmland Retirement Problem

The communities most affected by SGMA's land retirement requirements are among the poorest in California. The small towns of the western and southern San Joaquin Valley, places like Mendota, Huron, Firebaugh, and Avenal, are majority-Latino, majority-farmworker communities where agriculture is the primary or sole economic base. When farmland is fallowed permanently, the jobs associated with it (field labor, packing, processing, equipment operation) disappear, and the small businesses that serve farmworker families (grocery stores, laundromats, taco trucks, dollar stores) lose their customer base.

DWR's Multibenefit Land Repurposing Program and the state's investment in managed aquifer recharge (Flood-MAR) are intended to provide alternatives: paying landowners to convert farmland to recharge basins, solar installations, or habitat restoration. But the funding, approximately $200 million to $300 million appropriated to date for land repurposing programs, is a fraction of the annual economic value of the land that will be retired. The transition management problem is enormous: how to maintain viable rural communities when the economic activity that sustains them is being permanently reduced by regulatory requirement. California has no precedent for managing an economic transition of this scale in its rural interior.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California allowed the Central Valley's groundwater to be mined without regulation for over a century, then passed a law that gives the miners another 20 years to stop. SGMA is a framework for managed decline, not restoration. The aquifer storage that has been permanently lost to subsidence cannot be recovered. The 28 feet of subsidence near Corcoran is a geological fact that no law can reverse. The 500,000 to 1 million acres of farmland retirement will devastate the poorest communities in the state, communities that had no voice in the decisions that created the overdraft and that will bear the concentrated costs of ending it. The GSP process, governed by local agencies whose boards are dominated by the agricultural interests that benefit from continued pumping, is a fox-guarding-the-henhouse structure that has already produced plans DWR found inadequate. California's groundwater regulation arrived 50 years too late and operates on a timeline that ensures another 15 years of damage before sustainability is achieved, if it is achieved at all.

The generous read is that SGMA accomplished what no previous California governor or legislature had been willing to attempt: imposing regulation on groundwater for the first time in the state's history, against the opposition of agricultural interests that had blocked every previous effort. The local governance structure, while imperfect, allows the people who depend on the aquifer to participate in decisions about its management. The 2040 timeline, while long, reflects the genuine difficulty of transitioning agricultural economies that have been built on unsustainable pumping for generations. And the investments in managed aquifer recharge, the Flood-MAR pilots, and the Sites Reservoir project represent a serious effort to increase the supply side of the equation: capturing flood flows during wet years and storing them underground for extraction during dry years. SGMA is not a perfect law. It is the law that passed, and the alternative was no regulation at all.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

DWR publishes groundwater level measurements from a monitoring network of approximately 4,000 wells, but coverage is uneven: some subbasins have dense monitoring networks and others have sparse data. The actual volume of groundwater in storage, as opposed to the level at specific well locations, is estimated from geological models with significant uncertainty. The total volume of the Central Valley's aquifer system is not known with precision, meaning that the "overdraft" figure (extraction minus recharge) is an estimate derived from incomplete data.

Individual well-level pumping data is collected under SGMA reporting requirements but is not publicly available in most GSA jurisdictions due to concerns about privacy and competitive sensitivity. The aggregate data published by DWR and the GSAs does not allow independent verification of whether individual pumpers are complying with allocation reductions. The infrastructure for measuring compliance with groundwater sustainability plans is still being built, and whether it will be adequate to enforce the plans is an open question.
