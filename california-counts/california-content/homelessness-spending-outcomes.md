# California Counts: The $24 Billion Question
## What $24 billion in five years, 181,000 people unsheltered on a single night, and a 53 percent cost variance reveal about the gap between spending and solving

California spent approximately $24 billion on homelessness between fiscal year 2018-19 and fiscal year 2023-24, according to the State Auditor's January 2024 report. During that same period, the state's homeless population, as measured by the Department of Housing and Urban Development's Point-in-Time count, rose from approximately 151,000 in 2019 to approximately 181,000 in 2023, an increase of roughly 20 percent. The state that spends more on homelessness than any other, by a factor that dwarfs per-capita comparisons, has more homeless people than when it started spending. That sentence is not an argument. It is a data point. And the data point raises a question that $24 billion should have answered by now: what did the money buy?

---

## The Spending: Who Spent What

The State Auditor's report identified $24 billion in homelessness-related expenditures across 30 state programs administered by nine different departments. The spending was not centrally coordinated. No single agency tracked total expenditures, outcomes, or cost-effectiveness across programs. The auditor found that the state "ichoes not have a comprehensive understanding of the exposed population of people experiencing homelessness, the exposed cost of services, or whether its programs are effective."

The largest single expenditure category was Homekey, a program created during the COVID-19 pandemic that converted hotels, motels, and commercial properties into permanent supportive housing. Homekey received approximately $3.3 billion in state funds through three funding rounds and produced approximately 15,000 units by early 2025. The average per-unit cost was approximately $220,000 in the first round, rising to roughly $290,000 in subsequent rounds, a fraction of the $600,000 to $700,000 per-unit cost of new affordable housing construction in California.

The second-largest category was the Homeless Housing, Assistance, and Prevention program, known as HHAP, which distributed approximately $4.7 billion in block grants to local governments and Continuums of Care. HHAP funds were flexible: jurisdictions could use them for shelters, rental assistance, outreach, navigation centers, or permanent housing. The state required recipients to set measurable goals and report outcomes, but enforcement was limited and reporting was inconsistent.

Additional spending included Project Roomkey ($800 million for pandemic hotel rooms), the Encampment Resolution Fund ($1.1 billion for clearing encampments and connecting residents to services), behavioral health bridge housing ($1.5 billion), the CalWORKs housing support program, Medi-Cal homeless services, and various smaller programs. Veterans' homelessness programs, federal Housing Choice Vouchers, and federal Emergency Solutions Grants added billions more in federal dollars not counted in the state's $24 billion figure.

## The Count: Who Is Homeless

The Point-in-Time count, conducted biennially in January by local Continuums of Care under HUD requirements, is the official measure of homelessness in America. California's 2023 PIT count found approximately 181,399 people experiencing homelessness on a single night, of whom roughly 123,423 (68 percent) were unsheltered, meaning they were sleeping outside, in vehicles, or in places not designed for human habitation. California accounted for approximately 28 percent of all homeless people in the United States and approximately 49 percent of all unsheltered homeless people, despite having 12 percent of the national population.

The PIT count is widely acknowledged to be an undercount. It is conducted on a single night in January. It misses people who are temporarily doubled up, couch surfing, in hospitals, in jail, or otherwise not visible on the night of the count. Studies comparing PIT counts to other methodologies suggest the true number of people experiencing homelessness in California at any point during a year may be two to three times the single-night count.

The 2024 PIT count results, released in early 2025, showed the first decline in California's count in years, dropping to approximately 171,521, a reduction of roughly 5.4 percent. HUD and state officials characterized the decline as evidence that investments were beginning to work. Critics noted that the decline followed changes in counting methodology in several jurisdictions and that year-over-year PIT count comparisons are unreliable due to methodological variability.

Los Angeles County alone accounts for roughly 75,000 of California's homeless population, more than any state except New York. The City of Los Angeles passed Measure HHH in 2016, authorizing $1.2 billion in bonds for supportive housing. By 2024, HHH had produced approximately 1,500 completed units at an average cost of $600,000 to $837,000 per unit, with several projects exceeding $700,000 per door. A 2024 city controller audit found that the program's per-unit costs had risen 53 percent above initial projections.

## The Per-Unit Problem

The cost of producing one unit of permanent supportive housing in California varies enormously by geography, program, and method. New construction in Los Angeles averages $600,000 to $837,000 per unit, depending on the data source and what costs are included. New construction in the Bay Area ranges from $700,000 to $1.1 million per unit. These figures include land, construction, prevailing wages, environmental compliance, architectural fees, permitting, financing costs, and developer fees.

Homekey's model of acquiring and converting existing buildings produced units at $220,000 to $290,000 on average, a dramatic reduction. The cost advantage came from bypassing new construction entirely: buying a functioning motel and converting rooms to studio apartments requires renovation, not ground-up building. The tradeoff is that converted motels are typically smaller units with limited common space, and the supply of suitable acquisition properties is finite.

Modular and prefabricated construction, promoted as a cost-reduction strategy, has not yet achieved scale in California. Several modular projects have been completed at costs of $300,000 to $450,000 per unit, below conventional construction but above Homekey conversion costs. Regulatory barriers, including local zoning, building codes designed for site-built construction, and limited factory capacity, have constrained modular deployment.

The operating cost of permanent supportive housing, separate from the capital cost of building it, is approximately $20,000 to $35,000 per unit per year for supportive services (case management, behavioral health, substance use treatment) plus whatever rental subsidy is required to cover operating expenses not covered by tenant rent. For a formerly homeless individual receiving SSI/SSP of approximately $1,100 per month, the subsidy gap between that income and operating costs is covered by project-based vouchers, operating reserves, or ongoing county or state funding.

The lifetime cost of maintaining one person in permanent supportive housing, including capital amortization and services, is approximately $40,000 to $60,000 per year. Studies comparing this to the cost of chronic homelessness, including emergency room visits, psychiatric hospitalizations, jail bookings, shelter stays, and outreach contacts, consistently find that housing is cost-neutral or cost-saving for the highest-utilizer chronically homeless population. For the broader homeless population, including people experiencing short-term homelessness due to eviction or job loss, the cost comparison is less favorable because their counterfactual costs are lower.

## The Inflow Problem

The fundamental challenge of California's homelessness crisis is not exits from homelessness but entries into it. The state's Homeless Management Information Systems record both entries and exits. In Los Angeles County, HMIS data shows that for every person who exits homelessness into permanent housing, approximately 1.2 to 1.5 people newly enter homelessness. The system is filling faster than it drains.

The primary drivers of entry into homelessness in California are economic: rents that consume more than 50 percent of income for the lowest-income households, a housing shortage of approximately 2.5 million units below what the state's population requires, evictions (formal and informal), and income shocks from job loss, medical debt, or family dissolution. Behavioral health conditions, including serious mental illness and substance use disorders, are present in approximately 30 to 35 percent of the chronically homeless population but are less prevalent among the broader homeless population, which is disproportionately composed of people who were housed until recently and became homeless primarily for economic reasons.

The McKinsey Global Institute estimated in 2022 that California would need to produce 3.5 million housing units over the following decade to meet demand and reduce housing cost burdens to affordable levels. Actual production runs approximately 100,000 to 120,000 units per year, roughly one-third of what is needed. The gap between housing production and housing need is the upstream condition that generates homelessness downstream. No amount of spending on homeless services can overcome a structural housing deficit that pushes more people into homelessness each year than the system can rehouse.

## The Accountability Gap

The State Auditor's January 2024 report found that California "cannot determine whether its homelessness programs are effective" because the state does not track spending and outcomes in a unified system. Individual programs report to individual agencies, which use different metrics, different definitions of success, and different reporting timelines. The auditor recommended that the state designate a single agency to coordinate homelessness efforts, establish standardized outcome metrics, and require all programs to report cost per outcome.

Governor Newsom created the California Interagency Council on Homelessness (Cal ICH) in 2017 to coordinate state efforts. The auditor found that Cal ICH lacked authority to direct spending, enforce reporting requirements, or hold agencies accountable for outcomes. It functioned as a coordination body without coordination power.

The Encampment Resolution Fund, created in 2023, required local jurisdictions to clear encampments and provide services to displaced residents. The program spent approximately $1.1 billion and reported moving thousands of individuals into shelter or housing. Critics, including homeless advocacy organizations, argued that encampment sweeps without sufficient housing supply simply displaces people from one location to another. A 2024 UC San Francisco study of encampment clearances in Los Angeles found that 60 percent of residents of cleared encampments were unsheltered again within 90 days.

The fundamental accountability problem is that California's spending on homelessness is distributed across so many programs, agencies, and levels of government that no one is responsible for the overall result. The federal government provides vouchers and grants. The state provides bonds, block grants, and program funding. Counties provide behavioral health and social services. Cities provide shelter and enforcement. Nonprofit providers deliver most direct services. Each entity tracks its own outputs: beds created, people served, vouchers issued. No entity tracks the system-wide outcome: is the total number of people experiencing homelessness going up or down, and why?

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has spent $24 billion and the problem has gotten worse. The state built a homelessness-industrial complex of programs, agencies, consultants, and providers that consumes billions annually while the population on the street grows. The per-unit costs of new supportive housing in Los Angeles have risen to levels that make solution impossible at scale: at $700,000 per unit, housing California's 181,000 homeless people would cost $127 billion in capital alone, plus billions annually in services. The state does not track whether its spending works, does not hold anyone accountable for results, and cannot explain where $24 billion went. The Homekey program demonstrated that units can be produced cheaply, but the state reverted to expensive new construction rather than scaling what worked. Meanwhile, the inflow of newly homeless people exceeds the outflow of those being rehoused, a dynamic that no amount of spending on the downstream symptom can reverse without addressing the upstream cause: a housing market that produces one-third of the units it needs.

The generous read is that California inherited a homelessness crisis decades in the making, driven by housing underproduction that dates to the 1970s, and has mounted the largest state response in American history. Homekey produced 15,000 units in three years at a fraction of conventional cost, an achievement no other state has matched. The 2024 PIT count decline, however modest, is the first evidence that spending is beginning to outpace inflows. HHAP's block grant structure gave local jurisdictions flexibility to address local conditions, a necessary design in a state where homelessness in San Francisco differs from homelessness in Fresno. The accountability gaps the auditor identified are real, but they reflect the difficulty of coordinating across a federal system, not the absence of effort. And the comparison between spending and outcomes ignores the counterfactual: what would homelessness look like in California if the state had spent nothing? The answer, visible in the 2019 count of 151,000 before the major spending began, is that the trajectory was already catastrophic. The spending may not have solved the problem. It may have prevented it from being worse.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The PIT count, while the official metric, captures a single night and misses the vast majority of people who experience homelessness during any given year. It does not capture flows: how many people became homeless, how long they remained homeless, and how they exited. HMIS data, which does capture flows in some jurisdictions, is incomplete because not all service contacts are recorded and many homeless people have no service contact at all.

The data does not capture the experience of homelessness: the health consequences, the trauma, the difficulty of accessing employment or education without a stable address, or the mortality. Studies of homeless populations in California find life expectancies 20 to 30 years below the general population. Unsheltered homelessness, the category that dominates California's count, is associated with dramatically elevated rates of assault, hypothermia, heat stroke, infection, and overdose death. The $24 billion figure does not include the costs borne by homeless individuals themselves, costs measured not in dollars but in years of life.

The data also does not separate what works from what does not at a program level. Some rapid rehousing programs achieve 80 percent housing retention at 12 months. Some achieve 40 percent. The state cannot identify which programs produce better outcomes because it does not systematically compare them. The State Auditor recommended randomized evaluations and standardized metrics. Neither has been implemented at scale.

And the data says nothing about the political economy of homelessness spending. The $700,000 per-unit cost of supportive housing in Los Angeles includes prevailing wages mandated by project labor agreements, environmental review costs, permitting delays measured in years, and community opposition to siting that adds litigation and redesign costs. These are policy choices, not laws of physics. Other jurisdictions, including Houston, which reduced its homeless population by 63 percent between 2011 and 2023, have produced housing at a fraction of California's cost by streamlining approvals, using existing buildings, and prioritizing speed over perfection. Whether California can adopt those models depends on whether the constituencies that benefit from the current system, including construction unions, environmental organizations, neighborhood groups, and the nonprofit homeless services sector itself, will accept the changes required to reduce costs and increase speed.

---

California spends more per homeless person than any jurisdiction in the world except possibly New York City. It has more homeless people than any state. It does not know whether its spending works. These three facts are not contradictory. They are the natural product of a state that treats homelessness as a spending problem rather than a systems problem. The money flows through 30 programs, nine departments, hundreds of local agencies, and thousands of nonprofit contracts, each tracked separately, each measured by its own outputs, none accountable for the collective outcome. The $24 billion bought units, beds, services, outreach contacts, and administrative capacity. What it did not buy, and what no amount of money can buy without systemic reform, is a rate of housing production that exceeds the rate at which Californians lose their housing. Until the inflow stops exceeding the outflow, every dollar spent on homelessness is a bucket thrown at a rising tide. The state knows this. Its own auditor said so. The question is whether the political system can act on what the data makes obvious, or whether it will authorize another $24 billion and count again.
