# California Counts: The Tax That Treats
## What a 1 percent millionaire's tax, $3.8 billion per year, 58 county mental health systems, and Proposition 1's $6.4 billion bond reveal about California's attempt to build a behavioral health system one ballot measure at a time

California's Mental Health Services Act (MHSA), approved by voters as Proposition 63 in 2004, imposed a 1 percent income tax surcharge on personal income above $1 million. The tax generates approximately $3.5 billion to $3.8 billion per year, depending on capital gains realizations and the number of millionaires filing California returns. Since its enactment, the MHSA has generated more than $40 billion in cumulative revenue, making it the largest dedicated mental health funding stream in the nation. The money is distributed to California's 58 counties, which operate the public mental health system through a patchwork of county behavioral health departments, community-based organizations, and contract providers.

The MHSA was designed to fund prevention, early intervention, and community-based services for people with serious mental illness. It allocates revenue across five components: Community Services and Supports (76 percent of county allocations), Prevention and Early Intervention (19 percent), Innovation (5 percent), Workforce Education and Training, and Capital Facilities and Technology. The largest component, Community Services and Supports, funds Full Service Partnerships (FSPs), intensive wraparound programs that provide housing, treatment, employment support, and case management for individuals with the most severe mental illness. An FSP serves approximately 60,000 to 70,000 Californians at any given time.

## Proposition 1: The $6.4 Billion Overhaul

In March 2024, California voters approved Proposition 1 by a margin of less than 1 percent, the narrowest statewide ballot measure victory in decades. Proposition 1 had two components: a $6.38 billion general obligation bond for behavioral health treatment facilities and housing for people with behavioral health conditions, and a restructuring of the MHSA (renamed the Behavioral Health Services Act, or BHSA) that redirected approximately 30 percent of county MHSA allocations toward housing interventions for people with serious mental illness or substance use disorders.

The bond allocates approximately $4.4 billion for behavioral health treatment and residential care facilities and $2 billion for housing, including permanent supportive housing and transitional housing for veterans. The facility construction is intended to address a critical gap: California has lost approximately 50 percent of its psychiatric inpatient beds since the 1990s, from approximately 8,000 to approximately 4,000, while the number of Californians with serious mental illness has remained stable or increased. The state has approximately 10 psychiatric inpatient beds per 100,000 population, well below the national average of approximately 15 per 100,000 and far below the levels that mental health professionals consider adequate.

The BHSA restructuring was more controversial. By redirecting 30 percent of county MHSA allocations toward housing, the law reduces the funding available for the community-based prevention and early intervention programs that counties have built over two decades. County behavioral health directors warned that the redirection would force cuts to existing programs, particularly prevention programs that serve children and transition-age youth. Supporters argued that housing is the most critical intervention for people with serious mental illness and that the MHSA's existing allocation structure had failed to produce adequate housing outcomes despite 20 years of funding.

## The County Patchwork

California's 58 counties operate functionally independent mental health systems, with wildly varying capacity, quality, and access. Los Angeles County, with approximately 10 million residents, operates the largest county mental health system in the nation, spending approximately $3.5 billion per year (including MHSA, Medi-Cal, and county general fund sources) and serving approximately 250,000 clients annually. Small rural counties like Alpine, Modoc, and Sierra have mental health budgets in the single-digit millions and may have one or two licensed clinicians serving the entire county.

The variation produces access disparities that are geographic lotteries. A person experiencing a psychiatric crisis in San Francisco has access to multiple crisis stabilization units, a Psychiatric Emergency Services center at Zuckerberg San Francisco General Hospital, and a network of community mental health providers. A person experiencing the same crisis in a rural county may face a 100-mile drive to the nearest psychiatric inpatient bed, hours in a general hospital emergency department that lacks psychiatric capacity, and discharge with a referral to a provider with a three-month wait list. The county governance structure means that the quality of public mental health care in California depends on where you live, a variation that no state law has equalized.

The workforce shortage compounds the access problem. California has approximately 5,000 psychiatrists, producing a ratio of approximately 12.5 per 100,000 population, below the estimated need. The distribution is heavily concentrated in urban coastal areas: the Bay Area and Los Angeles have adequate or surplus psychiatric capacity while the Central Valley and rural north have severe shortages. The MHSA's Workforce Education and Training component has funded approximately 10,000 stipends and scholarships for mental health professionals, but the pipeline has not kept pace with demand, and the competition for psychiatrists and clinical psychologists from private practice and telehealth platforms pays two to three times what county behavioral health departments offer.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has spent more than $40 billion on the MHSA since 2004 and cannot demonstrate that the investment has reduced the prevalence of untreated serious mental illness, reduced homelessness attributable to mental illness, or reduced the number of Californians in psychiatric crisis who end up in jails and emergency departments rather than treatment settings. The 58-county governance structure produces 58 different systems with no statewide standards for access, quality, or outcomes. The annual MHSA reporting that counties submit to the Mental Health Services Oversight and Accountability Commission (MHSOAC) tracks spending by component but does not systematically track patient outcomes: whether the people served by MHSA-funded programs are housed, employed, staying out of the hospital, or experiencing symptom improvement. Proposition 1 restructured the funding again, this time toward housing, without resolving the fundamental accountability gap: the state taxes millionaires, distributes the money to counties, and does not systematically measure whether the money produces health.

The generous read is that the MHSA built the community mental health infrastructure that California did not have before 2004. The Full Service Partnerships, which provide intensive wraparound services to the most severely ill, have demonstrated outcomes in peer-reviewed research: reduced hospitalization, reduced homelessness, reduced criminal justice involvement. Prevention and Early Intervention programs serve hundreds of thousands of Californians per year, including school-based mental health services, crisis intervention training for law enforcement, and stigma reduction campaigns that have measurably increased help-seeking behavior. Proposition 1's investment in facilities will produce the first significant expansion of psychiatric inpatient and residential treatment capacity in decades. The system is imperfect, fragmented, and difficult to evaluate at scale. It is also the largest dedicated public mental health investment in the nation, and it exists because California's voters chose to tax millionaires to pay for it, twice.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The MHSOAC publishes county-level MHSA expenditure data and annual updates. It does not publish a statewide, standardized dataset of client outcomes. Individual county evaluations of FSPs and PEI programs exist, but the methodologies, metrics, and reporting periods vary by county, making statewide aggregation unreliable. The fundamental question, whether $3.8 billion per year in MHSA funding is producing better mental health outcomes for Californians than would occur without the funding, is not answerable from the publicly available data because the state has never established a baseline measurement and outcome-tracking system commensurate with the scale of the investment.
