# California Counts: The Horizon Bet
## What 25 GW of planned offshore wind capacity, zero turbines currently installed, a 2045 target, and floating platform technology that has never been deployed at commercial scale reveal about the energy source California is counting on before it exists

California's offshore wind energy target, established by AB 525 (2021), calls for 2 to 5 gigawatts of installed capacity by 2030 and 25 gigawatts by 2045. The 25 GW target would generate approximately 90,000 gigawatt-hours per year, enough to power approximately 15 million homes and representing approximately 25 percent of California's projected 2045 electricity demand. As of 2026, California has zero installed offshore wind capacity. The state's first lease areas, off Morro Bay (Central Coast) and Humboldt Bay (North Coast), were auctioned by the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management in December 2022 for a total of $757 million in winning bids across five lease areas totaling approximately 373,000 acres.

California's offshore wind challenge is fundamentally different from the East Coast's. The continental shelf drops off steeply along the California coast, meaning that water depths exceed 60 meters within a few miles of shore. Fixed-bottom turbines (the technology used in all current U.S. offshore wind projects and most European installations) cannot be anchored in these depths. California requires floating offshore wind technology: turbines mounted on floating platforms anchored to the seafloor by mooring lines. Floating offshore wind has been demonstrated at pilot scale (single turbines or small arrays) in Norway, Portugal, Scotland, and Japan, but no commercial-scale floating wind farm (50 or more turbines) has been built anywhere in the world.

---

## The Infrastructure Gap

Building 25 GW of offshore wind requires port infrastructure (marshaling and assembly ports with sufficient acreage, water depth, and heavy-lift capacity), transmission infrastructure (subsea cables connecting offshore arrays to onshore grid interconnections), and manufacturing capacity (turbine components, floating platforms, mooring systems, and cables). California currently has none of this infrastructure at the scale required.

The California Energy Commission's offshore wind port assessment identified Humboldt Bay and the Port of Long Beach as potential marshaling ports, but both require hundreds of millions of dollars in upgrades. Transmission planning by CAISO has identified the need for approximately $10 billion to $20 billion in new transmission to connect offshore wind to load centers. The Jones Act (which requires vessels moving cargo between U.S. ports to be U.S.-built, U.S.-flagged, and U.S.-crewed) adds cost and complexity: the specialized wind turbine installation vessels used in European offshore wind are foreign-flagged, and no Jones Act-compliant installation vessel for floating platforms currently exists.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California is planning its energy future around a technology that does not exist at commercial scale, in an ocean environment that has never hosted a commercial wind farm, using infrastructure that has not been built, connected by transmission that has not been planned to completion, installed by vessels that do not exist, on a timeline (2030 first power, 2045 full buildout) that assumes resolution of dozens of technical, regulatory, environmental, and economic challenges simultaneously. The 25 GW target is an aspiration, not a plan. The $757 million in lease payments purchased the right to develop, not the certainty of development. Every floating wind project globally is currently behind schedule and over budget. California is betting 25 percent of its 2045 electricity supply on the successful commercialization of an unproven technology at unprecedented scale.

The generous read is that all energy technologies were unproven before they were proven, and the history of technology deployment shows that costs decline and capabilities improve once markets are created. Fixed-bottom offshore wind was considered exotic and expensive in 2005; by 2024, it is the fastest-growing energy source in Europe. Floating wind follows the same trajectory on a 10-to-15-year lag. California's 25 GW target creates a guaranteed market that will attract global capital, manufacturing investment, and engineering talent. The state's deep-water wind resource (average wind speeds of 8 to 10 meters per second, capacity factors of 50 to 60 percent) is among the best in the world. The question is not whether floating offshore wind will work (it works at pilot scale today) but whether it will reach commercial scale on the timeline California needs. The target makes that timeline achievable by creating the demand signal that drives investment.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Energy Commission publishes offshore wind planning documents, resource assessments, and port studies. It does not publish cost projections for California-specific floating wind (levelized cost of energy estimates range from $80 to $150 per megawatt-hour depending on assumptions, versus $30 to $60 for onshore wind and utility-scale solar). The fundamental economic question, whether offshore wind will be cost-competitive with the alternative of building more onshore renewables plus long-duration storage, depends on cost trajectories that cannot be known until commercial-scale projects are built.
