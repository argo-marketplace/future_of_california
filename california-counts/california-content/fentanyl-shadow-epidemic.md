# California Counts: The Shadow Epidemic
## What 12,000 overdose deaths per year, a 1,100 percent increase since 2015, $3.8 billion in federal settlement funds, and a 58-county patchwork of harm reduction reveal about the crisis California saw coming and could not stop

California recorded approximately 12,200 opioid-involved overdose deaths in 2024, the vast majority involving illicitly manufactured fentanyl. That figure has risen from approximately 2,000 in 2015, a 1,100 percent increase in nine years. Fentanyl, a synthetic opioid 50 to 100 times more potent than morphine, has displaced heroin and prescription opioids as the dominant driver of overdose mortality. It is cheap to manufacture, easy to transport, and lethal in quantities measured in milligrams. Two milligrams, approximately the weight of a few grains of salt, is a potentially fatal dose. The drug is pressed into counterfeit pills designed to resemble prescription medications (oxycodone, Xanax, Adderall) and mixed into methamphetamine, cocaine, and heroin supplies, meaning users often do not know they are consuming fentanyl until they stop breathing.

California's overdose death rate of approximately 31 per 100,000 residents places it in the middle nationally, below states like West Virginia (90), Tennessee (56), and Ohio (44), but above the national average of approximately 28. In absolute numbers, California's 12,200 deaths are the second-highest of any state behind Florida. The demographic profile of overdose deaths has shifted: the crisis that began in Appalachian communities with prescription opioids has spread to urban Black and Latino communities, the homeless population, and the working-age population broadly. In San Francisco, the Tenderloin district's open-air drug market has become a national symbol of the crisis, with overdose deaths per square mile among the highest in the country.

## The Supply Chain: From Precursors to Street Sales

Illicit fentanyl consumed in California is predominantly manufactured in Mexico by transnational criminal organizations, primarily the Sinaloa Cartel and the Jalisco New Generation Cartel, using precursor chemicals imported from China. The precursors, principally 4-ANPP and NPP, are synthesized in Chinese chemical plants and shipped to Mexico by sea, through Central American transshipment points, or by direct mail. The DEA has identified more than 300 chemical companies in China that have supplied fentanyl precursors.

In Mexico, the precursors are synthesized into fentanyl in clandestine laboratories, pressed into pills or packaged as powder, and transported across the U.S.-Mexico border. The primary entry points are the San Ysidro and Otay Mesa ports of entry in San Diego County, the Calexico port in Imperial County, and various points along the Arizona border. Customs and Border Protection seized approximately 27,000 pounds of fentanyl at the southern border in fiscal year 2023, a figure that law enforcement estimates represents 5 to 10 percent of total flow.

The street price of a fentanyl pill in California is approximately $2 to $5, making it cheaper than a cup of coffee. A kilogram of fentanyl, which can be pressed into approximately 500,000 pills, costs the manufacturing organization roughly $5,000 to produce and $50,000 to transport to the United States. At a street price of $3 per pill, a single kilogram generates $1.5 million in revenue. The economics are as compelling for the suppliers as the pharmacology is devastating for the users. No enforcement strategy has demonstrated the ability to significantly restrict supply of a product with these margins.

## The Response: $3.8 Billion in Settlements and a Patchwork of Programs

California is receiving approximately $3.8 billion over 18 years from the national opioid settlements with pharmaceutical manufacturers (Johnson & Johnson), distributors (McKesson, Cardinal Health, AmerisourceBergen), and pharmacy chains (CVS, Walgreens, Walmart). The funds are distributed to the state (approximately 15 percent), counties (approximately 70 percent), and cities (approximately 15 percent), with expenditure restricted to opioid abatement activities: treatment, harm reduction, prevention, and recovery services.

The settlement structure creates a fragmented response. Each county determines how to spend its allocation, subject to broad opioid abatement guidelines but with significant local discretion. Some counties have invested in medication-assisted treatment expansion (buprenorphine, methadone, naltrexone). Others have funded naloxone distribution. Others have invested in recovery housing or workforce training. The absence of a statewide strategy means that a person experiencing opioid use disorder in Alameda County has access to a different set of services than someone in Fresno County, with treatment capacity and harm reduction availability varying by jurisdiction.

Naloxone (Narcan), the opioid overdose reversal medication, is the most widely deployed harm reduction tool. California enacted SB 10 (2023), which requires schools to maintain naloxone and allows pharmacists to dispense it without a prescription. CalRx, the state's generic drug program, distributes free naloxone. Approximately 3 million doses of naloxone were distributed statewide in 2024 through a combination of state programs, county health departments, and community-based organizations. The Department of Health Care Services estimates that naloxone reversed approximately 50,000 overdoses in California in 2024, a figure that represents lives saved but also the scale of the ongoing crisis.

Medication-assisted treatment with buprenorphine or methadone is the gold standard for opioid use disorder, with evidence showing 50 to 75 percent reductions in overdose mortality for patients who remain in treatment. California expanded Medi-Cal coverage of MAT through the Drug Medi-Cal Organized Delivery System waiver, and the number of buprenorphine prescribers in the state has increased from approximately 3,000 in 2016 to over 10,000 in 2024. Treatment capacity has expanded but remains insufficient: an estimated 50 to 60 percent of Californians with opioid use disorder do not receive any form of treatment in a given year, due to a combination of treatment slot scarcity, wait times, geographic access barriers, and individual reluctance to seek care.

## The Harm Reduction Debate

San Francisco authorized the first sanctioned supervised drug consumption site in the United States, after years of legal and political debate. The facility, operated under a city health department license with a state waiver, allows individuals to consume pre-obtained drugs under medical supervision, with naloxone and resuscitation equipment immediately available. Proponents cite evidence from international facilities (more than 120 operate worldwide, including in Canada, Australia, and Europe) showing zero overdose deaths at supervised sites and significant reductions in public drug use, needle sharing, and emergency department visits. Opponents argue that supervised consumption normalizes drug use, attracts drug markets, and delays recovery.

The supervised consumption debate illustrates the broader tension in California's drug policy between harm reduction (meeting people where they are, reducing death and disease without requiring abstinence) and enforcement (reducing supply, deterring use, mandating treatment). Governor Newsom vetoed two supervised consumption site bills in 2022, citing concerns about the approach and its implementation, before a modified version received authorization for San Francisco only. The state's approach is effectively permissive at the local level (allowing cities to establish sites with state approval) and cautious at the state level (not mandating or funding sites statewide).

The enforcement response has intensified in parallel. California passed several fentanyl-specific laws in 2023-24, including enhanced penalties for fentanyl trafficking (SB 44, imposing 10-year enhancements for trafficking resulting in death), requiring fentanyl awareness curriculum in schools, and expanding drug-checking services (fentanyl test strips, which were previously classified as drug paraphernalia, were legalized by SB 234 in 2022). The combination of harm reduction and enhanced enforcement represents a both-and approach that satisfies neither the advocates who want full harm reduction without criminalization nor the enforcement advocates who want penalties severe enough to deter trafficking.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California watched the opioid crisis unfold for a decade, first in Appalachia, then in the Midwest, then on its own streets, and failed to build the treatment infrastructure, the harm reduction network, or the enforcement capacity to prevent 12,000 annual deaths. The $3.8 billion in settlement funds, distributed across 58 counties with no statewide coordination, will produce 58 different responses to a crisis that does not respect county boundaries. Treatment capacity reaches barely half of those who need it. Supervised consumption, the single most evidence-supported intervention for preventing overdose death, exists in one city. Naloxone distribution is heroic and insufficient: reversing 50,000 overdoses per year means 50,000 people nearly died and will be at risk again tomorrow. The enforcement strategy, targeting trafficking while maintaining harm reduction for users, is coherent in theory and overwhelmed in practice by a supply chain that produces fentanyl pills for pennies and delivers them to every neighborhood in the state. California is spending billions on a crisis it is not winning.

The generous read is that California's overdose death rate, while horrific in absolute terms, is significantly below the hardest-hit states, reflecting a combination of earlier Medi-Cal expansion (which provides treatment access to the population most at risk), earlier adoption of harm reduction (naloxone distribution, syringe services, drug-checking), and a public health infrastructure that, however strained, has prevented the per-capita mortality rates that devastated states with fewer resources. The 10,000 buprenorphine prescribers represent a tripling in eight years. The 3 million naloxone doses distributed represent the most aggressive reversal medication program in the country. The opioid settlement funds, however fragmented, represent a generational investment in treatment and prevention infrastructure that did not exist before. And California's willingness to authorize supervised consumption, however limited and tentative, places it ahead of every other state in evidence-based harm reduction. The crisis is not solved. The response is not sufficient. But the tools are deployed, the investment is funded, and the trajectory of innovation, from naloxone to MAT expansion to supervised consumption to CalRx distribution, is more advanced than anywhere else in the country.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Overdose Surveillance Dashboard reports overdose deaths by county, drug type, age, race, and gender. It does not report non-fatal overdoses comprehensively: emergency department data captures events that result in hospital contact, but many overdoses are reversed by bystanders, outreach workers, or the users themselves and never appear in any dataset. The true number of overdose events in California is estimated at 5 to 10 times the death count, or 60,000 to 120,000 per year.

The data does not capture the economic cost of the fentanyl crisis. Overdose deaths among working-age adults (the median age of fentanyl overdose death is approximately 38) represent lost productivity, lost tax revenue, and increased social service costs for surviving dependents. A 2022 Congressional Joint Economic Committee estimate placed the national economic cost of the opioid crisis at approximately $1.5 trillion per year. California's proportional share, roughly 12 percent, would be approximately $180 billion, a figure that dwarfs the settlement funds.

The data also does not capture what public health researchers call "deaths of despair," the broader category that includes overdose, alcohol-related mortality, and suicide, all of which share overlapping risk factors: economic dislocation, social isolation, untreated mental illness, and housing instability. Fentanyl does not kill in a vacuum. It kills people whose lives have already been made fragile by conditions that the overdose data does not record.
