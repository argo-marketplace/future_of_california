# California Counts: The Aging Concrete
## What 1,530 dams, a 57-year average age, the Oroville spillway collapse, and $90 billion in deferred water infrastructure reveal about the plumbing California built for a climate that no longer exists

California has approximately 1,530 dams regulated by the Division of Safety of Dams within the Department of Water Resources. Their average age is approximately 57 years. More than 300 are classified as "high hazard," meaning that their failure would result in probable loss of life. Another 200 are classified as "significant hazard." The dams store approximately 42 million acre-feet of surface water, the backbone of the state's water delivery system and the physical infrastructure that makes California's agriculture, cities, and environment possible.

The system was built for a climate that delivered water as predictable winter snowpack and spring runoff. That climate is disappearing. Warming temperatures are shifting precipitation from snow to rain, reducing the natural storage that snowpack provides and concentrating runoff into shorter, more intense flood pulses. Atmospheric rivers, the narrow corridors of tropical moisture that deliver 30 to 50 percent of California's annual precipitation in a handful of storms, are becoming more intense under climate change. The 2023 winter delivered atmospheric rivers that filled every major reservoir in the state and tested spillway capacity at multiple dams. The infrastructure designed for 20th-century hydrology is now receiving 21st-century storms.

## Oroville: The $1.1 Billion Warning

The Oroville Dam, at 770 feet the tallest dam in the United States, nearly failed in February 2017. Heavy rains filled Lake Oroville, the State Water Project's principal storage reservoir, to capacity. When DWR opened the main spillway to release water, the concrete surface eroded and collapsed, creating a crater 250 feet wide, 500 feet long, and 45 feet deep. Releases were diverted to the emergency spillway, an unlined hillside that had never been used. The emergency spillway immediately began eroding, threatening to send an uncontrolled torrent of water downstream. Nearly 190,000 people in Oroville, Yuba City, and Marysville were evacuated, the largest dam-related evacuation in American history.

The dam held. The rain stopped. The crisis passed. But the investigation revealed systemic failures: the main spillway had known structural deficiencies identified in inspections but not repaired. The emergency spillway had never been tested under actual flow conditions. The dam's operations protocols did not account for the intensity of the storm. The repair and reconstruction cost approximately $1.1 billion, funded by DWR and partially reimbursed by FEMA.

The Oroville crisis prompted a nationwide reassessment of dam safety. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission required enhanced spillway inspections at dams it regulates. California's Division of Safety of Dams increased its inspection staff and revised its review protocols. But the fundamental issue, aging infrastructure designed for a different climate and stressed by storms the original engineers did not anticipate, applies to hundreds of dams across the state.

## The Deferred Maintenance Problem

The American Society of Civil Engineers' 2025 Infrastructure Report Card gave California's dams a grade of C-. The state's total water infrastructure (dams, levees, canals, aqueducts, treatment plants, and distribution systems) faces an estimated $90 billion in deferred maintenance and necessary upgrades, according to DWR and the Association of California Water Agencies. The $90 billion includes approximately $12 billion for dam safety improvements, $30 billion for levee upgrades (primarily in the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta), $20 billion for water treatment and distribution system replacement (lead service lines, aging mains, treatment plant upgrades), and $28 billion for conveyance infrastructure (canal lining, aqueduct repair, pump station replacement).

The Delta levee system is the most vulnerable single element. The Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta, where the state's two largest rivers converge before flowing to San Francisco Bay, is protected by approximately 1,100 miles of levees, many built in the 19th century by Chinese laborers using hand tools and local peat soil. The levees protect islands that sit 10 to 25 feet below sea level due to decades of peat oxidation and subsidence. A major earthquake or sustained flooding could breach multiple levees simultaneously, flooding the islands, drawing saltwater into the Delta, and disrupting the water supply for 27 million Californians and 3 million acres of farmland that depends on Delta exports through the State Water Project and Central Valley Project pumps.

DWR's Delta Risk Management Strategy estimated that a catastrophic levee failure in the Delta could disrupt water exports for 18 months and cause $40 billion to $70 billion in economic damage. The probability of a major levee failure in any given year is estimated at 2 to 5 percent, a figure that compounds to a 40 to 75 percent probability over a 30-year period. The Delta levees are the single most consequential infrastructure vulnerability in California, larger in potential impact than any earthquake scenario except a direct hit on a major metropolitan area.

## Climate Stress: Too Much and Too Little

Climate change is imposing contradictory stresses on California's dam infrastructure. In wet years, atmospheric rivers deliver precipitation in concentrated bursts that test spillway capacity and flood control operations. In dry years, reduced snowpack means less storage refill and longer periods at low reservoir levels, which exposes concrete and earthen structures to thermal cycling and desiccation cracking.

The shift from snow to rain is particularly consequential for reservoir operations. Snowpack acts as natural storage, releasing water gradually over spring and summer. Rain runs off immediately. A climate that delivers the same total precipitation as rain rather than snow requires more reservoir storage capacity to capture the runoff, but reservoir capacity is fixed. Building new dams in California is effectively prohibited by environmental regulation, cost, and the absence of suitable sites. The Sites Reservoir project in the Sacramento Valley, the only major new reservoir under active development, is projected to cost $4 billion to $6 billion for 1.5 million acre-feet of off-stream storage, a per-acre-foot cost that exceeds any previous California water storage project.

The alternative to new surface storage is groundwater recharge: capturing flood flows and percolating them into underground aquifers for later extraction. The Flood-MAR (Managed Aquifer Recharge) concept, promoted by DWR, envisions using agricultural land as temporary recharge basins during atmospheric river events. Pilot projects have demonstrated feasibility, but scaling requires infrastructure (conveyance channels, percolation basins), willing landowners, water rights modifications, and institutional coordination that does not yet exist at the scale needed.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California built the most ambitious water storage and delivery system in the world between 1930 and 1970, then stopped building and started deferring. The dams are 57 years old on average. The levees are 150 years old. The canals are cracking. The Oroville spillway collapsed because known deficiencies were not repaired. The Delta levees have a 40 to 75 percent chance of catastrophic failure in the next 30 years. The $90 billion in deferred maintenance has no funding source. And the climate that the system was designed for, reliable snowpack, moderate storm intensity, predictable runoff timing, is disappearing. California designed its water infrastructure for the 20th century's climate, built it in the 20th century, and has maintained it like a 21st-century state that believes infrastructure is someone else's problem.

The generous read is that California's dam and water infrastructure has performed remarkably well for its age and the stresses imposed on it. The 2023 atmospheric river sequence filled reservoirs to capacity without a single dam failure, a testament to the engineering margin built into the original designs. The Oroville repair, completed in less than two years, demonstrated the state's capacity for rapid infrastructure response when the political will exists. DWR's increased inspection regime, the Flood-MAR pilots, and the Sites Reservoir project are evidence of forward-looking investment. And the fundamental achievement of the system, delivering 42 million acre-feet of stored water to 40 million people and 9 million acres of farmland, remains operational and essential.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

DWR publishes dam safety inspection reports and reservoir storage data. It does not publish a statewide assessment of the remaining useful life of individual dams or a prioritized capital needs list with cost estimates at the dam level. The $90 billion deferred maintenance figure is an aggregate estimate. The breakdown by individual facility, which would allow prioritization and targeted investment, is not publicly available in a comprehensive format. The state knows its dams are aging. It does not publish a facility-by-facility accounting of how much time and money each one needs.
