# California Counts: The Deferred Paradise
## What 280 park units, 340 miles of coastline, a $9 billion maintenance backlog, and 68 million annual visitors reveal about the natural estate California preserved and then stopped maintaining

The California Department of Parks and Recreation manages 280 park units encompassing approximately 1.6 million acres, 340 miles of coastline, 970 miles of lake and river frontage, 15,000 campsites, and over 4,000 buildings and structures. The system includes redwood groves, gold rush ghost towns, desert wildlands, wetland preserves, and the most visited state park in the nation (Old Town San Diego, at approximately 8 million visitors per year). It drew approximately 68 million visits in the most recent reporting year, making it one of the most heavily used public park systems in the world.

The system's operating budget is approximately $850 million per year, funded through a combination of the general fund (approximately 40 percent), park revenue (entrance fees, camping fees, concessions, approximately 35 percent), and special funds and federal sources (approximately 25 percent). The per-visit cost to the state is approximately $12.50. The estimated economic impact of state park visitation, spending by visitors on lodging, food, fuel, equipment, and recreation, exceeds $6 billion per year, producing a return on the state's investment of approximately 7 to 1.

## The Backlog

The deferred maintenance backlog for California's state park system is estimated at approximately $8 billion to $9 billion. The backlog includes deteriorating roads and bridges, aging water and wastewater systems, structurally deficient buildings, eroding trails, outdated campground infrastructure, and historic structures that are actively deteriorating. Approximately 25 percent of the system's buildings are in poor or critical condition. Many campgrounds, designed and built in the 1960s and 1970s, have not been significantly upgraded since construction: the utility hookups, restrooms, and access roads reflect the expectations and standards of a half-century ago.

The annual maintenance budget allocates approximately $150 million to $200 million for capital outlay and deferred maintenance, a pace that does not reduce the backlog because new maintenance needs accumulate faster than the existing backlog is addressed. At the current pace, eliminating the backlog would take 40 to 50 years, by which time the assets repaired first would have aged into disrepair again. The cycle is the familiar story of California's public infrastructure: build it, defer maintenance, let the backlog grow, and eventually face a bill that is orders of magnitude larger than the ongoing maintenance would have been.

Proposition 68 (2018) provided $1.3 billion for state park infrastructure and access improvements. Proposition 4 (2024) added additional park funding. These bond measures have funded significant individual projects, but one-time bond funding cannot solve a structural maintenance deficit that requires sustained annual appropriation. The bonds build new facilities and rehabilitate the worst cases. They do not fund the routine, unglamorous, ongoing maintenance that prevents facilities from becoming deferred maintenance in the first place.

## The Equity Gap

California's state parks are not equally accessible to all Californians. A 2019 Parks Forward Commission analysis found that park usage patterns skew toward white, higher-income, suburban and rural populations. Communities of color and low-income urban populations, particularly in the Inland Empire, Central Valley, and dense urban neighborhoods of Los Angeles and the Bay Area, have significantly lower visitation rates. The causes are multiple: distance from parks (many state parks are in rural or coastal locations far from population centers), cost of entry (day-use fees of $10 to $15 plus the cost of driving), lack of public transit access, and cultural and linguistic barriers (park signage, programming, and marketing have historically been oriented toward English-speaking audiences).

The Outdoor Equity Grant Program, funded through Proposition 68, provides grants to community organizations that transport underserved youth and families to state parks. The program has served hundreds of thousands of participants but operates at a scale far below the access gap. An estimated 11 million Californians, disproportionately low-income and people of color, live in neighborhoods with insufficient access to parks and open space of any kind (local, state, or national), according to the California Department of Parks and Recreation's Park Access Tool.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California preserved one of the most magnificent state park systems in the world and then declined to maintain it. The $9 billion backlog is the accumulated cost of decades of deferred maintenance, annual budget cycles that prioritized short-term spending over long-term asset preservation, and the political reality that maintenance is invisible work that generates no ribbon cuttings. The 280 park units are slowly deteriorating: roads that wash out and are not repaired, buildings that leak and are not re-roofed, water systems that fail and are replaced with temporary fixes. The bond measures provide periodic infusions that address the most urgent needs but do not change the structural dynamic. And the equity gap means that the system's benefits, its landscapes, its history, its recreation, flow disproportionately to the Californians who already have the most access to nature, while the communities with the least green space and the highest pollution burden are the least likely to visit.

The generous read is that 280 park units across 1.6 million acres, maintained and operated for 68 million annual visits at a per-visit cost of $12.50, is a remarkable public good by any standard. The state park system preserves ecosystems (old-growth redwoods, desert tortoise habitat, coastal wetlands) that would have been developed without public protection. It provides the most affordable vacation option available to California families: a campsite costs $25 to $50 per night, a fraction of the cost of any other family vacation. The Outdoor Equity grants and the Parks Access Tool represent a genuine institutional commitment to closing the access gap. And the $9 billion backlog, while large, reflects the accumulated cost of maintaining 1.6 million acres of public land in a state subject to earthquakes, wildfires, floods, and 68 million visitors per year. The backlog is a measure of the system's size and the severity of the demands placed on it, not a measure of neglect alone.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Department of Parks and Recreation publishes visitation data, but the methodology has historically relied on vehicle counts at park entrances, which undercount walk-in visitors, cyclists, and visitors to parks without staffed entry stations. A visitor counting methodology upgrade is underway but not yet deployed systemwide. The 68 million figure is an estimate, not a census.

The deferred maintenance backlog is itself an estimate based on facility condition assessments that are not conducted on a uniform schedule across all 280 park units. The actual backlog may be higher or lower than the published figure because not all assets have been recently assessed. The infrastructure data the state needs to accurately prioritize maintenance spending is itself a form of deferred maintenance: the inventory and assessment systems have not been fully funded.
