# California Counts: The Floor and the Ceiling
## What a $16 minimum wage, $20 for fast food, a $24 living wage, and 3.2 million workers earning within $2 of the floor reveal about the wage policy California uses to fight poverty in the most expensive state in the nation

California's statewide minimum wage is $16.00 per hour as of 2024, the second-highest state minimum in the nation (behind Washington at $16.28). Approximately 3.2 million California workers, roughly 17 percent of the workforce, earn within $2 of the minimum wage. They are concentrated in food service, retail, agriculture, personal care, building maintenance, and warehouse operations. They are disproportionately female (55 percent), Latino (50 percent), and young (approximately 30 percent under age 25). The minimum wage increase from $8.00 in 2014 to $16.00 in 2024, a doubling over a decade, was enacted through SB 3 (2016) and subsequent annual adjustments indexed to the Consumer Price Index.

AB 1228 (2023) created a separate minimum wage for fast-food workers of $20.00 per hour, effective April 2024. The law also established the Fast Food Council, a first-in-the-nation sectoral bargaining body with the authority to set wages and working conditions for fast-food employees statewide. The $20 fast-food minimum is approximately 25 percent above the general minimum, creating a wage premium for an industry that has historically been among the lowest-paying in the economy. The law was vigorously opposed by the restaurant industry and supported by SEIU, the union that organized the "Fight for $15" campaign that drove minimum wage increases across California and nationally.

## The Living Wage Gap

The gap between the minimum wage and the living wage, the hourly rate required to cover basic expenses (housing, food, transportation, healthcare, childcare, taxes) without public assistance, is the central tension in California's wage policy. The MIT Living Wage Calculator estimates the living wage for a single adult with no children in California at approximately $24 per hour. For a single adult with one child, it exceeds $38 per hour. For a two-adult, two-child household with one working adult, it exceeds $42 per hour.

The $16 minimum wage, while high by national standards, is approximately 67 percent of the living wage for a single adult and approximately 42 percent of the living wage for a single parent. A full-time minimum-wage worker earning $33,280 per year qualifies for Medi-Cal, CalFresh, subsidized childcare, and housing assistance in most California counties. The state's minimum-wage workforce is, by design, a publicly subsidized workforce: the minimum wage is set below the cost of living, and public programs fill the gap.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's minimum wage policy is a half-measure that creates the appearance of worker protection while leaving 3.2 million workers unable to afford the state they live in without public assistance. The $16 minimum wage is a poverty wage in a state where median rent exceeds $2,000 per month and a one-bedroom apartment requires an hourly wage of $39 to afford at the federal affordability standard of 30 percent of income. The fast-food minimum of $20 is closer to adequate but still below the living wage, and it applies to a single industry while workers in equally demanding jobs (home healthcare, childcare, agriculture) earn less. The minimum wage is the floor. The ceiling, the cost of living in California, is so far above it that the floor provides little protection.

The generous read is that California's minimum wage increases have delivered the largest wage gains for low-income workers in the state's history. The doubling of the minimum from $8 to $16 over a decade increased annual full-time earnings from $16,640 to $33,280, a $16,640 increase that directly benefited the 3.2 million workers at or near the floor. Research by the UC Berkeley Labor Center found that the minimum wage increases reduced poverty rates among affected workers by approximately 10 percent and reduced public assistance expenditures by several hundred million dollars per year. The fast-food minimum established a sectoral bargaining model that could be extended to other low-wage industries. The increases are not sufficient to close the living-wage gap. They have materially improved the economic conditions of millions of California's lowest-paid workers.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The EDD publishes wage data by industry and occupation. It does not publish data on the number of workers earning exactly the minimum wage versus slightly above it, the industries and occupations most affected by minimum wage increases, or the employment effects (hours reduced, positions eliminated, businesses closed) attributable to minimum wage changes. The employment effects of California's minimum wage increases are studied by academic researchers using various methodologies that produce contested results. The state does not conduct its own impact assessment.
