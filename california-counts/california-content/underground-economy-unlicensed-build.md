# California Counts: The Underground Build
## What one in six construction jobs off the books, a $774 million tax gap, 20,000 annual complaints, and a 400 percent increase in underground construction employment since 1972 reveal about the unlicensed labor force building California in the shadows

California requires a license to perform any construction project valued at $500 or more, a threshold set in 1962 and never adjusted for inflation. In 1962 dollars, $500 purchased a significant home repair. In 2026 dollars, $500 covers approximately two hours of a plumber's time and a replacement faucet. The practical effect of the unadjusted threshold is that virtually every construction project in California, from a bathroom tile replacement to a high-rise tower, requires a licensed contractor. The gap between this legal requirement and the reality of who actually performs construction work in California is the underground economy: an enormous volume of construction activity performed by unlicensed individuals and businesses operating outside the regulatory system the CSLB was created to enforce. California's overall underground economy is estimated at $60 billion to $140 billion annually across all industries, and construction is among the sectors most heavily represented.

The underground construction economy is not a fringe phenomenon. The Economic Roundtable, a Los Angeles-based research organization, found in its landmark 2014 study that one in six construction jobs in California, approximately 143,900 workers, were informal or underground. Of those, 104,100 workers were unreported by their employers entirely and another 39,800 were misclassified as independent contractors to evade payroll taxes and insurance requirements. Underground construction employment had increased 400 percent since 1972. The informal wage gap, the difference between what these workers were paid and what they would have earned in formal employment, totaled $1.2 billion. The workers performing this labor are not invisible. They advertise on Craigslist, Nextdoor, and Facebook Marketplace. They solicit business at Home Depot parking lots. They bid on residential projects through word-of-mouth referral networks. They operate in plain sight because the enforcement apparatus is too small to reach them and because the demand for affordable construction labor in a state with $500-per-hour licensed contractors is bottomless.

## The Enforcement Math

The CSLB employs approximately 400 staff to regulate an industry of 231,000 active licensed contractors and an unknown but substantial number of unlicensed operators across a state of 164,000 square miles. The board receives approximately 20,000 complaints per year. In calendar year 2023, CSLB completed and closed 21,406 complaint investigations, which led to 3,833 legal actions and more than $40 million in restitution for consumers. The board's Statewide Investigative Fraud Team (SWIFT) conducts hundreds of undercover sting operations and sweeps per year: in the first seven months of 2024 alone, SWIFT conducted 217 sweeps across 43 counties and 174 cities, opening 969 complaints and issuing 704 administrative or criminal actions.

The math is still unfavorable. If the underground construction economy involves even 100,000 unlicensed operators (a conservative estimate given the 143,900 informal workers documented in 2014), the CSLB's enforcement apparatus contacts a single-digit percentage of them in any given year. The penalties for unlicensed contracting include administrative fines of up to $15,000 and, on criminal referral, misdemeanor charges carrying up to six months in county jail and a $5,000 fine. These penalties are rarely imposed at the maximum. District attorneys in most counties treat unlicensed contracting as a low-priority offense. A first-time citation typically results in a fine far below the statutory maximum, an amount that functions as a minor cost of doing business for an operator earning $50,000 to $100,000 per year in unreported income.

The enforcement challenge is compounded by the difficulty of distinguishing between an unlicensed contractor and a homeowner performing work on their own property, which is legal, or a handyman performing work below the $500 threshold, which is also legal. The gray zones are wide. A homeowner who hires a friend to remodel a bathroom and pays him $3,000 in cash has employed an unlicensed contractor. The transaction looks, from the outside, like a favor between neighbors. The CSLB cannot investigate what it cannot see, and most unlicensed construction activity occurs behind closed doors on private residential property.

## Who Gets Hurt

The consumer harm from unlicensed construction is real and unevenly distributed. Unlicensed contractors are not required to carry workers' compensation insurance, general liability insurance, or a surety bond. When an unlicensed contractor's work fails, the substandard electrical wiring that causes a fire, the improperly graded foundation that floods a basement, the unpermitted structural modification that compromises a load-bearing wall, the homeowner has no bond to claim against, no licensing board to complain to, and no insurance carrier to pursue. The only recourse is civil litigation against an individual who may have no assets, no registered business, and no permanent address.

The CSLB's Consumer Protection data indicates that complaints against unlicensed contractors involve higher average dollar losses than complaints against licensed contractors. The median consumer loss in an unlicensed contractor complaint is approximately $15,000, compared to approximately $8,000 for complaints against licensed contractors. The difference reflects both the absence of bond protection (the licensed contractor's $25,000 bond covers at least a portion of the loss) and the nature of the work: unlicensed contractors tend to take on projects they are not qualified to perform, producing failures that are more complete and more expensive to remediate.

The fiscal harm is also substantial. The Economic Roundtable documented a total informal tax gap of $774 million from underground construction: $301 million in unpaid federal taxes, $63 million in unpaid state unemployment insurance, $146 million in unpaid state disability insurance, and $264 million in unpaid workers' compensation premiums. The Joint Enforcement Strike Force, a multi-agency partnership including the CSLB, EDD, DIR, and Franchise Tax Board, has collected more than $37 million in restitution and fines over recent fiscal years, a fraction of the annual tax gap.

The harm extends beyond fiscal losses and individual consumers. Unlicensed construction produces unpermitted structures that do not appear in county assessor records, are not subject to building inspection, may not meet seismic or fire safety codes, and create title complications when the property is sold. The California Building Officials Association estimates that unpermitted construction reduces the assessed value of improvements statewide by billions of dollars, representing lost property tax revenue for local governments. An unpermitted second unit that would generate $5,000 per year in property tax if assessed generates zero revenue when it does not appear in the assessor's records.

## The Labor Market Underneath

The unlicensed construction workforce is disproportionately immigrant, disproportionately Latino, and disproportionately male. The UCLA Labor Center and the National Day Laborer Organizing Network have documented that day laborers, the most visible segment of the unlicensed construction workforce, earn a median of $12 to $15 per hour (below the state minimum wage), receive no overtime, no workers' compensation coverage, and no health insurance. An estimated 25 to 30 percent of day laborers report wage theft: employers who hire them for a day's work and pay less than promised or nothing at all. The workers have minimal legal recourse because reporting wage theft risks exposing their own unlicensed status and, for undocumented workers, their immigration status.

The licensed construction workforce is also affected. Licensed contractors who pay prevailing wages, carry insurance, and maintain bonds compete against unlicensed operators whose overhead is a fraction of theirs. A licensed general contractor's fully loaded cost to employ a journeyman carpenter is approximately $75 to $95 per hour, including wages, benefits, insurance, and overhead. An unlicensed operator paying a worker $20 per hour in cash with no insurance can underbid the licensed contractor by 50 percent or more. The price difference is visible to every homeowner who solicits bids for a kitchen remodel: the licensed contractor bids $80,000, the unlicensed operator bids $35,000. The homeowner who chooses the lower bid is not making an irrational decision. They are responding to a price signal that reflects the cost of compliance with the licensing system.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that the underground construction economy is the market's verdict on California's licensing system: the barrier to entry is too high, the cost of compliance is too great, and the enforcement is too weak to prevent circumvention. The $500 threshold, unchanged since 1962, criminalizes routine household repairs. The examination pass rate below 30 percent on first attempt excludes competent tradespeople who are poor test-takers or whose English proficiency is limited (the examinations are offered only in English and Spanish). The four-year experience requirement excludes workers with genuine skills acquired through informal apprenticeships. The system is designed around a model of construction labor that assumes formal employment, documented experience, and English-language test-taking ability, a model that does not describe the workforce that actually builds and maintains California's housing stock. The underground economy is the gap between the regulatory model and reality.

The generous read is that the underground economy exists precisely because it externalizes the costs that licensing internalizes. An unlicensed contractor's lower prices reflect the absence of insurance that would protect the homeowner if the project fails, the absence of workers' compensation that would protect the worker if they are injured, the absence of building code compliance that would protect future occupants, and the absence of tax reporting that would fund the public services the construction industry uses. The underground economy is not savings. It is transferred risk: from the contractor to the homeowner, from the employer to the worker, from the present to the future. The CSLB's licensing requirements, however burdensome, exist because the consequences of unregulated construction in a state with earthquakes, wildfires, and 40 million people are not theoretical. They are structural failures, electrical fires, and workers who fall off roofs with no insurance to pay for their treatment.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The CSLB publishes complaint data, enforcement action data, and individual license records. It does not publish an estimate of the total size of the unlicensed construction market, the number of unlicensed operators, or the geographic distribution of unlicensed activity. The most comprehensive study of the underground construction economy, the Economic Roundtable's "Sinking Underground" report, used 2011 data published in 2014. No comparable study has been conducted since. The construction industry has grown substantially in the intervening decade, and the absolute scale of the underground economy has almost certainly grown with it, but the current figures are extrapolations from aging data, not direct measurements. The board enforces against unlicensed activity it encounters but does not systematically measure the market it is unable to reach.

The data also does not capture outcomes: how often does licensed construction outperform unlicensed construction in terms of code compliance, safety, and durability? The intuitive answer is "almost always," but the empirical answer is unavailable because unpermitted construction is not inspected, not tracked, and not measured. The CSLB can document the cases where unlicensed work caused harm. It cannot document the cases where unlicensed work was performed competently, because competent unlicensed work, by definition, generates no complaint.
