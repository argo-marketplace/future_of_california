# California Counts: The Quarter Who Showed Up
## What a 23 percent primary turnout, the lowest gubernatorial primary participation in modern California history, a 15-point decline from 2018, and 18 million registered voters who stayed home reveal about the election that chose the next governor's finalists while the state looked the other way

On June 2, 2026, approximately 5.3 million Californians cast ballots in the state's gubernatorial primary election, representing approximately 23 percent of the state's 23.1 million registered voters. Democrat Xavier Becerra and Republican Steve Hilton advanced to the November general election under California's top-two primary system. Becerra led with approximately 28 percent of the vote and Hilton followed at approximately 25 percent. The remaining votes scattered across a field of additional candidates.

The 23 percent turnout is the lowest for a gubernatorial primary in modern California history. It continues a decades-long decline in primary participation that has accelerated since the adoption of the top-two primary system in 2012. Approximately 18 million registered voters, more than three-quarters of the electorate, did not participate in the election that determined which two candidates will compete for the governorship of a state with 40 million residents and a $4 trillion economy. The two finalists for governor were selected by fewer than 3 million voters combined.

## The Trend

California gubernatorial primary turnout has declined in six of the last eight cycles. The pattern is unmistakable.

| Year | Registered Voters | Turnout (%) | Context |
|------|------------------|-------------|---------|
| 1994 | 14.7 million | 45% | Wilson vs. Brown (contested both parties) |
| 1998 | 15.0 million | 41% | Davis vs. Lungren (contested both parties) |
| 2002 | 15.3 million | 36% | Davis vs. Simon (March primary) |
| 2006 | 15.7 million | 34% | Schwarzenegger (incumbent, low interest) |
| 2010 | 17.3 million | 33% | Brown vs. Whitman (contested both parties) |
| 2014 | 17.7 million | 25% | Brown (incumbent, first top-two) |
| 2018 | 19.4 million | 38% | Open seat, Newsom vs. Cox (top-two) |
| 2022 | 22.0 million | 33% | Newsom (incumbent, low interest) |
| 2026 | 23.1 million | 23% | Open seat, Becerra vs. Hilton (top-two) |

The 2018 primary, the last open-seat gubernatorial primary, drew 38 percent of registered voters. The 2026 primary, also an open seat, drew 23 percent, a decline of 15 percentage points. The difference is striking because open-seat primaries historically generate higher turnout than incumbent primaries (voters perceive the stakes as higher when the outcome is uncertain). The 2026 primary broke that pattern: turnout fell below even the incumbent-year primaries of 2006, 2014, and 2022.

The raw numbers tell the same story differently. California has added approximately 8.4 million registered voters since 1994 (from 14.7 million to 23.1 million), driven by population growth, motor voter registration, and automatic voter registration. Over the same period, the number of ballots cast in the gubernatorial primary has declined from approximately 6.6 million (1994) to approximately 5.3 million (2026). The state has more registered voters than ever and fewer of them vote in the primary that determines their choices.

## The Structural Explanations

Several structural factors contribute to the decline. The top-two primary system, adopted through Proposition 14 in 2010 and first used in 2012, eliminated party primaries and placed all candidates on a single ballot, with the top two vote-getters advancing regardless of party. The reform was designed to increase participation by allowing all registered voters (including the approximately 5.3 million registered with no party preference) to vote in every primary. It has not produced that effect. Turnout in the two gubernatorial primaries under the old system (2006 and 2010) averaged 33.5 percent. Turnout in the four gubernatorial primaries under the top-two system (2014, 2018, 2022, 2026) has averaged 30 percent, with 2026 pulling the average sharply downward.

The Voter's Choice Act (2016) expanded vote-by-mail and established vote centers in participating counties, replacing traditional polling places. By 2026, approximately 85 percent of California voters received mail ballots automatically. The shift to mail voting has been associated with higher turnout in general elections but has not arrested the decline in primary elections, suggesting that the barrier to primary participation is not access but motivation.

The June primary date itself may contribute. California moved its primary from March to June for non-presidential years, placing it deep in the calendar when media attention and voter engagement are typically lower. The June 2026 primary competed with the end of the school year, the beginning of summer, and the general fatigue of a post-presidential-election cycle.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that 23 percent turnout in a gubernatorial primary is a democratic legitimacy crisis. The two candidates who will compete for the governorship of the most populous state in the nation were selected by approximately 1.5 million and 1.3 million voters, respectively, out of 23.1 million registered and approximately 27 million eligible. The electorate that voted in the 2026 primary is older, whiter, wealthier, and more partisan than the California population, meaning that the candidates were selected by a subset that does not represent the state. The top-two primary was supposed to broaden participation. Instead, it has produced the lowest turnout in modern history, and the finalists were chosen by approximately 6 percent each of the registered electorate. A system in which 6 percent of registered voters can determine a general election matchup is a system that has decoupled electoral outcomes from popular engagement.

The generous read is that low primary turnout reflects voter confidence, not voter alienation. Primaries are low-salience elections by design: they are the process by which party coalitions (or, under the top-two system, ideological lanes) sort their candidates, not the process by which the public makes its governing choice. That choice comes in November, when turnout typically doubles or triples. The 2026 primary produced a clear ideological contrast (Becerra, a progressive Democrat and former attorney general; Hilton, a populist Republican and former Fox News host) that gives voters a meaningful choice in November. The system worked: it sorted the field and produced a competitive general election. Whether 23 percent or 53 percent participated in the sorting is less important than whether the November electorate is satisfied with the options.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Secretary of State publishes turnout data by county and statewide. Ballots are still being counted as of this writing (mail ballots received by election day and provisional ballots are processed for up to 30 days after the election), and the final certified turnout may differ by one to three percentage points from the current estimate. The data does not isolate the reasons voters did not participate: whether nonvoters were unaware of the election, aware but uninterested, aware but unable (work, transportation, health), or aware but strategically abstaining. The difference between apathy and alienation is the difference between a healthy democracy with a low-engagement sorting process and a failing democracy with a legitimacy deficit, and the turnout number alone cannot distinguish between them.
