# California Counts: The Hunger Map
## What 4.5 million CalFresh recipients, a 23 percent food insecurity rate, 7,300 food banks and pantries, and a $230 monthly benefit that cannot buy a healthy diet reveal about the hunger California hides behind the richest agricultural state in the nation

California produces more food than any other state: $55 billion in farm gate value, 400-plus commodities, the fruits and vegetables that stock grocery stores across the nation. It is also one of the hungriest. Approximately 9 million Californians, 23 percent of the population, experience food insecurity, defined as limited or uncertain access to adequate food due to lack of money or other resources. The rate is higher for children (approximately 28 percent), for Latino households (approximately 30 percent), and for households in the Central Valley, where the food is grown by workers who cannot afford to buy it.

CalFresh, California's implementation of the federal Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), serves approximately 4.5 million Californians in approximately 1.9 million households. The average monthly benefit is approximately $230 per person, an amount set by federal formulas and intended to supplement household food spending, not to cover total food costs. Nutrition researchers at the UCLA Center for Health Policy Research and the USDA's Thrifty Food Plan estimates have found that $230 per month is insufficient to purchase a nutritionally adequate diet at California grocery prices, which are approximately 15 to 20 percent above the national average.

## The Emergency Food Network

California's emergency food network, the food banks, food pantries, and meal programs that distribute free food to anyone who shows up, serves approximately 5 million people per month through approximately 7,300 distribution sites statewide. The network is coordinated through the California Association of Food Banks and anchored by six large regional food banks: Los Angeles Regional Food Bank (the largest), SF-Marin Food Bank, Second Harvest of Silicon Valley, Feeding San Diego, Sacramento Food Bank, and Community Food Bank of San Joaquin. The food banks distribute approximately 1.5 billion pounds of food per year, sourced from federal commodity programs (USDA commodities), agricultural surplus donations, grocery store donations (near-expiration products), and purchased food funded by charitable donations and grants.

The demand surged during the COVID-19 pandemic (distribution increased approximately 60 percent in 2020-2021) and has not returned to pre-pandemic levels. Food bank operators report that demand remains approximately 30 percent above pre-pandemic levels as of 2026, driven by inflation in food and housing costs that has reduced the purchasing power of low-income households even as employment has recovered.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that food insecurity in the richest agricultural state in the nation is a policy choice, not a scarcity problem. California produces enough food to feed its 40 million residents many times over. The food insecurity rate reflects the distribution of income, not the distribution of food: the state's cost of living, particularly housing, consumes so much of low-income household budgets that food is the expendable category, the expense that can be reduced, skipped, or replaced with cheaper, less nutritious alternatives when the rent is due. The CalFresh benefit of $230 per month, set by federal formula with no California cost-of-living adjustment, is inadequate for a state where a gallon of milk costs $5 and a head of lettuce costs $3. The 7,300 emergency food distribution sites are evidence of system failure, not system success: a functioning food security system would not require an emergency network operating at permanent scale.

The generous read is that California has built the most extensive food assistance infrastructure of any state: CalFresh serves 4.5 million people, school meal programs feed 3.4 million children per day (California was the first state to provide universal free school meals regardless of income, under SB 364 in 2021), CalFresh Healthy Living promotes nutrition education, and the food bank network distributes 1.5 billion pounds of food per year. The universal free school meals program alone ensures that every child in a California public school receives breakfast and lunch at no cost, eliminating the stigma and administrative burden of means-tested school meals. The food insecurity rate, while high, would be dramatically higher without these programs: CalFresh alone lifts an estimated 1 million Californians above the food insecurity threshold.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

USDA publishes food insecurity rates from the Current Population Survey, measured at the household level. The data does not capture the coping mechanisms that food-insecure households use: the meals skipped, the nutritional quality sacrificed, the choice between paying the electricity bill and buying groceries. The food insecurity measure is binary (secure or insecure) and does not capture the severity, duration, or health consequences of inadequate food access.
