# California Counts: Frozen in 1978
## What a 1 percent cap, a 10-to-1 assessment gap, $80 billion in annual revenue, and the Prop 15 defeat reveal about the tax revolt that rewrote California's fiscal DNA

Proposition 13, approved by 65 percent of voters on June 6, 1978, did four things: it capped the property tax rate at 1 percent of assessed value, limited annual assessment increases to 2 percent until a property is sold (at which point it is reassessed at market value), required a two-thirds vote of the legislature for state tax increases, and required a two-thirds vote of local voters for special local taxes. Forty-eight years later, the measure's provisions remain essentially unchanged in the California Constitution and constitute the single most consequential fiscal policy in the state's history.

Property tax revenue in California is approximately $80 billion per year, collected by county assessors and distributed to local governments, schools, and special districts. That $80 billion sounds substantial. It is approximately 0.72 percent of the total assessed value of California real property, well below the national average effective property tax rate of approximately 1.1 percent. If California's property tax system assessed all property at market value and applied the national average rate, revenue would be approximately $150 billion per year, roughly $70 billion more than the state currently collects. That $70 billion is the annual fiscal cost of Proposition 13, a revenue gap larger than the entire general fund budget of every state except New York and Texas.

## The Assessment Gap

The 2 percent annual assessment cap creates a growing gap between a property's assessed value and its market value. A home purchased in 1978 for $100,000 has a current assessed value of approximately $260,000 (2 percent per year, compounded for 48 years). If that home's market value is $1.5 million, the assessment gap is $1.24 million, and the homeowner pays approximately $2,600 per year in property tax instead of the $15,000 they would pay at market value. Their neighbor who purchased an identical home in 2024 for $1.5 million pays $15,000.

The disparities are extreme in high-appreciation markets. In San Francisco, the ratio of assessed value to market value for the lowest-assessed 10 percent of properties is approximately 10 to 1. In Beverly Hills, Santa Monica, and Palo Alto, similar ratios exist. The assessment gap is a wealth transfer from recent buyers (who pay property tax at market rates) to long-tenured owners (who pay at historical rates), and from renters (whose landlords' property tax savings are not passed through to rent) to homeowners.

The commercial property loophole compounds the disparity. Residential property is reassessed when it is sold, but commercial and industrial property can avoid reassessment through ownership structure changes (transferring interests in a legal entity that owns the property rather than transferring the property itself). A 2020 analysis by the Los Angeles County Assessor estimated that commercial properties in the county were assessed at an average of 50 to 60 percent of market value due to these structures, resulting in approximately $2 billion per year in foregone revenue for Los Angeles County alone.

Proposition 15 (2020), the "split roll" initiative, proposed reassessing commercial and industrial property (but not residential) at market value, generating an estimated $6.5 to $11.5 billion per year for schools and local governments. It lost 52 to 48 percent, defeated by a campaign that spent $80 million arguing it would raise costs for small businesses and renters. The defeat was narrow but definitive: the most politically achievable reform of Proposition 13 failed in a year when Democrats held supermajorities in both chambers and a pandemic had highlighted the state's fiscal fragility.

## The School Funding Legacy

Before Proposition 13, California's per-pupil K-12 spending ranked in the top 10 nationally. The property tax revenue that funded schools varied by district wealth, creating enormous disparities (the Serrano v. Priest decisions of 1971 and 1976 had begun to address these), but total spending was among the highest in the nation. Proposition 13 eliminated local control over property tax rates, shifting school funding to the state and creating the dependency on the general fund that persists today.

By the 1990s, California's per-pupil spending had fallen to the bottom third nationally. The recovery has been slow and only recently significant: the LCFF era has brought spending above the national average for the first time in decades. But the structural damage remains: California's schools are funded primarily through the state general fund (and therefore through volatile income tax revenue) rather than through the stable, locally controlled property tax revenue that funds schools in most other states. When the stock market falls and income tax revenue declines, school funding falls with it, creating the boom-bust cycle in education spending that districts have experienced repeatedly.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that Proposition 13 is the original sin of California's fiscal dysfunction. It destroyed the local property tax base that funded schools, police, fire, and parks. It created a two-tier property tax system that rewards longevity and penalizes mobility. It enabled commercial property owners to avoid reassessment through legal fictions. It forced the state to backfill local government funding through the general fund, making every local service dependent on the volatile income tax. And it required a two-thirds supermajority for tax increases, making fiscal reform nearly impossible through the normal legislative process. Every California fiscal problem, the budget volatility, the school funding instability, the local government revenue constraints, the special district proliferation, traces back to June 6, 1978.

The generous read is that Proposition 13 prevented the displacement of elderly and fixed-income homeowners who were being taxed out of their homes by soaring property values, a genuine injustice that the pre-1978 system inflicted. The measure provided certainty: a homeowner knows that their property tax will increase by no more than 2 percent per year regardless of market conditions. The $80 billion in annual property tax revenue, while below what a market-value system would generate, is not nothing. And the political reality is that Proposition 13 has survived every attempt at reform for 48 years because a majority of Californians, including renters who benefit only indirectly, consistently vote to maintain it. The measure is democratic. It was approved by 65 percent of voters. Prop 15's defeat confirmed that the public is not ready to reform even the commercial side. Proposition 13 may be bad fiscal policy. It is popular fiscal policy. And in a democracy, popular wins.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

County assessor data tracks assessed values but does not publicly report the market-value gap at the parcel level in a statewide database. Researchers must estimate the gap using sales data, comparable property analysis, and statistical modeling. The total assessment gap, the difference between aggregate assessed value and aggregate market value across all California property, is estimated at $3 trillion to $5 trillion, representing $30 billion to $50 billion in foregone annual property tax revenue. The precision of this estimate is limited by the opacity of commercial property ownership structures and the difficulty of assessing market value for properties that have not transacted in decades.
