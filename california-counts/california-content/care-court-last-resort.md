# California Counts: The Court of Last Resort
## What CARE Court's 12-month treatment mandate, 12,000 estimated eligible individuals, a conservatorship system that serves 1 percent of those who need it, and a behavioral health workforce insufficient to treat the people the court orders treated reveal about the mental health intervention California created when the voluntary system failed the most visible and the most ill

California's Community Assistance, Recovery, and Empowerment (CARE) Act, signed by Governor Newsom in September 2022 and implemented in phases beginning October 2023, creates a new civil court process for individuals with schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders who are unable or unwilling to engage in voluntary treatment. Under the CARE Act, a family member, first responder, behavioral health professional, or other qualified petitioner can ask the court to order an evaluation. If the court finds the individual eligible, it can order a one-year CARE plan that includes clinically appropriate behavioral health treatment, medication, housing, and other support services. If the individual does not comply with the CARE plan, the court can extend the plan for an additional year or refer the individual for conservatorship proceedings.

The Department of Health Care Services estimates that approximately 7,000 to 12,000 Californians meet the CARE Act's eligibility criteria: adults with schizophrenia spectrum or other psychotic disorders who are currently unhoused or at risk of homelessness, recently incarcerated, or not engaged in treatment. The population is the most visible manifestation of California's mental health crisis: people experiencing psychosis on streets and sidewalks, in encampments, in jail booking rooms, and in emergency departments, cycling through systems that provide episodic crisis response but not sustained treatment.

## The Capacity Question

CARE Court mandates treatment. It does not create treatment capacity. California's behavioral health workforce is approximately 25 to 30 percent short of the staffing needed to serve its current patient population, before adding the 7,000 to 12,000 individuals CARE Court is designed to reach. The state has approximately 5,000 psychiatrists (approximately 12 per 100,000 population, below the recommended ratio of 15 per 100,000), and the geographic distribution is heavily skewed toward urban and affluent areas. Rural counties may have one or two psychiatrists serving the entire county.

Housing is the other critical gap. The CARE Act requires that counties provide housing to individuals under CARE plans, but does not create housing. The state's supportive housing pipeline (approximately 5,000 to 8,000 new units per year through various programs) serves a population far larger than CARE Court's target, and existing demand exceeds supply by tens of thousands of units. In the early months of implementation, counties reported that identifying housing placements for CARE respondents is the primary bottleneck: the court can order treatment and housing, but the county cannot provide what does not exist.

---

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that CARE Court is a legal framework built on an operational fiction: the assumption that court-ordered treatment will produce treatment in a system that cannot provide voluntary treatment to the people who already want it. The 7,000 to 12,000 eligible individuals need psychiatrists (shortage), medication management (shortage), residential treatment beds (shortage), and permanent supportive housing (severe shortage). The court process adds legal infrastructure (judges, public defenders, court investigators, case managers) to a system whose failure is not legal but material. CARE Court does not add a single psychiatrist, a single treatment bed, or a single apartment to the supply. It adds a mechanism for ordering services that the system cannot deliver. The risk is that CARE Court becomes a pipeline to conservatorship and locked facilities for people whose primary need is housing and consistent care.

The generous read is that CARE Court fills a genuine gap in California's mental health system: the space between voluntary treatment (which the most severely ill often cannot engage in due to the nature of psychosis) and conservatorship (which is so restrictive and adversarial that it is used for only a fraction of those who need intensive intervention). The CARE plan is less restrictive than conservatorship, provides housing and services rather than locked confinement, and includes procedural protections (counsel for the respondent, judicial review, time limits). The capacity problem is real but addressable: the state has committed approximately $4.7 billion in behavioral health infrastructure (through the BHSA and bond measures) that is building treatment capacity. CARE Court creates the legal framework. The infrastructure investments are building the operational capacity. Both are necessary and neither alone is sufficient.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Judicial Council is collecting data on CARE Court petitions, hearings, and outcomes. Early data (covering the first year of phased rollout) shows modest petition volumes, reflecting both the newness of the process and the capacity constraints that limit the system's throughput. Whether CARE Court improves long-term outcomes (housing stability, treatment engagement, reduced hospitalization, reduced incarceration) for its target population will require years of data that does not yet exist.
