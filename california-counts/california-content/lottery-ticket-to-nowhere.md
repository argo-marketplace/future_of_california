# California Counts: The Lottery Ticket to Nowhere
## What $9.3 billion in annual sales, $2.1 billion for schools, and a 40-year substitution effect reveal about the most popular fiscal illusion in California

The California Lottery was approved by voters in 1984 with a promise that now appears on every ticket, every billboard, and every scratched-off hope: the proceeds benefit California's public schools. This is true. It is also one of the most successful misdirections in the history of public finance. In fiscal year 2024-25, the California Lottery generated approximately $9.3 billion in total sales and transferred approximately $2.1 billion to public education. That $2.1 billion represents roughly 1.5 percent of the state's total K-14 education budget of approximately $135 billion. It is not additional money. It is fungible money. The legislature, which controls the state's general fund, has for four decades treated lottery revenue as a partial substitute for general fund education spending rather than a supplement to it. Every dollar the lottery sends to schools is roughly a dollar the general fund does not have to send. The schools get the same amount they would have received anyway. The lottery funds simply change the color of the money, not the size of the check.

---

## The Promise: Proposition 37

The 1984 ballot measure that created the California Lottery, Proposition 37, was sold on a simple premise: gambling revenue would provide additional funding for education. The measure required that at least 34 percent of lottery revenues be allocated to public education, defined as K-12 schools, community colleges, the California State University, and the University of California. The word "additional" was not merely implied. It was explicit in the campaign. Pro-lottery advertisements featured schoolchildren, teachers, and classrooms. The voter pamphlet arguments promised that lottery funds would supplement, not replace, existing school funding.

The measure passed with 58 percent of the vote. Within five years, the substitution effect was documented. The Legislative Analyst's Office noted as early as 1989 that lottery revenues were being incorporated into baseline education funding calculations, effectively allowing the legislature to reduce general fund allocations by the amount the lottery provided. The schools received their Proposition 98 minimum guarantee (established by voters in 1988 to guarantee a minimum share of the general fund for education), and the lottery revenue counted toward satisfying that guarantee. The net effect: the lottery funded education as promised. Education funding did not increase as a result.

This is not a bug. It is the predictable consequence of fungible revenue in a system with a guaranteed minimum. When Proposition 98 requires the state to spend a minimum of roughly 40 percent of the general fund on K-14 education, and lottery revenue counts toward that minimum, every lottery dollar frees a general fund dollar for other purposes. The schools hit the same floor. The general fund gets more headroom. The lottery effectively funds whatever the freed-up general fund dollars are spent on: prisons, healthcare, debt service, tax relief. The schools are the pass-through. The real beneficiary is the rest of the budget.

## Who Plays: The Demographics of Hope

The California Lottery does not publish granular demographic data on its players. But national and state-level studies, including a 2022 analysis by the Howard Center for Investigative Journalism and a longitudinal study by the National Bureau of Economic Research, paint a consistent picture: lottery participation is inversely correlated with income. Lower-income households spend a higher percentage of their income on lottery tickets than higher-income households. The spending is concentrated in communities of color, immigrant communities, and areas with lower educational attainment.

The California Lottery's own retailer data reveals the geographic pattern. Of the state's approximately 23,000 licensed lottery retailers, the highest-volume locations are concentrated in low-income neighborhoods. Convenience stores, liquor stores, and check-cashing outlets in South Los Angeles, the Inland Empire, the Central Valley, and the older suburbs of the Bay Area sell disproportionately more lottery products per capita than retailers in affluent areas. This is not because wealthy Californians do not play the lottery. It is because the lottery is a fixed-dollar wager, and the people who wager it most frequently are those for whom the dream of a transformative payout is most compelling, which is to say, those for whom the present is most constrained.

The average California household that plays the lottery spends approximately $300 to $400 per year on tickets. But this average is skewed by the large number of occasional players. Frequent players, defined as those who purchase tickets weekly or more often, spend $1,000 to $3,000 per year, with a small percentage of heavy players spending $5,000 or more. The lottery is a regressive revenue source: it collects a higher effective rate from lower-income Californians than from higher-income ones. If it were structured as a tax, it would be the most regressive tax in the state's revenue system.

## The Product Mix: Scratchers and the Instant Gratification Machine

The California Lottery offers two primary product categories: draw games (Powerball, Mega Millions, SuperLotto Plus, Fantasy 5, and Daily games) and Scratchers, the instant-win tickets that are scratched to reveal prizes. Scratchers dominate. In fiscal year 2024-25, Scratchers accounted for approximately 73 percent of total lottery sales, or roughly $6.8 billion. Draw games accounted for the remaining 27 percent.

The shift toward Scratchers has been deliberate and accelerating. Scratchers offer instant results, immediate dopamine response, and a wide range of price points from $1 to $30 per ticket. The Lottery Commission has progressively expanded the Scratchers product line, introducing higher price points, licensed brand partnerships, and themed games designed to attract new demographics. The $30 Scratcher, once controversial, is now a standard product. The average payout rate on Scratchers is approximately 64 percent, meaning for every dollar spent, the player receives on average 64 cents back in prizes. The remaining 36 cents is split between education (approximately 26 cents) and operating costs (approximately 10 cents, including retailer commissions and administration).

Draw games have a lower payout rate, approximately 50 percent, but generate larger individual prizes and more media attention. The Powerball and Mega Millions jackpots regularly exceed $500 million and occasionally exceed $1 billion, generating surges in ticket sales that can double weekly revenue. These jackpot-driven spikes are temporary but lucrative: a $1 billion Powerball jackpot can generate $100 million or more in California sales for a single drawing.

The lottery's product evolution mirrors the broader gambling industry's discovery that frequency and immediacy of reward drive engagement more effectively than prize size. The $30 Scratcher that offers a top prize of $10 million and an average play time of 30 seconds is a more effective revenue generator than a $2 Powerball ticket for a $500 million jackpot drawn twice a week. The Lottery Commission understands this. Its annual business plans explicitly discuss player engagement, new game development, and strategies for increasing per-player spending. The commission is, by charter, a marketing organization whose product is hope sold at a significant markup.

## The Money Flow: Following $9.3 Billion

Of the lottery's $9.3 billion in fiscal year 2024-25 sales, the money flows through a specific pipeline. Approximately 64 percent ($5.95 billion) is returned to players as prizes. Approximately 7 percent ($650 million) goes to retailer commissions. Approximately 6 percent ($560 million) covers lottery operating expenses, including marketing, staff, technology systems, and administration. The remaining approximately 23 percent ($2.1 billion) is transferred to the Education Fund and distributed to K-14 public education.

The education distribution is allocated on a per-pupil and per-FTE basis. K-12 schools receive the largest share, distributed through the State Controller's Office to school districts based on average daily attendance. Community colleges receive a share based on full-time equivalent students. The UC and CSU systems receive smaller allocations. For a school district, lottery funds represent a modest supplement: the average K-12 district receives approximately $250 to $300 per student per year from the lottery, on a total per-pupil expenditure of approximately $18,000 to $24,000 depending on the district.

The $2.1 billion annual transfer to education is real and significant in absolute terms. But relative to the $135 billion total K-14 education budget, it is a rounding error. If the California Lottery ceased to exist tomorrow and its $2.1 billion disappeared, the Proposition 98 guarantee would require the general fund to backfill the loss, and the education budget would remain essentially unchanged. The lottery does not fund education. It subsidizes the general fund by providing an alternative revenue source that satisfies part of the constitutional education spending minimum.

## The Advertising Paradox

The California Lottery spent approximately $230 million on marketing and advertising in fiscal year 2024-25, making it one of the largest advertisers in the state. Lottery ads appear on television, radio, billboards, social media, bus shelters, and in-store displays. The advertising targets adults 18 and older and is required by statute to include the phrase "our schools win too" or equivalent messaging emphasizing the education benefit.

The paradox is structural. The lottery is a government entity whose mission includes maximizing revenue for education. Maximizing revenue means maximizing sales. Maximizing sales means marketing a product that is, by mathematical certainty, a losing proposition for the average buyer. The expected value of a $1 lottery ticket is approximately $0.50 to $0.64 depending on the game. The lottery must market this product aggressively to people who, on average, can least afford to lose the difference, because those are its best customers.

California law prohibits the lottery from advertising that is "aimed at target audiences that include a substantial proportion of minors" and requires that advertising not be "misleading." The law does not restrict advertising that targets low-income adults, does not cap the marketing budget, and does not require disclosure of the odds in advertising (though odds are printed on tickets). The Lottery Commission has rejected proposals to restrict advertising in low-income neighborhoods, arguing that such restrictions would reduce revenue and thereby reduce education funding, the circular logic that defines the institution.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that the California Lottery is a regressive tax disguised as entertainment, marketed by the state to its poorest residents, and justified by an education funding promise that is structurally hollow. The $2.1 billion the lottery sends to schools replaces $2.1 billion the general fund would otherwise provide. The net effect on education is zero. The net effect on low-income Californians who spend $300 to $3,000 per year on tickets is a wealth transfer from the poor to the state budget, laundered through the education system for political respectability. The lottery spends $230 million per year marketing a mathematically losing product to a customer base that skews low-income, non-white, and less educated. It is, by every empirical measure, the most regressive revenue instrument the state operates. And it is the only one with a marketing department.

The generous read is that the California Lottery is a voluntary revenue source that generates $2.1 billion per year for public education without imposing a mandatory tax on anyone. Nobody is required to buy a ticket. The product provides entertainment value that many players consider worth the cost, much as a movie ticket or a sporting event provides entertainment at a negative financial expected value. The lottery employs approximately 1,100 people, supports 23,000 retail partners, and has contributed more than $45 billion to education since its inception. The substitution effect is real but is a feature of the Proposition 98 guarantee, not of the lottery itself. If voters wanted lottery funds to be truly supplemental, they could amend the constitution to exclude lottery revenue from the Proposition 98 calculation. They have not, which suggests the current arrangement serves the public's revealed preferences: the satisfaction of knowing lottery proceeds go to schools, without the political cost of determining what "additional" means.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Lottery publishes sales data, prize data, retailer commissions, and education contributions in its annual report. It does not publish player demographics. It does not publish zip-code-level sales data in a format that allows researchers to correlate spending with income levels, though retailer-level data can be approximated through public records requests. The demographic picture is assembled from national surveys and academic research, not from the lottery's own systems. The lottery knows who its retailers are and where they are. Whether it knows who its heaviest players are and chooses not to disclose that information, or genuinely does not track it, is unclear.

The data does not capture problem gambling. The California Council on Problem Gambling estimates that 3 to 5 percent of California adults exhibit some level of disordered gambling behavior. The lottery's contribution to problem gambling, as distinct from casino gambling, sports betting, and card rooms, is difficult to isolate. But the lottery is the most accessible form of gambling in the state: available at 23,000 retail locations, requiring no account, no age verification beyond point of sale, and no minimum wager. The $1 Scratcher is the gateway gambling product. Whether it leads to escalation for vulnerable individuals is studied but not measured by the institution that sells it.

The data also does not capture what economists call the "utility of hope." For a person earning $30,000 a year in a state where a median home costs $750,000, a $2 lottery ticket purchases something that no other product in the economy offers: a nonzero probability, however small, of a fundamentally different life. The expected financial value is negative. The expected psychological value may be positive. The lottery's economists and the lottery's critics are measuring different things, and the data supports both of their conclusions simultaneously.

---

Forty years after voters approved it, the California Lottery is a $9.3 billion enterprise that delivers $2.1 billion to schools that would receive the same funding without it, markets hope to people who can least afford to buy it, and justifies its existence with a promise that is technically true and functionally meaningless. It is California's most popular fiscal illusion: a voluntary tax that feels like entertainment, an education funding source that funds nothing additional, and a government-run business that spends $230 million per year persuading its own citizens to participate in a transaction where the house always wins. The schools get their 1.5 percent. The general fund gets its headroom. The players get their 30 seconds of possibility. And the lottery, alone among California's institutions, has found the one business model that never loses: selling the dream that the next ticket is the one.
