# California Counts: The Other Prop 13
## What a 48-52 defeat for Proposition 15, $12 billion in unrealized commercial property tax revenue, 40-year-old commercial assessments, and the split roll that would have taxed Disney's land at market value while leaving homeowners untouched reveal about the property tax reform California almost enacted and will likely revisit

Proposition 15, the California Schools and Local Communities Funding Act, appeared on the November 2020 ballot and was defeated 48 percent to 52 percent. The measure would have created a "split roll" property tax system: commercial and industrial properties (excluding those owned by businesses with $3 million or less in California property) would have been reassessed at current market value, while residential properties would have remained under Proposition 13's acquisition-value assessment system. The Legislative Analyst's Office estimated that the split roll would generate $8 billion to $12 billion per year in additional property tax revenue for local governments and schools.

Proposition 13 (1978) assesses all property, residential and commercial, at its purchase price, with annual assessment increases capped at 2 percent regardless of changes in market value. A home or commercial property purchased in 1978 for $100,000 is assessed at approximately $250,000 today (reflecting 2 percent annual increases over 46 years), even if its market value is $2 million. The gap between assessed value and market value grows with each year of ownership. For commercial properties held for decades by corporations (Disneyland's land, purchased by Disney in the 1950s; Chevron's refineries, acquired decades ago; downtown Los Angeles office towers held by pension funds and REITs), the gap can be enormous: assessed values at 10 to 20 percent of market value, with correspondingly low property tax payments.

---

## The Revenue Gap

The gap between what commercial properties pay under Proposition 13 and what they would pay at market value has been estimated by the USC Program for Environmental and Regional Equity at approximately $12 billion per year. The revenue would flow to local governments (counties, cities, special districts) and schools through the existing property tax allocation formula. The $12 billion represents approximately a 20 percent increase in total property tax revenue statewide and would disproportionately benefit counties with high concentrations of long-held commercial property (Los Angeles, San Francisco, Orange, Santa Clara).

The distributional impact is progressive by geography and income: the additional revenue would fund schools, fire protection, libraries, parks, and local services in communities whose property tax base has been eroded by Proposition 13's assessment cap. However, the incidence of the tax (who ultimately pays) is disputed: property tax increases on commercial landlords may be passed through to commercial tenants (businesses) in the form of higher rents, which may be passed through to consumers in the form of higher prices. The economic literature on property tax incidence suggests that approximately 50 to 75 percent of commercial property tax increases are borne by property owners (reduced property values and investment returns), with 25 to 50 percent passed through to tenants and consumers.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that Proposition 13's application to commercial property is the largest ongoing tax expenditure in California: $12 billion per year in foregone revenue, subsidizing corporations that hold property for decades while schools, fire departments, and local governments operate with constrained budgets. Unlike homeowners (who have an emotional and practical attachment to assessment stability), commercial property owners hold real estate as investment assets. Assessment at market value is the norm for commercial property in 49 other states and produces no observable deterrent to commercial investment. California's commercial property tax subsidy is a windfall for long-term corporate property holders, financed by reduced local services for everyone.

The generous read is that Proposition 13's uniform treatment of residential and commercial property is the political foundation of the law's durability. The homeowner coalition that defends Proposition 13 includes both residential owners (who benefit directly) and business groups (who benefit from commercial assessment caps). The split roll would fracture that coalition, and once commercial property is reassessed, the political pressure to reassess residential property would increase. Proposition 15's defeat (despite strong support from education advocates, labor unions, and progressive organizations, and despite a massive spending advantage for the "yes" campaign) suggests that a majority of voters prefer the current system, either because they accept the argument that commercial tax increases will be passed through to consumers or because they see the split roll as the first step toward dismantling Proposition 13's protections for homeowners.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

County assessors publish assessed values for individual properties, but no state agency publishes a statewide analysis comparing assessed values to market values for commercial properties, the aggregate revenue gap by county and property type, or the distribution of the gap by property owner (corporate, individual, public). The $12 billion estimate is produced by academic researchers and advocacy organizations from sampled data, not from a comprehensive state assessment.
