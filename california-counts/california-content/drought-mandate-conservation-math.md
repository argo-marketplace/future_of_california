# California Counts: The Conservation Math
## What a 55-gallon per person per day indoor standard, a 20 percent urban reduction target, the 2012-2016 drought that cut use by 25 percent, and a "conservation as a way of life" mandate that makes permanent what used to be emergency reveal about the water rationing California legislated into the baseline

AB 1668 and SB 606 (2018) established California's "conservation as a way of life" framework, requiring urban water suppliers to meet individualized water budgets based on science-based efficiency standards. The indoor residential standard is 55 gallons per person per day (declining to 47 gallons by 2025 and stabilizing at 42 gallons), approximately half the pre-drought average of 100 to 120 gallons per person per day. Outdoor water budgets are calculated based on irrigable landscape area, local climate, and plant factors. Commercial, industrial, and institutional budgets are based on performance standards specific to each sector.

The 2012-2016 drought, the most severe in California's recorded history (based on precipitation, snowpack, reservoir storage, and groundwater levels), forced Governor Brown to impose mandatory conservation targets: a statewide 25 percent reduction from 2013 baseline levels. Urban water agencies achieved approximately 24 percent reduction, with some communities exceeding 30 percent. The conservation came from shorter showers, reduced landscape irrigation, replacement of turf with drought-tolerant plants, leak repair, and behavioral change. When the drought ended (heavy rains in 2016-2017), water use rebounded by approximately 10 to 15 percent but did not return to pre-drought levels. The "new normal" of water use is approximately 15 to 20 percent below the pre-drought baseline, reflecting both behavioral change and the permanent installations (low-flow fixtures, efficient appliances, turf removal) that the drought prompted.

## The Structural Shift

The 2018 legislation made the drought-era emergency conservation into a permanent structural requirement. Urban water suppliers must now calculate their total water budget (the sum of indoor, outdoor, commercial, and system-loss budgets for their entire service area) and demonstrate that actual use does not exceed the budget. Suppliers that exceed their budgets face escalating enforcement: technical assistance, then mandatory conservation plans, then potential state intervention in operations.

The implementation challenge is outdoor water use, which constitutes approximately 40 to 50 percent of residential water consumption and varies enormously by geography, lot size, and landscaping. A Sacramento homeowner with a 5,000-square-foot lawn uses 10 to 20 times more outdoor water than a San Francisco apartment dweller with no yard. The outdoor water budget must account for these differences while setting a standard that is enforceable, equitable, and politically sustainable. The Department of Water Resources is developing the regulatory framework, a process that involves balancing agricultural property rights, landscape industry interests, environmental flow requirements, and the reality that water use reduction will disproportionately affect communities (inland, suburban, agricultural) where outdoor water use is highest.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that "conservation as a way of life" is a euphemism for permanent rationing driven by the state's failure to invest in water supply infrastructure commensurate with population growth. California has added approximately 10 million people since the State Water Project was built in the 1960s without proportionally expanding storage, conveyance, or supply. The 55-gallon (declining to 42-gallon) indoor standard is achievable but prescriptive: it dictates how much water each person may use in their home, an intrusion into domestic life that no previous generation of Californians experienced. The conservation mandate places the burden of the water supply gap on residential users (who consume approximately 10 percent of the state's total water) while agricultural users (who consume approximately 80 percent) face less stringent regulation. Conservation is necessary. Its distribution is inequitable.

The generous read is that the conservation framework is the most important water policy reform in California since the State Water Project. The 2012-2016 drought demonstrated that Californians can reduce water use by 25 percent without significant hardship, and the 2018 legislation ensures that the efficiency gains are permanent rather than temporary. The 55-gallon indoor standard is achievable with standard low-flow fixtures (which most new homes already have) and requires no sacrifice beyond awareness. The framework will reduce urban water demand by approximately 1 million to 2 million acre-feet per year by 2030, the equivalent of building several new dams at a fraction of the cost. Conservation is the cheapest, fastest, and most environmentally benign water supply in California's portfolio.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The State Water Resources Control Board publishes monthly urban water production data by supplier. It does not publish per-capita consumption data by household type (single-family, multifamily, lot size), per-capita data by income level, or the cost of conservation (ratepayer investment in efficient fixtures, turf removal, and infrastructure) borne by individual households. The distributional impact of the conservation mandate (which communities bear the greatest burden, and whether low-income households face disproportionate costs) is not tracked.
