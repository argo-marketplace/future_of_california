# California Counts: The Final Bill
## What 350,000 Californians in hospice care per year, a $7 billion annual cost, a median enrollment of 18 days, and a fraud epidemic that turned dying into a business model reveal about the end-of-life care system California regulates while an industry exploits it

Approximately 350,000 Californians receive hospice care each year, a number that has grown approximately 5 percent annually as the population ages and end-of-life care preferences shift toward comfort-focused approaches. Hospice is a philosophy and a benefit: the Medicare Hospice Benefit, established in 1982, provides comprehensive palliative care (pain management, symptom control, emotional and spiritual support) to individuals with a terminal prognosis of six months or less who choose to forgo curative treatment. The benefit covers all care related to the terminal diagnosis: physician services, nursing, medical equipment, medications, counseling, and bereavement support. Medicare pays hospice providers a per-diem rate (approximately $200 to $1,100 per day depending on the level of care) that is intended to cover all services.

California has approximately 1,900 licensed hospice agencies, approximately 25 percent of all hospice agencies in the United States (despite California having approximately 12 percent of the U.S. population). The disproportionate number is driven by the concentration of for-profit hospice providers: approximately 80 percent of California hospice agencies are for-profit, compared to approximately 70 percent nationally. The for-profit hospice industry in California has grown rapidly, with approximately 500 new agency licenses issued between 2018 and 2023.

## The Fraud Problem

The rapid growth of for-profit hospice in California has been accompanied by widespread fraud. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services Office of Inspector General, the California Department of Public Health, and the FBI have investigated and prosecuted dozens of California hospice fraud cases involving enrollment of patients who are not terminally ill (to collect the per-diem payment), provision of minimal or no services (pocketing the per-diem while delivering less care than billed), kickback schemes (paying marketers, physicians, or assisted living facilities to refer patients), and targeting of non-English-speaking elderly residents in assisted living facilities who may not understand that they are enrolled in hospice.

SB 1004 (2023) and subsequent legislation strengthened California's hospice licensing requirements, imposed background check requirements for hospice operators, and gave the Department of Public Health additional enforcement tools (including the authority to deny licenses to applicants with fraud histories). The reforms respond to the reality that California's hospice licensing process was, for years, minimal: an applicant could obtain a hospice license with limited scrutiny of qualifications, financial resources, or history, enabling bad actors to enter the market easily.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California allowed the hospice industry to become a fraud vector by failing to regulate market entry while Medicare provided the financial incentive. The per-diem payment structure (a fixed daily payment regardless of services delivered) creates an incentive to enroll patients and provide minimal care: the difference between the per-diem received and the care delivered is profit. The 1,900 licensed agencies (in a state where the legitimate demand might support 500 to 800) include a substantial number whose business model depends on enrolling patients who are not hospice-appropriate, delivering less care than the per-diem funds, or both. The patients harmed are among the most vulnerable: elderly, often cognitively impaired, often non-English-speaking, and in the final months of life. The fraud is both financial (billing Medicare for services not provided) and human (denying dying people the care they deserve).

The generous read is that hospice care, when delivered by legitimate providers, is among the most humane and cost-effective forms of healthcare. Patients who receive quality hospice care report higher satisfaction, less pain, fewer unwanted medical interventions, and more time at home than patients who receive conventional end-of-life treatment in hospitals. The Medicare Hospice Benefit saves the healthcare system approximately $2,000 to $3,000 per beneficiary (by avoiding hospitalizations, ICU stays, and aggressive treatments that the patient does not want). The fraud problem is a regulatory failure, not a hospice failure: the solution is enforcement (prosecuting fraud, revoking licenses, strengthening oversight) rather than restricting access to a benefit that dying patients need and value.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CMS publishes hospice utilization data (enrollment, length of stay, diagnoses, provider type). The California Department of Public Health publishes licensing and enforcement data. Neither publishes a systematic comparison of care quality across California's 1,900 hospice agencies: which agencies provide high-quality palliative care, which provide minimal services, and which engage in fraudulent practices. The hospice quality measures published by CMS (patient experience surveys, process measures) capture some variation but do not identify fraud. A patient receiving no care and a patient receiving excellent care may generate identical billing records.
