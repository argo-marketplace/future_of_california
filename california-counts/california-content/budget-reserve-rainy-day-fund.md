# California Counts: The Rainy Day Fund
## What a $22 billion constitutional reserve, a $97 billion revenue decline from peak, Proposition 2's automatic deposit formula, and a budget system that swings from surplus to deficit in 18 months reveal about the fiscal volatility California chose when it built a tax system on capital gains

California's Budget Stabilization Account (BSA), commonly called the rainy day fund, held approximately $22 billion at its peak in fiscal year 2022-23. The fund was created by Proposition 2, approved by voters in November 2014 with 69 percent support. Proposition 2 requires the state to deposit 1.5 percent of General Fund revenues into the BSA each year, plus an additional transfer equal to capital gains tax revenues above 8 percent of General Fund tax revenues. The constitutional cap on the BSA is 10 percent of General Fund revenues, approximately $20 billion to $22 billion at current revenue levels. Once the cap is reached, excess deposits are redirected to infrastructure spending and supplemental debt payments.

The BSA was empty in 2014 when Proposition 2 passed. California had entered the Great Recession with no meaningful reserves and was forced to issue IOUs to vendors, cut services by approximately $30 billion, and borrow from internal funds. The state's bond rating fell to the lowest of any state (A-minus from Standard and Poor's). The fiscal trauma of 2008-2012 motivated Proposition 2, a rare moment of bipartisan agreement that California needed a structural savings mechanism to buffer its volatile revenue system.

---

## The Volatility Problem

California's General Fund revenue is among the most volatile of any state because of its heavy dependence on personal income tax (approximately 65 percent of General Fund revenues) and, within that, capital gains realizations from the state's concentration of technology, venture capital, and equity compensation wealth. In fiscal year 2021-22, capital gains realizations in California exceeded $280 billion, generating approximately $25 billion in state tax revenue. By fiscal year 2023-24, capital gains realizations had declined by approximately 40 percent, reducing revenue by approximately $10 billion to $15 billion. Total General Fund revenues fell from approximately $217 billion at peak (2021-22) to approximately $120 billion in the 2024-25 revised estimate, a decline of approximately $97 billion over two fiscal years.

The $22 billion rainy day fund, while the largest nominal reserve of any state, represents approximately 10 percent of the General Fund at normal revenue levels and approximately 15 percent of the revenue decline from peak to trough. The fund can cushion a mild recession but cannot offset the revenue collapse that occurs when the stock market declines and capital gains evaporate. California's fiscal system is structurally designed to produce surpluses during expansions and deficits during contractions, and no feasible reserve fund can fully smooth that cycle.

---

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that the rainy day fund is a Band-Aid on a structural wound. California's tax system generates extreme revenue volatility because it taxes capital gains at the same rate as ordinary income (up to 13.3 percent) and depends on a small number of ultra-high-income taxpayers for a disproportionate share of revenue (the top 1 percent pay approximately 50 percent of personal income tax). The $22 billion reserve is large in absolute terms but structurally insufficient: it covers less than two months of General Fund spending and less than one-sixth of a typical revenue decline. When the next recession arrives, the state will exhaust the BSA within one to two fiscal years, then face the same choices (service cuts, borrowing, accounting maneuvers) it faced in 2008. The rainy day fund creates the appearance of fiscal preparedness without the substance of fiscal stability.

The generous read is that Proposition 2 represents a genuine structural reform: a constitutional savings requirement that cannot be overridden by the legislature without a supermajority vote, with an automatic formula that forces deposits during good years when political pressure to spend is strongest. The BSA grew from zero to $22 billion in eight years, the most rapid reserve accumulation in state history. The fund successfully buffered the 2023-25 fiscal contraction without the IOUs, credit downgrades, and emergency cuts that characterized the 2008-2012 period. California's credit rating has improved from A-minus to AA-minus since Proposition 2's passage. The fund does not eliminate volatility. It has demonstrably reduced the fiscal damage volatility inflicts.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Department of Finance publishes BSA balances, deposit calculations, and withdrawal authority. It does not publish scenario analyses showing how the reserve would perform under various recession scenarios (mild, moderate, severe), how long it would last, or what level of reserves would be required to avoid mid-recession service cuts. The question of whether $22 billion is "enough" depends on assumptions about recession depth and duration that the state does not formally model for public consumption.
