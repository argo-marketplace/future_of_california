# California Counts: The Longest Cleanup
## What a 75 percent reduction in smog, 7 million asthma patients, a $3.4 billion cap-and-trade market, and 8 of the 10 worst-air cities in America reveal about the state that invented air quality regulation and still can't breathe

In 1970, Los Angeles recorded 200 days per year exceeding federal ozone standards. In 2024, it recorded approximately 40. The South Coast Air Basin, the nation's smoggiest air shed, has reduced peak ozone concentrations by approximately 75 percent since the 1970s while its population doubled and vehicle miles traveled tripled. The transformation is the greatest sustained air quality improvement in the history of industrialized civilization, achieved through the California Air Resources Board's regulatory program, the world's most aggressive vehicle emission standards, and four decades of increasingly stringent controls on stationary sources, fuels, and consumer products.

And yet. The American Lung Association's 2025 State of the Air report ranked eight California metropolitan areas among the 10 most ozone-polluted in the nation. Fresno, Bakersfield, and Visalia rank among the top five for year-round particle pollution. Approximately 7 million Californians have been diagnosed with asthma, the highest absolute number of any state. The Central Valley, trapped by geography between the Sierra Nevada and the Coast Range with limited air circulation, experiences pollution levels that would violate federal standards in any other state. California has the best air quality regulations in the world and the worst air quality in the nation. Both statements are true simultaneously because the state's geography, climate, population density, and wildfire burden overwhelm even the most aggressive regulatory program.

## CARB: The Most Powerful Environmental Agency You've Never Heard Of

The California Air Resources Board, established in 1967 (three years before the federal EPA), is the single most influential environmental regulatory agency in the world outside of national governments. Its authority to set vehicle emission standards, granted by a waiver in the federal Clean Air Act, means that California effectively sets emission standards for the entire U.S. auto industry: 17 states and the District of Columbia have adopted California's standards, representing approximately 40 percent of the national auto market. When CARB mandates that a certain percentage of vehicles sold must be zero-emission, automakers retool their global production lines because the California-plus-follower-states market is too large to ignore.

CARB's regulatory portfolio includes: vehicle emission standards (passenger cars, trucks, buses, off-road equipment); the Advanced Clean Cars II regulation requiring 100 percent zero-emission new car sales by 2035; the Advanced Clean Fleets regulation requiring zero-emission medium- and heavy-duty trucks; the cap-and-trade program, which covers approximately 80 percent of California's greenhouse gas emissions and generates $3.4 billion per year in auction revenue; fuel standards (the Low Carbon Fuel Standard); consumer product regulations (aerosol cans, coatings, solvents); and stationary source standards for refineries, power plants, and industrial facilities.

CARB's budget is approximately $1.3 billion per year, funded primarily through vehicle registration fees, cap-and-trade auction proceeds, and federal grants. It employs approximately 2,000 staff, including atmospheric scientists, engineers, toxicologists, and enforcement officers. Its regulatory output, measured by the number of rules adopted and the economic impact of those rules, exceeds that of many national environmental agencies.

The agency's effectiveness is rooted in a structural advantage unique to California: the Clean Air Act waiver. Section 209 of the Clean Air Act prohibits states from setting their own vehicle emission standards, with one exception: California, which had emission standards before the federal government, may apply for a waiver to maintain stricter standards. Other states may then adopt California's standards. This provision gives CARB leverage that no other state agency possesses: the ability to shape the national and global auto industry through regulation of a single state's market.

## The Geography Trap: Why Regulations Are Not Enough

California's worst air quality occurs not where pollution is generated most heavily but where geography traps it most effectively. The South Coast Air Basin (Los Angeles, Orange, Riverside, and San Bernardino counties) sits in a bowl between mountains and ocean, with an inversion layer that traps pollutants near the surface. The San Joaquin Valley (Fresno, Bakersfield, Visalia, Stockton, Modesto) is a 250-mile trough between the Sierra Nevada and the Coast Range, with stagnant air patterns during winter that trap particulate matter for weeks.

The San Joaquin Valley Air Pollution Control District has been in nonattainment of federal ozone and PM2.5 standards since the standards were established. The district estimates that approximately 1,300 premature deaths per year in the valley are attributable to air pollution. Hospitalization rates for asthma in the valley's agricultural communities are approximately double the state average. Children in Fresno and Bakersfield have asthma rates of approximately 20 percent, compared to 8 percent statewide.

The pollution sources in the San Joaquin Valley are distinct from those in Los Angeles. Agricultural operations (tractors, pumps, dust from tilling, ammonia from dairy operations) contribute approximately 25 percent of the valley's PM2.5. Vehicles and trucks (including the freight corridor along Interstate 5 and Highway 99) contribute approximately 30 percent. Residential wood burning contributes approximately 15 percent during winter. Industrial sources contribute the remainder. The valley's air quality problem cannot be solved by vehicle standards alone because a significant share of the pollution comes from agricultural and residential sources that are harder to regulate and politically more sensitive to restrict.

Wildfire smoke has emerged as the variable that overwhelms regulatory progress. The 2020 fire season produced particulate matter concentrations that exceeded anything recorded in the monitoring era. Single fire events can generate more PM2.5 in a week than the entire regulated economy produces in a year. CARB's regulatory authority does not extend to wildfire emissions, which are classified as exceptional events under federal air quality standards and excluded from attainment calculations. The practical result: California can achieve every regulatory target and still have the worst air quality in the nation if the fire season is severe, a frequency that climate change is increasing.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's air quality regulatory achievement, however real, has produced a two-tier outcome: the affluent, coastal, well-ventilated communities breathe air that meets federal standards, while the inland, low-income, geographically trapped communities breathe air that violates them. The 75 percent reduction in ozone is measured in Los Angeles. Bakersfield, Fresno, and the San Joaquin Valley have never met ozone standards and are not projected to under current regulations. The 7 million asthma patients are not randomly distributed: they are concentrated in the Central Valley, the Inland Empire, and the communities adjacent to freeways, ports, and industrial zones. CARB regulates vehicles brilliantly. It does not regulate geography. And the communities with the worst air quality are the communities with the least political power to demand the agricultural, residential, and industrial emission reductions that would make a difference.

The generous read is that without CARB, California's air quality would be catastrophically worse. The 75 percent ozone reduction occurred while population doubled and driving tripled, a decoupling of economic growth from pollution that no other jurisdiction has achieved at this scale. The Advanced Clean Cars II and Advanced Clean Fleets regulations will eliminate tailpipe emissions from new vehicles within 15 years, the most aggressive transportation decarbonization timeline in the world. The cap-and-trade program has reduced greenhouse gas emissions to below 1990 levels while generating $3.4 billion per year for climate investment. And the AB 617 Community Air Protection Program is the first regulatory framework in the nation to require community-level emission reduction plans in the most impacted areas, a direct response to the environmental justice critique. California's air is cleaner than it has been since pre-industrial times in every pollutant except wildfire smoke. The remaining challenge is geography, fire, and the last mile of agricultural and residential emissions. Those challenges are harder than regulating tailpipes. They are not impossible.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CARB operates the most extensive air quality monitoring network in the nation, with more than 250 monitoring stations measuring ozone, PM2.5, PM10, nitrogen dioxide, carbon monoxide, and toxic air contaminants. The network does not, however, capture exposure at the individual level: a resident who lives 100 meters from a freeway breathes air significantly worse than what the nearest monitoring station records. The South Coast AQMD's MATES V study (Multiple Air Toxics Exposure Study) found that cancer risk from air toxics varied by a factor of four across the Los Angeles Basin, with the highest risks in communities adjacent to industrial facilities, rail yards, and freeways.

The data does not capture indoor air quality, where most people spend 90 percent of their time. Cooking with natural gas, which approximately 70 percent of California households use, generates nitrogen dioxide and particulate matter at levels that can exceed outdoor standards inside the home. CARB has not regulated indoor air quality (which falls under building codes and consumer product standards rather than ambient air quality regulation), but the health burden from indoor pollution may rival that from outdoor pollution for some populations.

The data also does not capture the full health cost of California's air pollution. A 2024 study by the American Thoracic Society estimated that air pollution in California causes approximately 7,500 premature deaths, 3,800 hospitalizations, and 5,700 emergency department visits per year, at an economic cost of approximately $28 billion. These costs are borne disproportionately by the communities that CalEnviroScreen identifies as most burdened, the same communities that appear in every California Counts analysis of inequality: the Central Valley, the Inland Empire, South Los Angeles, and the port-adjacent neighborhoods of San Pedro Bay.
