# California Counts: The Other Pension
## What $338 billion in assets, a $79 billion unfunded liability, a 30-year repayment plan, and a benefit structure that penalizes mobility reveal about the retirement promise California made to its teachers and has not fully funded

The California State Teachers' Retirement System (CalSTRS) is the largest educator-only pension fund in the world. It provides retirement, disability, and survivor benefits to approximately 1 million members: active, inactive, and retired teachers, administrators, and other certificated employees of California's K-12 schools and community colleges. The fund holds approximately $338 billion in assets, making it the second-largest public pension fund in California (behind CalPERS) and one of the 10 largest in the world.

The system's unfunded liability, the difference between its projected long-term obligations and the assets available to pay them, is approximately $79 billion, producing a funded ratio of approximately 81 percent. The funded ratio has improved from approximately 67 percent in 2014, when AB 1469 enacted a funding reform that increased contribution rates for the state, school districts, and active teachers. The improvement has been achieved through a combination of increased contributions and investment returns, but the $79 billion gap remains one of the largest pension liabilities in the nation.

---

## The Contribution Structure

CalSTRS is funded through contributions from three sources: active teachers contribute approximately 10.25 percent of salary, school districts contribute approximately 19.1 percent of salary, and the state contributes approximately 10.8 percent of the CalSTRS payroll base from the general fund. The state contribution, approximately $3.5 billion per year, is one of the largest single line items in the state budget and represents money that funds retirement obligations rather than current educational services. Every dollar that goes to CalSTRS contributions is a dollar that does not go to teacher salaries, classroom supplies, school counselors, or facility maintenance.

The AB 1469 funding plan is designed to eliminate the unfunded liability by approximately 2046, a 30-year amortization period. The plan assumes an investment return rate of 7 percent, the same assumed rate used by CalPERS and discussed in the "Seven Percent Bet" installment of this series. If actual returns fall below 7 percent for a sustained period, the amortization period extends and the contribution rates must increase further. If returns exceed 7 percent, the unfunded liability shrinks faster than projected. The 30-year plan is a bet on investment returns, and the consequences of losing the bet fall on future teachers (higher contribution rates), future school districts (higher employer contributions that reduce funds for instruction), and future state budgets (higher general fund appropriations).

## The Benefit Structure

A CalSTRS member who retires at age 62 with 30 years of service receives a pension equal to approximately 60 percent of their final compensation (calculated as 2 percent per year of service times final compensation). The benefit formula is relatively straightforward compared to private-sector retirement plans, but two structural features are significant.

First, CalSTRS members do not participate in Social Security. Teachers hired before 2013 and employed in CalSTRS-covered positions do not pay into Social Security and do not earn Social Security credits. A teacher who worked 30 years in California schools and then worked 10 years in a Social Security-covered job receives a reduced Social Security benefit under the Windfall Elimination Provision (WEP), a federal law that reduces Social Security benefits for workers who also receive a pension from employment not covered by Social Security. The WEP penalty effectively means that CalSTRS members cannot supplement their pension with Social Security benefits earned from non-teaching work, a limitation that penalizes career-changers and workers with mixed employment histories.

Second, the CalSTRS benefit is not portable. A teacher who leaves California's public school system before vesting (typically five years) forfeits employer contributions and receives only a refund of their own contributions, without interest in many cases. A teacher who moves to another state must start a new pension in that state's system, losing the benefit of California service unless both states participate in a reciprocity agreement (CalSTRS has reciprocity with CalPERS but not with most out-of-state teacher retirement systems). The lack of portability penalizes teacher mobility, contributing to the state's difficulty recruiting teachers from other states.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that CalSTRS represents a $79 billion promise that California has not funded, made to workers who will not learn of the shortfall until retirement. The 30-year funding plan is a generational transfer: the gap was created by decades of insufficient contributions, and it will be closed by decades of elevated contributions that reduce the resources available for current students and current teachers. The 7 percent assumed return is not guaranteed, and any shortfall extends the repayment timeline or increases the contribution burden. The benefit structure, which excludes teachers from Social Security and penalizes interstate mobility, traps teachers in a system that may not fully deliver on its promises. California's teachers accepted lower current compensation in exchange for a defined benefit pension. Whether the pension will be fully paid depends on investment returns that no one controls and contribution levels that future legislatures may or may not maintain.

The generous read is that CalSTRS provides retirement security to approximately 300,000 retired teachers, most of whom receive pensions of $40,000 to $60,000 per year, a modest but stable income that keeps former teachers out of poverty in retirement. The AB 1469 funding reform was politically difficult (increasing contributions from all three sources) and has improved the funded ratio by 14 percentage points in a decade. The $338 billion in assets is professionally managed and diversified. And the defined benefit model, which provides a guaranteed income for life, is a fundamentally better retirement vehicle for workers than the defined contribution (401k) model that has replaced pensions in most of the private sector, where the average 401k balance at retirement is approximately $200,000, enough to generate income of $10,000 to $15,000 per year, a poverty-level retirement.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CalSTRS publishes its actuarial valuation, investment performance, and contribution rates annually. It does not publish a projection of the funded ratio under adverse market scenarios (sustained returns of 4 to 5 percent rather than the assumed 7 percent) that would allow policymakers and the public to understand the downside risk of the current funding plan.
