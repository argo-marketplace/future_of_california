# California Counts: The Standard
## What AB 392's "necessary" force standard, 400 fatal police shootings per year, a decertification process that took until 2022, and a transparency law that most departments resist reveal about the police accountability California legislated after a decade of protest

In 2019, California enacted AB 392, the California Act to Save Lives, which changed the legal standard for police use of deadly force from "reasonable" to "necessary." Under the prior standard (identical to the federal constitutional standard from Graham v. Connor), officers could use deadly force when a reasonable officer in the same circumstances would have believed it justified. Under AB 392, officers may use deadly force only when "necessary in defense of human life," meaning when there is an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury and no reasonable alternative (de-escalation, less-lethal force, disengagement) exists. The law made California the first state to adopt a "necessary" standard, exceeding the federal constitutional minimum.

California law enforcement officers fatally shoot approximately 150 to 200 people per year, with total fatal encounters (including shootings, tasers, restraint deaths, and vehicle strikes) of approximately 350 to 400 per year. The rate has not declined significantly since AB 392's passage in 2019, though researchers note that isolating the law's causal effect from other variables (COVID-19, protests, workforce changes, other reforms) is methodologically difficult. Fatal encounters disproportionately involve Black Californians (approximately 15 percent of deaths, versus 6 percent of the state population) and people experiencing mental health crises (approximately 25 percent of fatal encounters).

## The Accountability Infrastructure

SB 2 (2021) created the first decertification process for California peace officers, establishing the Peace Officer Standards Accountability Division within the Commission on Peace Officer Standards and Training (POST). Prior to SB 2, California was one of only four states without a mechanism to strip an officer's badge for misconduct. Under SB 2, POST can decertify officers for serious misconduct including excessive force, sexual assault, dishonesty, and bias-motivated conduct. The commission began accepting decertification cases in January 2023.

SB 1421 (2018) and SB 16 (2021) opened police personnel records to public disclosure for the first time, requiring departments to release records of officer shootings, use of serious force, confirmed findings of dishonesty, confirmed findings of sexual assault, and confirmed findings of racial bias. Prior to these laws, California had among the strongest police secrecy protections in the nation under Penal Code section 832.7. Compliance with the transparency laws has been inconsistent: multiple departments have been sued for withholding records, claiming various exemptions, or delaying responses beyond statutory deadlines.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California built an accountability architecture on paper (necessity standard, decertification, transparency) that has not yet produced measurable changes in police behavior. Fatal encounters have not declined. Decertification has been applied to a small number of officers (approximately 20 to 30 cases resolved in the first two years). The transparency laws are resisted by departments that spent decades operating under secrecy protections. AB 392's "necessary" standard is evaluated after the fact by the same district attorneys and juries that rarely found "unreasonable" force under the prior standard. The standard changed. The culture, the training, the supervision, and the consequences have not changed at a pace that produces different outcomes for the people who encounter California police at their most dangerous moments.

The generous read is that the accountability infrastructure built between 2018 and 2022 represents the most significant structural reform of California policing since the 1960s. Decertification means that officers fired for misconduct in one department can no longer be hired by another (the "wandering officer" problem). Transparency means that patterns of misconduct are now discoverable by journalists, researchers, and civil rights organizations. The "necessary" standard elevates de-escalation from a preferred tactic to a legal requirement. These are institutional changes whose effects compound over time as training adapts, supervisors enforce the standard, and the decertification threat alters career incentives. Measuring the effect in three to five years is premature for structural reform that reshapes institutional culture over a generation.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Department of Justice publishes use-of-force data reported by law enforcement agencies under AB 71 (the URSUS system). The data captures incidents but does not capture non-events (encounters where force was considered and not used, de-escalation successes) or near-misses. The question of whether officers are de-escalating more frequently since AB 392 cannot be answered because the baseline (de-escalation attempts before the law) was never measured, and current de-escalation is not systematically recorded.
