# California Counts: The Regressive Dollar
## What a 7.25 percent base rate, $76 billion in annual revenue, 10.25 percent combined rates, and a services exemption that erodes the base every year reveal about the consumption tax California never modernized

California's base state sales tax rate is 7.25 percent, among the highest in the nation. The rate has seven components: a 6 percent state rate allocated to the general fund, a 0.25 percent allocated to local governments, a 0.5 percent allocated to local public safety (the 2011 realignment), and a 0.5 percent allocated to the Local Revenue Fund. Local jurisdictions add district taxes, approved by voters, that bring the combined rate to 8.5 to 10.25 percent in most California communities. The highest combined rates, in parts of Los Angeles County and the City of Santa Fe Springs, exceed 10.25 percent.

The sales tax generates approximately $76 billion per year in combined state and local revenue, making it the second-largest tax revenue source in California after the personal income tax. Local governments, particularly cities, depend on the sales tax as a primary discretionary revenue source. The "fiscalization of land use," in which cities zone for retail rather than housing to capture sales tax revenue, is a direct consequence of Proposition 13's restriction on property tax revenue and the sales tax's allocation to the city where the sale occurs.

## The Services Exemption

California's sales tax applies to tangible personal property (goods) but exempts most services. A consumer who buys a $500 television pays sales tax. A consumer who pays a $500 legal bill, accounting fee, hair salon visit, gym membership, streaming subscription, or software-as-a-service contract does not. The services exemption was established when the sales tax was created in 1933, when the economy was predominantly goods-based. In 2026, services constitute approximately 70 percent of consumer spending in California, meaning the sales tax applies to a shrinking share of economic activity.

The erosion of the tax base is structural and accelerating. As consumption shifts from goods to services, from physical retail to digital delivery, and from ownership to subscription, the sales tax base narrows. The Legislative Analyst's Office has estimated that extending the sales tax to services (with exemptions for healthcare, education, and housing) would generate approximately $25 billion to $40 billion per year in additional revenue at the current rate. No California governor or legislature has proposed taxing services in the modern era. The political opposition from service industries (legal, financial, medical, technology) and the regressivity concerns (a services tax would increase costs for necessities like haircuts and car repair) have made the proposal politically untouchable.

---

## The Regressivity Problem

The sales tax is regressive: it consumes a larger share of income for low-income households than for high-income households because low-income households spend a larger share of their income on taxable goods. A household earning $40,000 per year and spending 80 percent of income on consumption, including taxable goods, may pay an effective sales tax rate of 4 to 5 percent of income. A household earning $400,000 per year, spending 40 percent of income on consumption, much of it on exempt services, pays an effective rate of 1 to 2 percent.

California partially offsets the regressivity through exemptions: groceries (food for home consumption) are exempt from sales tax, as are prescription medications. These exemptions reduce the tax burden on necessities but do not eliminate the structural regressivity. The sales tax remains the most regressive major tax in California's revenue system, a counterweight to the highly progressive income tax.

---

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California relies on a consumption tax designed in 1933 for a goods-based economy to fund 21st-century government. The services exemption narrows the base every year as the economy dematerializes. The regressivity means that the tax falls hardest on the workers least able to afford it. The local government dependence on sales tax revenue produces land-use decisions (approving auto malls and shopping centers over housing) that exacerbate the housing crisis. And the combined rates above 10 percent create a price differential that drives online shopping (partially addressed by the Wayfair decision and subsequent legislation requiring online sales tax collection) and cross-border purchasing. The sales tax is an obsolete revenue instrument that California has not modernized because modernization (taxing services, reducing rates, broadening the base) requires political courage that no coalition has mustered.

The generous read is that the sales tax provides $76 billion in stable, broadly distributed revenue that funds essential local and state services. Unlike the income tax, which swings 20 to 30 percent between boom and bust years, the sales tax is relatively stable because consumption patterns change less dramatically than capital gains realizations. The exemptions for groceries and medications protect necessities. The district tax structure allows local communities to fund specific needs (transportation, public safety, libraries) through voter-approved measures. And the administrative infrastructure for collecting the sales tax is mature, efficient, and well-understood by businesses. The sales tax is imperfect. It is also $76 billion per year in revenue that funds the schools, roads, and public safety that Californians use every day.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Department of Tax and Fee Administration (CDTFA) publishes sales tax revenue by jurisdiction, industry category, and quarter. It does not publish sales tax burden data by household income, which would quantify the regressivity at the household level. The effective sales tax rate by income decile, the key measure of regressivity, must be estimated from consumer expenditure surveys and tax modeling rather than from direct measurement.
