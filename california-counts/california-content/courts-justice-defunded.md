# California Counts: Justice Delayed, Justice Defunded
## What 4.6 million case filings, 1,700 judicial vacancies and unfunded positions, a $3.7 billion budget, and a 76 percent self-representation rate reveal about the largest court system in the Western Hemisphere

California's judicial branch processes approximately 4.6 million case filings per year across 58 superior courts, six appellate districts, and one Supreme Court. It is the largest court system in the Western Hemisphere and the third-largest in the world, behind only the national court systems of China and India. The system employs approximately 18,000 people, including roughly 1,700 authorized judicial officers (judges and commissioners). Its total budget for fiscal year 2025-26 is approximately $3.7 billion from the state General Fund, supplemented by filing fees, fines, and county contributions. That $3.7 billion funds the adjudication of every felony, misdemeanor, civil dispute, family law matter, juvenile case, probate proceeding, traffic citation, and small claims action in a state of 39 million people. It works out to roughly $95 per resident per year. For comparison, California spends approximately $132,000 per prisoner per year, $11,300 per Medi-Cal enrollee, and $18,000 per K-12 student. The institution charged with resolving every legal dispute in the state receives less per capita funding than any other major branch of California government.

---

## The Underfunding: A Timeline

California's court system was historically funded by the counties. Trial Court Funding Reform, enacted through AB 233 in 1997, shifted primary funding responsibility from the 58 counties to the state, a change designed to equalize resources across jurisdictions and insulate courts from county budget pressures. The transition was never fully funded. The state assumed responsibility at levels below what the counties had been spending, and subsequent budget crises eroded the base further.

The Great Recession devastated the courts. Between fiscal year 2008-09 and 2013-14, the judicial branch absorbed approximately $1.3 billion in cumulative budget cuts, a reduction of roughly 30 percent in real terms. Courts responded by closing courthouses, eliminating clerk positions, reducing hours of operation, and consolidating case types. The Los Angeles Superior Court, the largest trial court in the nation with more than 580 judicial officers, closed 10 courthouses during the budget crisis. The San Francisco Superior Court reduced its clerk staff by 40 percent. Rural courts in the Northern California counties operated on skeleton staffs.

Funding has recovered partially but not fully. The $3.7 billion fiscal year 2025-26 budget exceeds the pre-recession level in nominal dollars but remains below it in inflation-adjusted terms when accounting for population growth, case complexity increases, and unfunded mandates. The Judicial Council, the administrative body of the California courts, has identified an annual funding shortfall of approximately $700 million to $1 billion relative to what is needed to provide adequate access to justice statewide.

The structural problem is that court funding competes with Medi-Cal, education, prisons, and other general fund obligations that have constitutional or political protections the courts do not. Proposition 98 guarantees education spending. Federal law and court orders mandate Medi-Cal and prison spending levels. The judicial branch has no comparable protection. In every budget negotiation, the courts are the easiest cut because their constituency, litigants and crime victims, has no organized lobbying presence, and the consequences of underfunding, delayed justice, unreachable clerks, longer case processing times, manifest not as a visible crisis but as a slow degradation of access that affects primarily those who cannot afford alternatives.

## The Access Crisis: 76 Percent Without a Lawyer

The most consequential number in California's court system is 76 percent: the percentage of litigants in civil cases who appear without an attorney, known as self-represented or pro se litigants. In family law cases, including divorce, custody, and domestic violence restraining orders, the self-representation rate exceeds 80 percent. In unlawful detainer (eviction) cases, approximately 90 percent of tenants appear without counsel. In small claims, self-representation is universal by design.

Self-represented litigants face a court system designed for attorneys. The forms are complex. The procedural rules are technical. The deadlines are unforgiving. A missed filing, an improperly served document, or a failure to respond within the statutory period can result in a default judgment regardless of the merits of the case. Studies consistently find that self-represented litigants achieve worse outcomes than represented parties, not because their cases are weaker but because they cannot navigate the procedural requirements that determine whether their cases are heard at all.

The self-representation crisis is a direct consequence of the cost of legal services. The average hourly rate for an attorney in California is approximately $350 to $500 in urban areas, with family law attorneys averaging $300 to $400 per hour. A contested divorce with custody issues can cost $15,000 to $50,000 or more per side. An eviction defense, if handled by a private attorney, costs $3,000 to $10,000. For a tenant earning $40,000 per year facing eviction, hiring an attorney is financially impossible. The choice is self-representation or no representation, which in practice often means no meaningful defense.

Legal aid organizations, funded through the Legal Services Corporation, the State Bar's Interest on Lawyers' Trust Accounts (IOLTA) program, and state appropriations, serve approximately 1.5 million Californians per year through direct representation, advice clinics, and self-help resources. But legal aid budgets meet an estimated 15 to 20 percent of the legal needs of low-income Californians. The Justice Gap study, conducted by the Legal Services Corporation nationally, found that 92 percent of civil legal problems experienced by low-income Americans receive inadequate or no legal help. California's figures are comparable.

## The Criminal Backlog

The COVID-19 pandemic created a criminal case backlog that the courts have not fully resolved. Court closures and reduced operations from March 2020 through mid-2021 delayed jury trials for more than a year in many jurisdictions. The constitutional right to a speedy trial, codified in California as the right to trial within 60 days of arraignment for felonies and 30 days for misdemeanors (with waivers), was effectively suspended through emergency orders during the pandemic. Defendants who did not waive time waited in jail or on monitored release for trials that could not be scheduled.

By early 2026, the criminal backlog has been substantially reduced but not eliminated. The Judicial Council reported that pending criminal cases have returned to approximately pre-pandemic levels in most large courts, but that case processing times remain 15 to 25 percent longer than 2019 baselines. The time from felony arraignment to disposition averaged approximately 180 days statewide in 2024, compared to approximately 150 days in 2019. For defendants in custody awaiting trial, every additional day of delay is a day of pretrial incarceration, a cost borne by the individual (lost employment, family disruption) and by the county (jail costs of approximately $200 to $400 per day depending on the facility).

The criminal justice reforms of the past decade, including Proposition 47 (reclassifying certain felonies as misdemeanors), Proposition 57 (parole consideration for nonviolent offenders), and SB 1437 (limiting felony murder liability), have reduced certain case volumes but increased legal complexity. Resentencing petitions under SB 1437 and SB 775 alone generated tens of thousands of additional proceedings. Each reform, however meritorious in its policy goals, adds procedural requirements that consume judicial time and clerk resources in a system already operating below capacity.

## The Technology Gap

California's courts operate on technology infrastructure that ranges from modern to antiquated, varying by county. The pandemic forced an acceleration of remote proceedings: Zoom hearings, electronic filing, and digital case management became standard in many courts within months. These changes, resisted for years on procedural and cultural grounds, proved workable and in some cases superior to in-person alternatives for routine matters.

But the technological transformation is uneven. Large courts, Los Angeles, San Francisco, San Diego, have invested in modern case management systems, electronic filing platforms, and remote hearing infrastructure. Smaller rural courts, with limited IT budgets and staff, still operate on legacy systems, some dating to the 1990s. The Judicial Council's effort to develop a statewide case management system has been in various stages of planning for more than a decade. No unified system exists. Each superior court operates its own case management system, creating data silos that prevent statewide analysis of case outcomes, judicial workload, and resource needs.

The lack of integrated data is itself a governance problem. The Judicial Council cannot determine, at the statewide level, the average time to disposition for specific case types, the rate of default judgments against self-represented litigants, the correlation between judicial vacancies and case processing delays, or the outcomes of cases involving legal aid versus self-represented parties. The data exists in 58 separate systems, in 58 different formats, collected with 58 different methodologies. The state's court system processes 4.6 million cases per year and cannot answer basic questions about how well it does so.

## The Judicial Vacancy Problem

California has approximately 1,700 authorized judicial positions across its 58 superior courts. At any given time, 80 to 150 of those positions are vacant due to retirements, elevations, and the appointment process. The governor appoints superior court judges to fill vacancies, and the appointment process, which involves background checks, vetting by the Commission on Judicial Nominees Evaluation, and political considerations, can take months. During that period, the vacancy is unfilled and the court's caseload is redistributed among remaining judges.

The vacancy problem is compounded by the issue of unfunded positions. The Judicial Council has identified approximately 200 additional judicial positions needed statewide to handle current caseloads, based on its Judicial Needs Assessment workload model. These positions have not been authorized or funded. The result is that even a court with no vacancies may be understaffed relative to its caseload. Courts in fast-growing jurisdictions, including Riverside, San Bernardino, and Sacramento, have caseloads per judge that significantly exceed the statewide average.

Judges are not fungible. A judge experienced in complex civil litigation cannot be efficiently reassigned to juvenile dependency cases. A family law specialist reassigned to criminal trials requires training and ramp-up time. The assignment of judges across case types within a court is managed by the presiding judge, but the flexibility to reassign is constrained by specialization, courtroom availability, and the simple mathematics of too many cases for too few judges.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has defunded its judiciary to the point where the constitutional promise of access to justice is a fiction for most residents. The 76 percent self-representation rate in civil cases means that three-quarters of people who seek justice in California's courts do so without the professional help needed to navigate the system. The $95 per resident per year spent on courts is less than what most Californians spend on streaming subscriptions. The criminal backlog and extended processing times mean that defendants wait months longer for trials than the law intends. The technology is fragmented across 58 systems. The vacancy rate means that even authorized positions go unfilled for months. The court system is the branch of government that every other branch depends on for enforcement, interpretation, and dispute resolution, and it receives the smallest share of the budget, the least political attention, and the fewest structural protections of any major institution. Justice in California is available to those who can afford an attorney and a procedural obstacle course for those who cannot.

The generous read is that California's court system processes 4.6 million cases per year with a budget of $3.7 billion and a staff of 18,000, a throughput that no other state judicial system matches at comparable per-capita funding. The pandemic-era technology adoption, remote hearings, electronic filing, and digital access, has permanently expanded access for litigants who previously had to take time off work, arrange transportation, and appear physically in court for routine matters. The self-help centers operated by most superior courts provide free assistance to self-represented litigants, and their expansion has been a priority of the Judicial Council. The criminal backlog has been substantially resolved. Legal aid funding, while insufficient, has increased in recent years through state appropriations and philanthropic investment. And the Judicial Council's governance structure, which combines judicial leadership with public accountability, has navigated budget crises, a pandemic, and sweeping criminal justice reforms while maintaining a system that, however imperfect, provides due process to every person who enters a California courtroom. The system is strained. It has not broken.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Judicial Council publishes aggregate case filing data, budget documents, and workload assessments. What it does not publish in a standardized, statewide format is outcome data: disposition rates by case type, rates of default judgment, outcomes for self-represented versus represented litigants, or case-level processing times. The 58 superior courts collect this data locally but do not report it in a format that allows statewide comparison or analysis. The result is that the most basic question about California's court system, whether it produces just outcomes, cannot be answered with data.

The data does not capture the cases that are never filed. An estimated 50 to 70 percent of civil legal problems experienced by low-income Californians, including wage theft, habitability violations, consumer fraud, and benefit denials, do not result in court filings because the affected individuals do not know they have a legal remedy, cannot afford an attorney, or conclude that the cost and complexity of litigation exceed the potential benefit. These unfiled cases are the dark matter of the justice system: they represent injustices that the court system could theoretically address but never sees.

The data also does not capture the human cost of delay. A family law case that takes 18 months to resolve is 18 months of uncertainty about custody, support, and housing for parents and children. An eviction case that takes three months to hear is three months of a tenant living under the threat of displacement. A criminal case that takes six months from arraignment to trial is six months of a defendant presumed innocent living under restrictions that may include electronic monitoring, curfew, and employment limitations. The cost of delay is not measured in the court system's statistics. It is measured in the lives of the 4.6 million people whose cases enter the system each year and wait.

---

California's Constitution guarantees that "a person may not be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law." Due process requires a functioning court system. A functioning court system requires judges, clerks, courtrooms, technology, and legal assistance for those who cannot afford it. California's court system has fewer of each of these than its caseload demands. The result is not the dramatic failure that makes headlines. It is the quiet failure that does not: the default judgment entered against the tenant who could not figure out the forms, the custody order delayed for a year because the court has no available hearing dates, the small business owner who does not sue because the cost of litigation exceeds the amount in dispute. Justice in California is not denied. It is rationed, by budget, by complexity, and by the accident of whether you can afford someone to help you navigate it. The courthouse doors are open. What happens inside them depends on what you bring through the door, and for 76 percent of Californians who enter, what they bring is themselves, alone, against a system designed for someone else.
