# California Counts: The Bias Count
## What 2,100 reported hate crimes per year, a 50 percent increase since 2019, anti-Asian violence during COVID, and a reporting system that captures perhaps one-third of actual incidents reveal about the bias-motivated violence California tracks imperfectly and prosecutes rarely

California reported approximately 2,100 hate crimes in 2023, the most of any state and a roughly 50 percent increase from approximately 1,400 in 2019. The increase tracks national trends but is particularly pronounced in California for anti-Asian hate crimes (which increased approximately 200 percent between 2019 and 2021, driven by COVID-era targeting), anti-Black hate crimes (approximately 30 percent of all reported incidents, the largest single category), anti-LGBTQ hate crimes (approximately 20 percent), and anti-Jewish hate crimes (approximately 10 percent, with a sharp increase in 2023-2024 following the Israel-Gaza conflict).

The California Department of Justice collects hate crime data from local law enforcement agencies under the Reporting of Hate Crimes Act. The data is compiled annually and published through the DOJ's OpenJustice portal. However, the reported numbers substantially undercount actual hate crime incidents. The Bureau of Justice Statistics' National Crime Victimization Survey estimates that approximately 60 to 70 percent of hate crimes go unreported to police, meaning California's actual hate crime incidence may be 5,000 to 7,000 per year rather than the 2,100 reported. Underreporting is driven by distrust of police (particularly in immigrant and LGBTQ communities), fear of retaliation, language barriers, and the belief that reporting will not lead to action.

---

## The Prosecution Gap

Of the approximately 2,100 hate crimes reported annually, approximately 1,000 to 1,200 result in the identification of a suspect. Of those, approximately 200 to 300 are prosecuted with a hate crime enhancement (an additional penalty applied to an underlying offense when bias motivation is proven). The prosecution rate for hate crime enhancements is approximately 10 to 15 percent of reported incidents, reflecting the high evidentiary bar: prosecutors must prove both the underlying offense and the specific bias motivation, typically through statements, social media posts, or documented pattern of bias that demonstrate the defendant selected the victim because of a protected characteristic.

AB 1947 (2020) expanded the reporting window for hate crime complaints to three years and required law enforcement agencies to adopt hate crime reporting protocols. SB 1161 (2022) required the DOJ to develop model hate crime training for officers. Despite these measures, multiple audits have found that law enforcement agencies inconsistently identify, document, and report hate crimes: the same incident may be classified as a hate crime in one jurisdiction and a generic assault in another, depending on officer training, departmental culture, and investigative resources.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's hate crime data is an undercount nested within an undercount: victims do not report (60 to 70 percent never contact police), police do not identify (inadequate training and inconsistent classification), prosecutors do not pursue (high evidentiary bar, plea bargains that drop the enhancement), and the published numbers create a false precision that understates the scope of bias-motivated violence. The 50 percent increase since 2019 may reflect increased actual violence, increased reporting by victims (driven by public campaigns encouraging reporting), increased identification by police (driven by training mandates), or all three simultaneously. The state cannot distinguish between these explanations because the baseline (true prevalence) is unknown. Hate crime data measures the system's detection capacity as much as it measures the phenomenon.

The generous read is that California has the most robust hate crime data collection system of any state, with mandatory reporting, annual publication, disaggregation by bias type and offense type, and geographic detail sufficient for analysis. The 50 percent increase, while alarming, partly reflects improved detection: training mandates, community reporting campaigns, and dedicated hate crime units in major police departments have increased the share of actual incidents that enter the data. The state's civil rights framework (including the Ralph Civil Rights Act and the Bane Civil Rights Act) provides civil remedies in addition to criminal prosecution, allowing victims to pursue accountability through lower-burden civil suits even when criminal prosecution is declined. The data system is imperfect. It is better than what most states produce, and it is improving.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The DOJ publishes hate crime counts by bias motivation, offense type, and jurisdiction. It does not publish data on the outcomes of reported incidents (investigation, arrest, prosecution, conviction, sentence), making it impossible to determine what happens after a hate crime is reported. The pipeline from report to accountability is opaque: the state tracks the input (reports) but not the output (outcomes). Whether reporting a hate crime leads to investigation, arrest, prosecution, or any form of accountability for the victim is not systematically measurable from state-published data.
