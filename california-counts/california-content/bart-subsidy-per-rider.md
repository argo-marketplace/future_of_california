# California Counts: The Subsidy Per Ride
## What 430,000 pre-pandemic daily riders, a post-COVID collapse to 180,000, a farebox recovery ratio that fell from 70 percent to 30 percent, and a $30 billion capital backlog reveal about the transit system the Bay Area built for a commute that no longer fills the trains

BART opened in 1972 as one of the most ambitious public transit projects in American history: a heavy-rail rapid transit system spanning five counties, designed to connect suburban bedroom communities to downtown San Francisco and Oakland employment centers. At its pre-pandemic peak in fiscal year 2018-19, BART carried approximately 430,000 daily riders, generating fare revenue of approximately $730 million per year and achieving a farebox recovery ratio of roughly 70 percent, one of the highest of any transit system in the nation. That financial model assumed a steady stream of commuters paying $5 to $15 per trip across the Bay five days a week. In March 2020, that stream stopped. Three years of federal relief funding filled the gap. When the money ran out in 2025, what remained was a system spending roughly what it spent before, carrying roughly 40 percent of the riders it once did.

---

## The Ridership Gap

BART's daily ridership in fiscal year 2024-25 averaged approximately 180,000 to 200,000 unlinked trips, representing roughly 42 to 47 percent of pre-pandemic levels. The recovery has been real but asymmetric. Weekend and off-peak ridership has recovered more fully, to approximately 65 to 70 percent of pre-pandemic levels, because recreational and essential travel patterns were less disrupted by remote work. Peak-hour commuter ridership, which historically drove the majority of BART's revenue due to the higher fares charged on longer cross-bay trips, has recovered to approximately 35 to 40 percent of pre-pandemic volumes. The San Francisco Transbay tube, BART's most heavily used segment and the one that generates the highest average fare per trip, carries far fewer riders than it did in 2019.

The reason is structural rather than temporary. Remote and hybrid work arrangements have settled at a new equilibrium. Bay Area employers surveyed in 2024 reported that approximately 55 to 60 percent of office workers were working from home at least two days per week, with the share higher in the technology sector that dominates San Francisco and the East Bay. A commuter who comes to the office three days per week instead of five generates 40 percent less BART fare revenue than the same commuter working in-person five days. Aggregated across the hundreds of thousands of workers who once rode BART to downtown San Francisco and Oakland, the revenue loss is not a temporary disruption. It is a new steady state.

## The Cost Per Ride

BART's operating budget for fiscal year 2024-25 is approximately $1.0 billion. With approximately 180,000 daily riders, that works out to an operating cost of roughly $5,500 per daily rider per year, or approximately $15.07 per one-way trip assuming an average of two trips per day per rider. Against an average fare of approximately $4.50 per trip, the operating subsidy embedded in each BART trip is approximately $10.57.

The farebox recovery ratio tells the same story differently. Fare revenue in fiscal year 2024-25 is approximately $310 million, against operating costs of approximately $1.0 billion. Fares cover roughly 31 percent of operating costs. The remaining 69 percent, approximately $690 million, is covered by a combination of property taxes in the four-county BART district (Alameda, Contra Costa, San Francisco, and San Mateo), state formula funding, federal grants, and one-time relief appropriations.

The property tax component is the bedrock. BART levies a property tax of approximately 0.1 percent of assessed value across its four-county district, generating approximately $300 million to $350 million per year. This tax was authorized by the voters who approved BART's creation in 1962 and has never been put to a re-vote, though the BART district has sought and won additional parcel taxes for specific purposes. In a region of rising property values, the assessed-value tax grows with the real estate market even when ridership does not.

## The Capital Backlog: $30 Billion Through 2050

BART's operating deficit is the immediate problem. The capital backlog is the long-term one. BART has identified approximately $30 billion in capital investment needs through 2050, including fleet replacement, station modernization, infrastructure rehabilitation, and system expansion. The current fleet of approximately 775 rail cars is aging; the average age of a BART car is now over 20 years for the legacy fleet, though the introduction of the new Fleet of the Future cars, approximately 775 new vehicles ordered at a cost of $3.3 billion, is gradually replacing the oldest vehicles. The stations, many of which opened in 1972, have accumulated deferred maintenance in electrical systems, escalators, elevators, and structural elements. BART escalators famously require high maintenance rates in the nation due to the combination of heavy use and the Bay Area's outdoor humidity.

The capital needs are funded through a patchwork of federal capital grants, state bond funds, and regional measure revenues. Measure RR, a $3.5 billion bond passed by Bay Area voters in 2016, provided a dedicated capital source for infrastructure rehabilitation. Measure RR funds are projected to run out in the early 2030s. The $30 billion figure through 2050 implies a capital investment rate of approximately $1.2 billion per year, against a current capital spending level of approximately $600 million to $800 million per year. The gap compounds the farther into the future it goes.

## The Public Good Question

BART's fiscal structure raises a question that the agency's planners and the region's voters have answered differently at different moments. Is BART a public utility, like a road, that should be funded through taxes because its existence benefits everyone in the region regardless of whether they ride it? Or is BART a service, like a movie theater, that should recover its operating costs from the users who consume it?

The pre-pandemic 70 percent farebox recovery ratio was unusually high by American transit standards and reflected an implicit policy of minimizing subsidy. The LA Metro, by comparison, recovers approximately 20 percent of its operating costs from fares. New York City's subway recovers approximately 45 percent. Most European urban rail systems operate at 50 to 70 percent farebox recovery. BART's high ratio was possible because it served a wealthy region with high average fares on long cross-bay trips. It created institutional habits of cost recovery that are now unsustainable given post-pandemic ridership levels.

The question of how much subsidy BART should receive per ride is not separable from the question of what BART is for. If BART is primarily a commuter rail service for downtown office workers, the 30 percent recovery ratio represents a genuine subsidy to workers who could afford other options. If BART is regional infrastructure, equivalent to Interstate 80 in its function as a connector across the Bay, then its subsidy is no more anomalous than the gas taxes and federal highway funds that keep the bridge lanes moving. The state does not require Interstate 80 to recover its operating costs from tolls.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that BART was built around a commuting pattern that remote work has partially obsoleted, and the agency's financial model has not adapted to the new reality. A system designed to recover 70 percent of its costs from fares cannot sustain that model when ridership is at 43 percent of peak levels, and the solution of filling the gap with property taxes amounts to a significant tax increase on Bay Area homeowners to subsidize a service that a declining share of them use. The $30 billion capital backlog means that even if BART stabilizes its operating budget, it faces a generation of capital investment that no current funding source covers. Bay Area residents have already approved multiple bond measures and parcel taxes for BART. The appetite for additional levies is not unlimited.

The generous read is that BART provides approximately $2 billion to $3 billion per year in economic benefits to the Bay Area through congestion reduction, land value enhancement near stations, reduced vehicle emissions, and transportation access for the 25 to 30 percent of Bay Area workers who do not drive. The subsidy per ride is real, but the alternative, those 180,000 daily riders driving instead, would require highway capacity that does not exist and would add to an already strained regional transportation network. Transit systems everywhere run operating deficits; the question is whether the regional benefits justify the regional cost. For a system that carried 430,000 people per day as recently as 2018, the answer from Bay Area voters has been a consistent yes. The post-pandemic fiscal crisis is a challenge of scale, not of purpose.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

BART publishes detailed ridership and financial data, but the public reports do not consistently show the distribution of trips by station pair, fare paid, or time of day in a way that would allow external analysis of which routes and segments are most and least cost-effective. The subsidy per ride figure of approximately $10.57 is a system average; the subsidy on a peak transbay trip is much lower than the subsidy on an off-peak local trip within the East Bay. Whether BART's fare structure captures those differences efficiently, or whether restructuring fares could improve cost recovery without reducing ridership, is an analysis the agency conducts internally but has not fully surfaced in public documents. The $30 billion capital need figure is also a planning estimate subject to revision as costs, federal funding availability, and ridership projections change.
