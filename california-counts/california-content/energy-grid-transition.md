# California Counts: The Grid That Swallowed the Sun
## What 278 terawatt-hours of electricity data reveal about a grid mid-transformation

California's electricity grid ran entirely on clean energy for an average of seven hours per day on most days in 2025. That sentence would have been science fiction a decade ago. In 2015, renewables supplied about 25 percent of the state's electricity. By 2024, the number was 52.3 percent, and clean energy including nuclear and large hydro reached 62 percent. The state is tracking ahead of its SB 100 targets, the 2018 law requiring 60 percent renewables by 2030 and 100 percent clean electricity by 2045.

But the grid data tells a more complicated story than the milestone press releases suggest. California is simultaneously leading the country in renewable deployment and curtailing 3.4 million megawatt-hours of clean energy it cannot use. It has the second-lowest per-capita electricity consumption in the nation and the second-highest residential electricity rates. It added 5,743 megawatts of battery storage in a single year and watched its largest battery facility catch fire. It kept Diablo Canyon nuclear running to preserve reliability while fighting federal cancellation of its offshore wind leases. The grid is not transitioning from one stable state to another. It is lurching forward and patching as it goes, and the data captures every lurch.

---

## The Generation Flip

The most important chart in California energy is the one that shows the generation mix over time. In 2020, natural gas produced roughly half of California's in-state electricity: 97.4 terawatt-hours. By 2024, gas had fallen to 35 percent. Solar alone had grown to 21.3 percent of total system power, exceeding half of all renewable generation for the first time. Wind contributed 11.9 percent, producing 33,102 gigawatt-hours.

The decline of gas and rise of solar is not gradual. It is a crossover that happened faster than most forecasts predicted. Gas-fired generation in the first eight months of 2025 was down 18 percent from the same period in 2020. Natural gas plants still represent 39,689 megawatts of installed capacity, roughly 45 percent of the state's total generating fleet. But many of those plants now run only during evening peaks and summer heat waves. They are becoming what grid operators call peakers: expensive insurance policies that sit idle most of the year and fire up when the sun goes down or demand spikes.

Coal tells a cleaner story. California's last in-state coal plant, the 63-megawatt Argus Cogeneration facility in San Bernardino County, shut down in 2022. Coal imports, which were 18.2 percent of the state's power mix in 2008, have fallen to roughly 3 percent and are expected to reach zero by 2026.

The generation flip has delivered real emissions reductions. Electricity sector emissions have fallen 28.6 percent since 2000 for in-state generation and 61.9 percent for imports. In 2023, the sector's carbon intensity hit its lowest level on record. The electricity grid is no longer the state's largest emissions problem. Transportation is.

## The Duck Becomes a Canyon

In 2013, CAISO published what became the most famous chart in energy policy: the duck curve. It showed that as solar generation grew, the midday net load, total demand minus renewable generation, would plunge, creating a deep belly that required conventional generators to ramp down sharply during the day and ramp up even more sharply in the evening. The original projection showed the midday net load minimum falling from about 22,000 megawatts in 2012 to roughly 12,000 megawatts by 2020 if 10 gigawatts of solar were added.

Reality outpaced the projection. By 2020, utility-scale solar exceeded 15 gigawatts, and net load minima dropped below 5,000 megawatts. By spring 2025, the duck curve had deepened into what analysts now call a canyon. Net load valleys swing over 24 gigawatts within eight hours on average spring days. The midday net load has gone negative on some days, meaning renewable generation exceeds total grid demand.

The evening ramp, the steep climb from the belly of the duck back up to the evening peak, is the operational challenge that defines the modern California grid. Conventional generators must fire up roughly 13,000 megawatts of capacity within three hours as the sun sets and solar production collapses. That ramp is steeper, faster, and more consequential than anything grid operators managed a decade ago.

Battery storage is the technology California has deployed to tame the ramp, and the deployment numbers are staggering.

---

## The Battery Bet

California's grid-scale battery storage capacity has grown from roughly 250 megawatts in 2019 to approaching 16,900 megawatts by mid-2025, an increase of more than 6,600 percent. The state added 5,743 megawatts in 2024 alone, 69 percent more than the 3,394 megawatts added in 2023. No other jurisdiction in the Western Hemisphere has deployed storage at this pace.

The batteries are doing what they were built to do. They charge during the midday solar surplus and discharge over the evening peak, smoothing the duck curve and reducing the need for gas peakers. On April 16, 2024, battery storage became the single largest source of supply on the California grid during the evening peak for the first time, exceeding 6 gigawatts and surpassing natural gas, hydro, nuclear, and renewables for approximately two hours.

The reliability payoff arrived during the summer of 2024. Mid-June through July was the hottest 20-day stretch on record in Sacramento. Palm Springs hit 124 degrees Fahrenheit, an all-time record. CAISO issued zero Flex Alerts. No rolling blackouts. No emergency calls for conservation. The grid held. By contrast, in August 2020, a comparable heat event, though one that affected the entire Western United States simultaneously, had triggered rolling blackouts for roughly 813,000 customers over two days. The difference between 2020 and 2024 was not demand management or better forecasting. It was seven-fold growth in battery capacity, from roughly 1,500 megawatts to over 10,000 megawatts.

The battery story is not unblemished. The Moss Landing facility in Monterey County, operated by Vistra and once the largest battery installation in the country at 750 megawatts, suffered its third and most severe failure on January 16, 2025, when a fire damaged roughly 55 percent of the 100,000 lithium-ion cells in its 300-megawatt system. The fire followed overheating events in September 2021 and February 2022. Battery removal began in September 2025. The co-located 1,020-megawatt natural gas plant and PG&E's adjacent 182.5-megawatt Elkhorn battery system were also taken offline.

Moss Landing is a cautionary data point, not a disqualifying one. The rest of the fleet has performed as designed. But it is a reminder that deploying new technology at unprecedented speed carries risks that the aggregate capacity numbers do not capture.

## The Curtailment Problem

California is now generating more solar energy than its grid can absorb during midday hours in the spring, and the excess is being thrown away. In 2024, CAISO curtailed 3.4 million megawatt-hours of renewable energy, a 29 percent increase over 2023. Solar accounted for 93 percent of the curtailed energy. In the first four months of 2025 alone, 738,000 megawatt-hours were curtailed.

The trajectory is steep. Curtailment was roughly 1.6 million megawatt-hours in 2020. It reached 2.4 million in 2022 and 3.4 million in 2024. In May 2022, the peak curtailment month, 566 gigawatt-hours of solar generation, roughly 15 percent of the month's total solar output, was simply wasted.

Some of the surplus is traded through the Western Energy Imbalance Market, which allows California to export excess solar to neighboring states. In 2024, that trading avoided roughly 274,000 megawatt-hours of curtailment, about 8 percent of the total curtailed volume. But most of the excess has no buyer. The neighboring states have their own solar, and the transmission links to move California's midday surplus to the Mountain West or Pacific Northwest are insufficient.

Curtailment is the clearest sign that the grid has outgrown its infrastructure. California has more solar generation capacity than it can use during the hours of peak production, and it does not yet have enough storage or transmission to shift that energy to the hours when it is needed. The 3.4 million megawatt-hours curtailed in 2024 represent enough electricity to power roughly 500,000 homes for a year. It was generated, it was clean, and it was discarded.

## The Price of the Transition

California's average residential electricity rate has roughly doubled in a decade. In 2015, the average was approximately 17 cents per kilowatt-hour, about 30 percent above the national average. By late 2025, it had reached 34.7 cents, roughly double the national average of 17.5 cents. SDG&E customers pay the most at roughly 39.7 cents per kilowatt-hour. The Little Hoover Commission reported in October 2025 that one in five California households is behind on energy bills.

The rate increase is not primarily driven by the cost of renewable generation, which has fallen dramatically. It is driven by the cost of everything around the generation: transmission, distribution, wildfire mitigation, and infrastructure hardening. Generation accounts for roughly 30 percent of a typical utility bill. Transmission, distribution, and public purpose programs account for the other 70 percent.

Wildfire costs are the single largest recent driver. From 2019 through 2023, California's three large investor-owned utilities spent approximately $27 billion on wildfire mitigation, including $11 billion on insurance alone. Wildfire-related costs represented 10 to 24 percent of total utility revenue requirements between January 2023 and April 2024. Distribution revenue requirements more than doubled from 2016 to 2025, reflecting vegetation management, infrastructure undergrounding, and system hardening.

The price trajectory creates a genuine political problem for the energy transition. Californians are being asked to electrify their vehicles, their heating, and their cooking, to shift from gas to the grid, at a moment when the cost of grid electricity is rising faster than inflation, faster than incomes, and faster than the national average. Full decarbonization of California's economy by 2045 would require roughly 70 percent more electricity consumption than current levels. If rates do not decline, that electrification will be regressive: a clean energy mandate paid disproportionately by the households least able to afford it.

## The Rooftop Solar Collapse

Net Energy Metering 3.0, the rate structure that governs how rooftop solar owners are compensated for excess electricity they export to the grid, took effect in April 2023. Under the previous NEM 2.0 regime, solar homeowners received roughly 30 cents per kilowatt-hour for exported energy. Under NEM 3.0, export credits dropped to roughly 5 to 8 cents per kilowatt-hour, a reduction of 75 percent.

The market response was immediate. Interconnection applications fell 66 to 83 percent year-over-year in the first five months. Monthly installations dropped to roughly 8,000 in the first quarter of 2024, the lowest since May 2020. California's distributed solar market contracted 31 to 41 percent in megawatts installed in 2024. Thirty-two solar businesses closed in California in 2023 and 2024. Solar and storage companies cut approximately 17,000 jobs by the end of 2023, representing 22 percent of all solar industry employment in the state.

The CPUC's rationale was economic: under NEM 2.0, solar homeowners were being compensated at retail rates for electricity whose wholesale value was often near zero during midday hours, and the cost of that subsidy was being borne by non-solar ratepayers, disproportionately lower-income renters who could not install panels. The policy correction was defensible on equity grounds. Its execution cratered an industry.

The one bright spot in the NEM 3.0 data is battery attachment rates. Before the tariff change, roughly 11 percent of new residential solar installations included battery storage. By the end of 2024, nearly 70 percent did. NEM 3.0 made batteries essential for solar economics: if export credits are low, the rational move is to store midday production and use it during expensive evening hours rather than sell it back to the grid for a fraction of its value. The policy accidentally created the strongest incentive for distributed storage in the country.

## Diablo Canyon: The Reversal

In 2016, PG&E announced it would close Diablo Canyon, California's last operating nuclear plant, when its licenses expired in 2024 and 2025. Environmental groups celebrated. The closure was framed as the culmination of California's renewable energy vision: enough solar and wind to replace the atom.

Six years later, the state reversed course. SB 846, signed in September 2022, authorized extended operations through 2029 and 2030, backed by $1.4 billion in state forgivable loans and $1.1 billion in federal Department of Energy support. In April 2026, the NRC approved 20-year license renewals extending through 2044 and 2045. The state legislature must pass new legislation in 2026 to allow operations past 2030 under state law.

Diablo Canyon provides approximately 8.6 percent of California's total electricity and 17 percent of its zero-carbon generation. It runs 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, indifferent to clouds, wind, and sunset. In a grid defined by the duck curve's daily drama of solar surplus and evening deficit, nuclear's flat output profile is either an anachronism or a stabilizer, depending on whom you ask.

The cost debate is unresolved. The CPUC approved $1.1 billion in above-market costs for 2025-2026 operations. Critics project lifetime costs through 2045 of $20 to $45 billion. Supporters cite a 2021 MIT and Stanford analysis claiming $2.6 billion in savings from 2025 to 2035 compared to replacement resources. The data does not settle the argument. It frames it: how much is a gigawatt of around-the-clock clean power worth in a grid that produces too much energy at noon and not enough at 8 PM?

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's grid transition is an engineering success wrapped in an economic failure. The state has deployed solar, wind, and storage at a pace that no comparable economy has matched. It has cut electricity sector emissions by nearly 30 percent. It runs on clean energy for hours at a stretch. And it has delivered these results while doubling electricity rates, curtailing 3.4 million megawatt-hours of clean energy it spent money to build, collapsing its rooftop solar industry, and producing electricity bills that one in five households cannot pay. The grid works better than ever. The bills have never been worse. The transition is technically ahead of schedule and economically unsustainable.

The generous read is that every energy transition in history has been expensive in the middle. The coal-to-gas transition raised rates. Rural electrification required massive public subsidy. California is in the expensive middle of a transformation that will, on the other side, produce electricity from fuel that costs nothing. Solar and wind have no fuel cost. Storage costs are falling 15 to 20 percent per year. The rate increases are driven not by generation, which is getting cheaper, but by the one-time costs of infrastructure hardening, wildfire adaptation, and grid modernization, costs that will plateau and eventually amortize. The state that solves these problems first will have the cheapest electricity in the developed world. The question is whether California's ratepayers can afford to be the ones who pay for the learning curve.

Both readings are supported by the data. The generation mix is greener than any large grid in the hemisphere. The rates are among the highest. Neither fact cancels the other.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The CAISO and CEC generation data captures what the grid produces. It does not capture what ratepayers experience. A grid that is 62 percent clean provides the same electricity to the wall socket as a grid that is 100 percent gas. The environmental benefit is real but invisible at the point of consumption. The rate increase is visible on every monthly bill. The political economy of the transition is shaped by this asymmetry: the costs are felt individually, the benefits are distributed collectively.

The data also does not capture the behind-the-meter revolution with precision. California has roughly 20 gigawatts of distributed solar on rooftops and commercial buildings, generation that does not appear in CAISO's supply stack because it reduces demand rather than adding supply. The true solar penetration of California's electricity system is higher than the 21.3 percent figure suggests.

And the data says nothing about what the grid will need to look like in 2045. Full electrification of transportation and buildings would increase electricity consumption by roughly 70 percent. The grid that is straining to manage today's solar surplus and evening ramp will need to manage dramatically higher demand, higher renewable penetration, and longer-duration storage challenges that lithium-ion batteries are not designed to solve. The CEC estimates California needs 37 gigawatts of long-duration storage to fully replace its gas fleet. As of 2026, the state has permitted one 500-megawatt compressed air facility and funded one 1.5-megawatt iron-air demonstration project. The gap between aspiration and infrastructure is measured in decades.

---

California's grid is not the grid of 2015. It is something new: a system that runs on sunlight for most of the day, stores it in batteries for the evening, keeps a nuclear plant running as a backstop, maintains 40 gigawatts of gas capacity as insurance it hopes never to use, throws away enough clean energy to power half a million homes, charges the highest rates in the continental United States, and has not had a rolling blackout or a Flex Alert in three years. It is the most successful and most expensive clean energy deployment in the country, running simultaneously ahead of its clean energy targets and behind on affordability. The data shows a grid in the middle of a transformation that is working by every measure except the one that arrives in the mail.
