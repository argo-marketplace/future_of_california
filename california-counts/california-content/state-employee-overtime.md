# California Counts: The Overtime State
## What $600 to $800 million in annual CDCR overtime, correctional officers earning $200,000 or more in total compensation, a chronic staffing deficit that makes mandatory overtime the default rather than the exception, and a Legislative Analyst's Office that has been saying for years that this costs more than just hiring people reveal about how California actually staffs its most expensive facilities

There is a number that appears each year in the Transparent California salary database, which publishes the total compensation of every California state employee. The number belongs to a correctional officer, or sometimes several dozen correctional officers, and it exceeds $200,000. The base salary is $100,000 or more. The rest is overtime, sometimes $70,000 additional, sometimes $90,000, sometimes more. The overtime is not voluntary extra work. In many cases it is mandatory: the officer worked a scheduled shift, a colleague called in sick or did not show up, and the officer was legally required under the labor contract to stay and cover the second shift. This happens not occasionally but routinely, in a department that is chronically understaffed relative to the mandatory minimum staffing ratios that govern how many officers must be present in each housing unit at any given time. California's corrections system does not solve its staffing problem by hiring enough staff. It solves it, year after year, by paying the staff it has to work more hours than any shift schedule contemplates, at overtime pay rates that are substantially higher than regular compensation.

---

## The Numbers: $600 to $800 Million Per Year

The California Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation spent approximately $600 million to $800 million per year in overtime compensation in recent fiscal years, according to CDCR budget documents and Controller data. The range reflects year-to-year variation driven by staffing vacancy rates, the prison population mix, special operation demands, and the prevalence of sick leave and other absences that trigger mandatory overtime obligations. In fiscal years with higher vacancy rates or more intense operational demands, the overtime bill has approached the upper end of that range.

CDCR's overtime expenditure is by far the largest source of overtime in California state government. Total statewide state employee overtime, across all departments, runs approximately $1 billion to $1.5 billion per year. CDCR accounts for approximately 50 to 60 percent of that total. The Department of State Hospitals, which operates psychiatric facilities for the mentally ill who have been found incompetent to stand trial or not guilty by reason of insanity, runs the second-largest overtime bill in state government, approximately $200 million to $300 million per year, driven by many of the same structural factors: mandatory staffing ratios, a specialized workforce that cannot be quickly recruited, and chronic vacancies.

---

## Why It Happens: Mandatory Staffing, Chronic Vacancies

The overtime bill is not a management failure in the sense of a choice being made poorly. It is the predictable consequence of several structural features that interact to make mandatory overtime the system's equilibrating mechanism.

CDCR operates under mandatory minimum staffing ratios embedded in the CCPOA labor contract and state regulations. Each housing unit, each yard, and each specialized function requires a minimum number of correctional officers on duty at all times. If a scheduled officer does not report for a shift, the ratio must still be met. The contract and regulations require that the vacancy be filled, and the standard solution is to require an officer from the preceding or following shift to stay or come in early. This "mandatory overtime" is not optional: officers can be disciplined for refusing. The premium pay rate for overtime is time-and-a-half for the first two hours beyond an eight-hour shift and double time thereafter, under the CCPOA contract.

The chronic vacancy rate drives the frequency of mandatory overtime. CDCR's authorized correctional officer positions have numbered approximately 29,000 to 32,000 in recent years, but actual filled positions have consistently run below that, with vacancy rates of approximately 5 to 10 percent in recent years. A 10 percent vacancy rate across 29,000 authorized positions represents approximately 2,900 unfilled shifts that must be covered daily through some combination of overtime, transfers, or modified operations. The scale of mandatory overtime is a direct function of the gap between authorized positions and filled positions.

Why are positions unfilled? Recruitment is the primary explanation offered by CDCR management. Correctional officer training at the Richard A. McGee Correctional Training Center takes approximately four to six months. The applicant pipeline must be maintained, applicants must pass background checks and medical evaluations, and new officers must complete probationary periods before they count as fully operational. CDCR has cited particular recruitment challenges in rural areas, where many California prisons are located, because the qualified applicant pool in a rural county is smaller than in a metropolitan area. The starting salary, while high by national standards, may not attract candidates quickly enough in a competitive labor market to fill vacancies faster than they accumulate through retirements and attrition.

## The Cost Comparison: Overtime vs. Hiring

The Legislative Analyst's Office has made the arithmetic on this subject explicit in multiple reports over the past decade. A correctional officer earning $110,000 in base salary who works 20 percent overtime earns approximately $132,000 to $154,000, depending on the premium rate that applies. The fully loaded cost of that officer, including benefits, pension contributions at CDCR's CalPERS rate of approximately 40 to 50 percent of salary, and other overhead, is approximately $185,000 to $215,000 per year. The cost of hiring an additional correctional officer at the starting salary of approximately $74,000, plus benefits and overhead, would be approximately $105,000 to $120,000 per year fully loaded. In pure per-FTE terms, an additional hired officer costs less than the overtime premium paid to an existing officer working extended hours.

The LAO's analysis has consistently concluded that chronic reliance on overtime is more expensive than maintaining adequate staffing levels, and that CDCR should be investing more aggressively in recruitment and retention to reduce mandatory overtime. CDCR has generally agreed with this analysis in principle while pointing to the practical constraints: recruitment pipelines take time to build, training has a fixed duration, and the rural location of many prisons limits the applicant pool regardless of how aggressively the department recruits.

The counter-argument from CDCR is that fixed employment costs are permanent, while overtime reflects variable operational needs. Hiring 2,000 additional officers to eliminate overtime assumes that those officers will be needed indefinitely. But if the prison population continues to decline through Proposition 57 credits, AB 109 Realignment, or future sentencing reforms, CDCR would be left with a permanently larger workforce serving a smaller population, driving up the cost per incarcerated person further. From a budget planning perspective, overtime is flexible in a way that permanent hires are not. The LAO's response has been that this argument has been used to justify chronic understaffing for more than a decade, and that the flexibility argument has produced a decade of $600 to $800 million annual overtime bills that exceed the cost of adequate permanent staffing.

## What $200,000 Looks Like

Transparent California's annual data allows identification of individual state employees by department, job classification, and total compensation. In the CDCR data for 2024, the corrections workforce includes hundreds of employees with total compensation exceeding $150,000, and dozens with total compensation exceeding $200,000. These are not executives or managers. They are correctional officers, sergeants, and lieutenants whose base salary is in the $90,000 to $130,000 range and whose total compensation is elevated by substantial overtime hours.

An officer earning $110,000 in base salary who works mandatory overtime equivalent to an additional 30 to 40 percent of scheduled hours would earn approximately $143,000 to $154,000 in regular and overtime pay combined. Adding employer pension contributions, health benefits, and other overhead brings the fully loaded cost to the state to $200,000 to $220,000. This is the unit economics of California's correctional overtime model: each mandatory overtime hour is charged at time-and-a-half or double time, the pension contribution applies to the overtime-inflated base for CalPERS calculation purposes in some scenarios, and the compounding of premium rates across thousands of officers and thousands of overtime hours produces the aggregate $600 to $800 million figure.

The Department of State Hospitals overtime data shows a similar pattern. DSH employs psychiatric technicians, nurses, and peace officers who work in facilities housing individuals found not guilty by reason of insanity or incompetent to stand trial. Staffing ratios are mandated by both federal court oversight (in facilities subject to consent decrees) and state licensing requirements. Vacancies, particularly among nurses and psychiatrists who earn more in private healthcare than in state service, drive mandatory overtime for the licensed clinical staff who can legally perform certain functions.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has created a correctional system that structurally depends on mandatory overtime as its primary workforce management strategy, producing compensation levels that would be considered excessive at any regular salary scale and that impose a premium of $200 million to $400 million per year over what adequate permanent staffing would cost. The LAO has said this clearly and repeatedly. CDCR has agreed in principle and done insufficient. Meanwhile, individual officers working mandatory overtime to cover for unfilled vacancies are working second consecutive eight-hour shifts in environments that are psychologically demanding and physically dangerous. Mandatory overtime is not a neutral cost-saving measure; it is a fatigue-inducing requirement imposed on workers in high-stress jobs, which creates its own safety and performance risks. The state is paying premium prices for exhausted workers in chronically understaffed facilities. This is not a system that is working well by any measure: not fiscal efficiency, not worker welfare, not operational safety, and not the experience of the people incarcerated in those facilities.

The generous read is that correctional staffing is genuinely difficult in ways that simple hiring math does not capture. The locations of California's prisons, largely in rural Central Valley and Sierra Nevada communities, limit the applicant pool in ways that recruiting budgets cannot easily solve. The six-month training pipeline means that even an aggressive hiring push produces results slowly, and a hiring surge followed by a population decline (driven by sentencing reform, Proposition 36 reversals, or other policy changes) would leave the department overstaffed with permanent employees who have tenure protections. Mandatory overtime, while expensive, at least ensures that facilities are adequately staffed in the short term, which matters for the safety of the approximately 90,000 people currently incarcerated and the 28,000 officers working in those facilities. The officers receiving high overtime compensation are working those hours, not billing for hours they did not work. And the salary and overtime levels, while high by national standards, are calibrated to a California cost of living that is also high by national standards.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CDCR publishes personnel cost data in its budget documents and annual reports, but the public-facing documents do not consistently publish overtime expenditures by facility, shift, or classification in a way that would allow analysis of which prisons or housing units drive the most overtime. Transparent California provides individual-level data but not the aggregate vacancy rates, shift coverage rates, and mandatory overtime invocation rates that would allow external analysis of the operational patterns driving the aggregate cost. The Department of State Hospitals' overtime data is similarly reported at an aggregate level. A detailed operational audit of overtime patterns, which the LAO has periodically called for, would require facility-level data that has not been published in accessible form. The state knows where the overtime is being generated. That knowledge has not been fully shared.
