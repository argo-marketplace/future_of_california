# California Counts: The Citizens' Map
## What a 14-member citizens commission, 30,000 public comments, zero competitive congressional seats in 2001, and Proposition 11's bet that amateurs could draw fairer lines than politicians reveal about the redistricting experiment California launched and the nation watched

California's Citizens Redistricting Commission (CRC) is a 14-member body of registered voters (5 Democrats, 5 Republicans, 4 decline-to-state or minor party) selected through a multi-stage application and screening process to draw state legislative and congressional district boundaries every ten years following the census. The commission was created by Proposition 11 (2008) for state legislative districts and expanded to congressional districts by Proposition 20 (2010). Before the commission, district lines were drawn by the state legislature, a process that produced maps optimized for incumbent protection rather than competitive representation.

The 2001 redistricting, the last drawn by the legislature, produced zero competitive congressional seats in California: every one of the state's 53 districts was drawn with margins that made the incumbent effectively unbeatable. The 2001 map was a bipartisan gerrymander, an agreement between Democrats and Republicans to protect all incumbents of both parties. The result was a congressional delegation with no meaningful electoral accountability for a decade. Proposition 11 was the direct response: voters removed redistricting authority from the interested parties (legislators drawing their own districts) and gave it to a commission of citizens with no personal electoral stake.

---

## The Process

The CRC operates under ranked criteria specified in the California Constitution: equal population, compliance with the Voting Rights Act, geographic contiguity, respect for city and county boundaries, respect for communities of interest, compactness, and nesting (assembly districts should nest within senate districts). The commission is explicitly prohibited from considering incumbents' addresses, political party registration data, or the electoral effects of proposed maps. The process is conducted entirely in public: all meetings are open, all map drafts are published, and public input is solicited through hearings (the 2021 commission held 35 public hearings) and written testimony (approximately 30,000 public comments in 2021).

The commission's maps have been challenged in court after both the 2011 and 2021 cycles. In both cycles, the maps survived legal challenge. The 2011 maps produced approximately 10 to 12 competitive congressional districts (compared to zero under the 2001 legislative map). The 2021 maps produced similar levels of competitiveness, though the exact number depends on the threshold used to define "competitive." Independent analyses by FiveThirtyEight, the Princeton Gerrymandering Project, and others have consistently rated California's post-CRC maps among the fairest in the nation by measures of partisan proportionality, competitiveness, and geographic compactness.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that the Citizens Redistricting Commission is a structural reform that works as designed but has not produced the transformative political change its proponents promised. Competitive districts have not made California politics more moderate (the legislature has moved steadily leftward since 2011). Turnover has not increased dramatically (most incumbents still win). The commission's amateur nature means that its members lack the technical expertise to fully evaluate the maps' implications, relying heavily on hired demographers and legal counsel who operate with less public visibility than the commissioners themselves. And the prohibition on considering partisan data means the commission cannot ensure proportional representation, which some reformers consider the actual goal of fair redistricting. A commission blind to party data can produce a proportional map by accident or a disproportional one by accident.

The generous read is that the commission restored legitimacy to a process that had none. When legislators draw their own districts, the conflict of interest is absolute and the outcome is predetermined: safe seats for all. The CRC produces maps that respect communities, comply with the Voting Rights Act, and treat partisan outcomes as irrelevant to the line-drawing exercise. Whether the maps produce "better" politics is the wrong metric; the correct metric is whether the process is fair, transparent, and free from self-dealing. By that measure, the CRC is among the most successful direct-democracy reforms in California history. The model has been studied and partially adopted by Arizona, Michigan, Colorado, and other states seeking to remove redistricting from legislative self-interest.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The CRC publishes its final maps, meeting transcripts, public comments, and census data used in the process. It does not publish the partisan performance data of its maps (because it is prohibited from considering that data), which means that independent analysts must evaluate partisan fairness after the fact using election results. The question of whether the CRC maps are more or less proportional than the maps the legislature would have drawn is counterfactual and unanswerable with certainty.
