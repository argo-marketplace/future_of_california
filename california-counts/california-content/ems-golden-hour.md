# California Counts: The Golden Hour
## What 4 million 911 calls per year, 33 local EMS agencies, a 20 percent paramedic vacancy rate, and a 45-minute rural response time reveal about the emergency medical system California funds like a charity and runs like a utility

California's emergency medical services system responds to approximately 4 million 911 medical calls per year, transporting approximately 2.5 million patients by ambulance to emergency departments. The system is operated by 33 local emergency medical services agencies (LEMSAs) that oversee a patchwork of approximately 250 ambulance providers: private companies (American Medical Response, Falck), fire department-based EMS (Los Angeles Fire Department, San Diego Fire-Rescue), and hospital-based services. The organizational structure varies by county: in some counties, fire departments provide paramedic-level first response and private companies provide ambulance transport. In others, fire departments do both. In rural counties, a single ambulance may cover a service area larger than some states.

The funding model is unlike any other essential service. Fire departments are tax-funded. Police are tax-funded. EMS ambulance transport is funded primarily through insurance reimbursement: the ambulance company bills the patient's insurance (or Medi-Cal, or Medicare) for the transport. Medi-Cal reimburses approximately $125 to $175 per ground ambulance transport, an amount that covers roughly 30 to 40 percent of the cost of providing the service. Medicare reimburses approximately $450 to $550. Private insurance reimburses $800 to $2,000. The reimbursement disparity means that ambulance companies lose money on every Medi-Cal patient they transport, a loss that is cross-subsidized by higher payments from private insurance, tax subsidies from local governments, and in rural areas, special district parcel taxes.

## The Rural Crisis

The EMS access disparity between urban and rural California is among the most severe of any public service. In Los Angeles, median ambulance response time (from 911 call to arrival at the patient) is approximately 8 to 10 minutes. In rural counties, median response times of 30 to 45 minutes are common, and in the most remote areas (parts of Modoc, Siskiyou, Trinity, Inyo, and Mono counties), response times can exceed one hour. The "golden hour" concept in trauma medicine, the principle that a critically injured patient's survival probability declines dramatically after 60 minutes without definitive care, means that a trauma victim in rural California may die from an injury that would be survivable in an urban setting solely because of the distance to care.

The rural EMS workforce shortage is acute. Paramedic vacancy rates in rural California exceed 20 percent, driven by compensation (rural paramedics earn $45,000 to $55,000, well below urban counterparts at $60,000 to $85,000), working conditions (24-to-48-hour shifts, single-crew coverage requiring response to every call), and career development (limited advancement opportunities in small agencies). Multiple rural ambulance providers have reduced service levels or closed in recent years, transferring coverage responsibility to neighboring agencies or to fire departments that provide basic life support but not advanced paramedic care.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California treats emergency medical services as a business rather than a public service, funding it through insurance reimbursement rather than taxation, and the result is a system that works well in affluent urban areas (where the payer mix includes private insurance) and fails in poor and rural areas (where the payer mix is dominated by Medi-Cal). The golden hour is a geographic lottery: a heart attack victim in San Francisco receives paramedic care in 8 minutes, and a heart attack victim in Modoc County waits 45 minutes, not because of clinical differences but because the funding model does not sustain ambulance service in areas where the revenue per transport is insufficient to cover costs. EMS is the only first-responder service in California that is expected to fund itself from fee revenue. The fire department does not bill for its services. The police department does not bill. The ambulance does.

The generous read is that California's EMS system transports 2.5 million patients per year and operates one of the most sophisticated paramedic and trauma care networks in the nation. The state's trauma center system (13 Level I, 26 Level II) provides definitive surgical care within the golden hour for the vast majority of the population. The air ambulance system (helicopter EMS) extends advanced care to rural areas that ground ambulances cannot reach in time. AB 1544 (2023) and subsequent legislation have increased Medi-Cal ambulance reimbursement rates and created grant programs for rural EMS sustainability. The system is imperfect. Its performance in urban settings, where the majority of Californians live, is among the best in the nation.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Emergency Medical Services Authority (EMSA) publishes limited statewide EMS data. California does not have a comprehensive, publicly accessible database of ambulance response times, patient outcomes, or transport volumes by provider and jurisdiction. The National Emergency Medical Services Information System (NEMSIS) collects EMS data nationally, but California's participation is incomplete. The fundamental performance question, whether EMS response times in California are improving, declining, or stable, and how they compare across jurisdictions, cannot be answered from publicly available state data.
