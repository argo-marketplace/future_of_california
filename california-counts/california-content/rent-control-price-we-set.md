# California Counts: The Price We Set
## What a 10 percent annual cap on rent increases, 17 million renters, the Costa-Hawkins Rental Housing Act of 1995, and a 54 percent rent-burdened population reveal about the rent regulation California imposed statewide after decades of local experimentation

California enacted the Tenant Protection Act of 2019 (AB 1482), establishing the state's first statewide rent cap. The law limits annual rent increases to 5 percent plus local Consumer Price Index (with a maximum of 10 percent total), and provides just-cause eviction protections for tenants who have occupied a unit for 12 months or more. AB 1482 applies to most residential rental units built more than 15 years ago that are not already subject to local rent control. Approximately 8 million rental units statewide fall under the law's coverage. The law is scheduled to sunset in 2030.

Prior to AB 1482, rent regulation in California operated exclusively at the local level. Approximately 25 California cities (including Los Angeles, San Francisco, Oakland, Berkeley, San Jose, and West Hollywood) have local rent control ordinances, covering approximately 1.5 million units. These local ordinances typically limit annual increases to 60 to 100 percent of CPI (3 to 7 percent in recent years). The Costa-Hawkins Rental Housing Act of 1995 constrains local rent control by prohibiting cities from applying rent control to single-family homes, condominiums, and units built after the city's rent control adoption date (1995 for cities adopting after that year). Costa-Hawkins also guarantees vacancy decontrol: when a tenant moves out, the landlord can reset the rent to market rate regardless of the local ordinance.

## The Rent Burden

California has approximately 17 million renters (approximately 44 percent of households rent). Approximately 54 percent of California renters are rent-burdened, paying more than 30 percent of household income on rent. Approximately 28 percent are severely rent-burdened, paying more than 50 percent of income on rent. The median rent in California is approximately $2,000 per month statewide, with significant variation: approximately $3,000 in San Francisco, $2,500 in Los Angeles, $2,200 in San Diego, and $1,400 to $1,800 in inland regions.

AB 1482's 10 percent cap (5 percent plus CPI, maximum 10 percent) is a weak form of rent stabilization by national and international standards. Strong rent control (as in New York City or Berlin) limits increases to well below inflation and restricts vacancy decontrol. AB 1482 allows increases that substantially exceed wage growth in most years and permits full market-rate rent resets on vacancy. The law functions less as rent control in the traditional sense and more as anti-gouging protection: preventing extreme single-year increases (20, 30, 50 percent) while permitting steady escalation at rates that can compound to 50 percent or more over five years.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that AB 1482 is rent control in name only. A 10 percent maximum annual increase in a state where median household income grows at 3 to 4 percent per year means that rents can legally outpace incomes indefinitely. Vacancy decontrol under Costa-Hawkins means that the cap constrains existing tenants but not the market: when tenants are eventually displaced (by a qualifying just-cause reason or by choosing to move), the unit resets to market rate. The law creates an incentive for landlords to encourage turnover. It does not create affordable housing, does not prevent displacement over time, and does not address the fundamental supply shortage that drives California rents. The rent cap is a political signal that the state cares about tenants, delivered through a mechanism too weak to materially alter their housing cost trajectory.

The generous read is that AB 1482 established a statewide floor of tenant protection that did not previously exist, covering 8 million units with both rent caps and just-cause eviction protections. Before 2019, a landlord in an uncontrolled city could double the rent with 60 days' notice or evict without cause with 60 days' notice. The just-cause eviction protection may matter more than the rent cap: it gives tenants the security to invest in their communities, enroll their children in schools, and plan their lives without the threat of arbitrary displacement. Research on rent stabilization consistently finds that stability (remaining in one's home) is as important as affordability (paying less) for tenant well-being. AB 1482 provides stability. The affordability problem requires building more housing.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Department of Housing and Community Development does not collect or publish data on actual rent increases charged across the state, the frequency of increases at or near the AB 1482 cap, vacancy rates and turnover rates in controlled versus uncontrolled units, or the displacement rates of tenants in units subject to the law. Whether AB 1482 has reduced displacement, moderated rent increases, or altered landlord behavior is not measurable from publicly available state data.
