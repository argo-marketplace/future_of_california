# California Counts: Who Actually Sues Under CEQA
## What 20 years of lawsuit data reveal about California's most powerful land use law

The California Environmental Quality Act was signed by Ronald Reagan in 1970 to protect the state's environment from unreviewed development. Fifty-six years later, it is the single most litigated land use statute in the country, the subject of an average of 192 lawsuits per year since 2002, and the instrument by which a neighborhood group in Berkeley nearly froze enrollment at a 45,000-student university because residents were an environmental impact. The law's text is about the environment. The lawsuit data tells a different story. According to Holland and Knight's analysis of every CEQA lawsuit filed between 2010 and 2012, only 13 percent of CEQA petitioners are recognized state or national environmental advocacy organizations. Sixty-four percent are individuals or local associations with no prior track record of environmental advocacy. The remainder are government agencies, private companies, labor unions, and tribes. CEQA is an environmental law that is used, most of the time, for purposes that have nothing to do with the environment. The litigation data is the proof.

---

## The Litigation Rate: Small Denominator, Large Impact

Roughly 18,000 projects per year undergo some form of CEQA review in California. Of those, approximately 500 require a full Environmental Impact Report, the most intensive and expensive tier. The rest are approved via Mitigated Negative Declarations, Negative Declarations, or categorical exemptions that require little or no review. The vast majority of projects sail through.

The litigation rate is low. Between 2013 and 2021, only 1.9 percent of CEQA-reviewed projects were challenged in court. In an earlier analysis of 2013-2015, the rate was 0.7 percent: fewer than 100 housing projects per year out of thousands reviewed.

The Rose Foundation, which has published the most comprehensive counterargument to CEQA reform advocates, cites this low rate as evidence that CEQA is not the obstacle its critics claim. The number is accurate. But it misses the asymmetry. CEQA lawsuits do not target projects at random. They disproportionately target the largest, densest, most consequential housing and infrastructure projects. In 2020, CEQA lawsuits sought to block approximately 48,000 approved housing units, nearly 50 percent of California's total annual housing production, concentrated in fewer than 200 lawsuits. Over the 2019-2021 study period, CEQA challenges targeted agency housing plans covering more than one million new units. A litigation rate of 1.9 percent that captures half the state's housing pipeline is not a rounding error. It is a chokepoint.

---

## The Plaintiff Breakdown: 13 Percent Environmental

The most important finding in the CEQA litigation data is who files the lawsuits. Holland and Knight's analysis, led by attorney Jennifer Hernandez, examined every CEQA lawsuit filed between 2010 and 2012 using Attorney General records. The breakdown:

| Plaintiff category | Share of CEQA lawsuits |
|---|---|
| Local associations or organizations with no environmental track record | 45% |
| Individuals or families | 19% |
| Recognized environmental advocacy organizations | 13% |
| Government agencies | 11% |
| Private companies, trade associations, or business competitors | 8% |
| Labor unions | 2% |
| Tribes | 2% |

Nearly two-thirds of all CEQA petitioners are individuals or neighborhood groups that have never engaged in environmental advocacy before filing their lawsuit and, in most cases, never do again after. The environmental review process created by CEQA has become the legal vehicle of choice for objectives that are economic, competitive, aesthetic, or exclusionary, but not environmental.

The pattern is consistent across the three study periods (2010-2012, 2013-2015, 2019-2021). Housing is the single most frequently targeted category of private-sector projects, accounting for 21 percent of lawsuits in the early studies and rising to 60 percent of all CEQA lawsuits against construction projects by 2018. Of the housing projects targeted, 45 percent are affordable or multifamily developments: apartments, condominiums, and mixed-use projects. Eighty percent are infill projects in existing communities. One hundred percent of Bay Area housing CEQA lawsuits in the study period targeted infill locations.

The data describes a law that was written to protect open space and is used, in practice, to prevent density.

---

## The Geography of Exclusion

CEQA litigation is not distributed evenly across the state. The Bay Area and Los Angeles account for 58 percent of all CEQA lawsuits. Adding San Diego brings the share to 65 percent. The concentration tracks the state's highest-cost housing markets, the places where new supply is most desperately needed and most fiercely resisted.

The environmental justice dimension of this geography is stark. In the six-county SCAG region of Southern California, 78 percent of housing units challenged by CEQA lawsuits were located in whiter, wealthier, and healthier areas. The top 10 percent most environmentally burdened communities in California are 90 percent people of color. But those communities rarely file CEQA lawsuits, because filing a CEQA lawsuit requires money, attorneys, and time that low-income communities do not have.

The structural effect is that CEQA reinforces the land use patterns it inherited. Neighborhoods that were historically exclusionary through restrictive covenants and discriminatory zoning now use CEQA to maintain that exclusion, framing the arrival of new residents, particularly in multifamily housing, as an environmental impact. The existing built environment, however segregated its origins, becomes the baseline against which any change is measured. More people in a neighborhood is an impact. More traffic is an impact. More noise is an impact. The law does not ask whether the baseline was just. It asks whether the project would change it.

## The Abuse Cases: A Taxonomy

The case law contains a taxonomy of non-environmental uses of CEQA that reads like a catalog of bad faith.

A gas station owner in San Jose sued a competing gas station under CEQA when it added fuel pump islands, claiming increased traffic. A property developer in Orange County used CEQA to prevent a competitor from building a 300-unit apartment complex adjacent to the developer's own buildings. A landlord near USC who owned 17 rental buildings filed serial CEQA lawsuits against a competitor's 1,600-unit housing development, then sued the competitor's other projects as well.

In Hollywood, a developer paid $5.5 million to settle meritless CEQA lawsuits challenging two infill hotel projects. The plaintiff then immediately sued to block a third project. The developer brought a federal RICO suit. The Ninth Circuit ruled in September 2024 that CEQA abuse is not actionable under federal racketeering law.

In Rancho Mirage, a group called Save Rancho Mirage sued to block an In-N-Out Burger restaurant under CEQA, claiming drive-through traffic, noise, and emissions. In-N-Out withdrew. The city paid $60,000 in legal fees. In-N-Out returned, won approval again, and opened in 2022.

In Sacramento, local businesses sued to block 200 tiny homes for the unhoused under a freeway overpass, claiming the city had not assessed the "environmental impact or health implications" of sheltering homeless people.

The case that crystallized the problem involved 43 acres in Tiburon, Marin County. A 43-unit subdivision on a 110-acre parcel faced nearly five decades of NIMBY opposition. Federal settlement agreements in 1976 and 2007 had guaranteed the developer could build. Neighbors kept filing CEQA suits. In 2022, the Court of Appeal issued a 108-page opinion upholding the project, writing that "something is very wrong with this picture" if CEQA can be "subverted into an instrument for the oppression and delay of projects."

And then there was UC Berkeley. Save Berkeley's Neighborhoods, a group of Berkeley homeowners, sued the university under CEQA, arguing that enrollment growth had created unacceptable noise. A court froze enrollment at 2020-2021 levels, threatening the admission of 3,000 incoming students. The Legislature passed emergency legislation. The case went to the California Supreme Court. In June 2024, the court reversed the lower courts and the Legislature passed AB 1307 to clarify that the social noise of people living in buildings is not an environmental impact. The fact that a state law was required to establish this proposition tells you everything about the distance between CEQA's text and its application.

## The Cost of the Process

A full Environmental Impact Report costs $300,000 to $400,000 and takes six to twelve months, though complex projects can run into the millions and take years. A 117-mile Southern California powerline project required an 11,000-page EIR evaluating more than 100 alternatives, a process that took five years and required 70 permits from more than 24 agencies.

CEQA litigation adds another two to five years and costs millions more. In the state's ten largest cities, CEQA appeals delayed projects by an average of 2.5 years, according to the Legislative Analyst's Office. Petitioners in CEQA cases win roughly 44 percent of the time over a 15-year sample of appellate decisions, far higher than the national 20 percent success rate for environmental lawsuits. Most victories are on procedural grounds: an alternatives analysis was too narrow, the traffic data was outdated, the mitigation measures were insufficiently specific. The Rose Foundation found no evidence of any project being permanently scrapped as a direct result of CEQA litigation. Delay, not cancellation, is the weapon. But in housing development, delay is cancellation by another name. Interest rates compound, construction costs escalate, equity partners withdraw, and the project that was feasible three years ago is no longer feasible today.

The costs are not borne equally. A well-capitalized developer building a luxury condominium tower can absorb CEQA litigation costs. A nonprofit affordable housing developer operating on 4 percent margins cannot. The law's procedural burden falls hardest on the projects that serve the people who need housing most.

## The Reform: 55 Years and Counting

CEQA reform has been the great third rail of California politics for decades. Governor Jerry Brown advocated reform across 16 years in office and achieved virtually nothing. The Legislature carved out project-specific exemptions for politically connected developments, the Sacramento Kings arena, a proposed Los Angeles football stadium, while leaving the underlying statute untouched. The pattern was consistent: individual projects with powerful sponsors got bespoke exemptions while the system that produced the problem remained in place.

The first meaningful structural reform was SB 35, signed in 2017, which created streamlined ministerial approval for housing in jurisdictions failing to meet their housing goals. Between 2018 and 2021, approximately 156 projects comprising more than 18,000 housing units were approved under SB 35, nearly three-quarters of them affordable. The law worked by removing discretionary review entirely, which removed CEQA as a factor since CEQA applies only to discretionary actions.

Then, in June 2025, the dam broke. Governor Newsom signed AB 130 and SB 131, described as the broadest CEQA reform in the law's 55-year history. AB 130 created an absolute statutory exemption for infill housing on sites up to 20 acres that meet local zoning and planning standards, with no affordability requirement and no prevailing wage requirement for most projects. SB 131 created 10 new categorical CEQA exemptions covering housing, childcare centers, clean water infrastructure, climate adaptation, broadband, health clinics, and more. AB 130 passed the Assembly 67-0 and the Senate 28-5. SB 131 passed the Assembly 50-3 and the Senate 33-1. The votes were not close.

A 2026 ballot initiative, the "Building an Affordable California Act," backed by the California Chamber of Commerce with a $50 to $100 million campaign budget, would go further, establishing binding 365-day timelines for CEQA reviews and 270-day timelines for CEQA lawsuits. It has collected nearly one million signatures and is likely to appear on the November 2026 ballot.

The Little Hoover Commission published its first CEQA review in 30 years in May 2024, recommending NEPA-style standing requirements, restrictions on late comment submissions, and specialized CEQA courts. The political consensus that made CEQA untouchable for decades appears to be dissolving.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that CEQA has become California's most powerful tool for preventing housing, blocking infrastructure, and preserving the exclusionary land use patterns the state claims to be dismantling. The litigation data is unambiguous: 87 percent of plaintiffs are not environmental organizations. The law is used to block homeless shelters, drive-through restaurants, student housing, infill apartments, and clean energy projects. It reinforces racial and economic segregation by allowing wealthy white neighborhoods to litigate against density while environmental justice communities lack the resources to use the same law to protect their own health. The 2025 reforms are real but narrow, exempting specific categories while leaving the underlying litigation framework intact. As long as any person or entity can file a CEQA lawsuit against any discretionary project and impose two to five years of delay at no cost to the petitioner, the law will continue to function as a subsidy for obstruction.

The generous read is that CEQA has prevented genuine environmental harm for 55 years and that the litigation data, while real, is not the whole story. The 98 percent of projects that are never sued move through CEQA review and emerge with enforceable mitigation measures that reduce air pollution, traffic, water contamination, and habitat destruction. The EIR process forces project sponsors to study their impacts, consider alternatives, and design mitigation before breaking ground. The fact that 44 percent of CEQA challenges succeed suggests that agencies are, in fact, approving projects with inadequate environmental review. CEQA is the primary mechanism by which California communities can participate in decisions about what gets built next to them, and participation is not the same as obstruction. The 2025 reforms and the 2026 ballot initiative are evidence that the political system is capable of adjusting the balance. The question is whether it can do so without losing the environmental protections that CEQA was built to provide.

Both readings rest on the same dataset. CEQA is simultaneously California's most important environmental protection law and its most effective anti-housing law. The data cannot tell you which function matters more. That is a political question the litigation data frames but does not answer.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Holland and Knight studies and the Rose Foundation studies use the same underlying Attorney General data and reach opposite conclusions about its significance. The Hernandez studies, funded by the pro-development Center for Jobs and the Economy, emphasize the scale of what is targeted: 48,000 housing units challenged in a single year. The Rose Foundation studies, funded by environmental organizations, emphasize the rate: 1.9 percent of projects litigated. Both numbers are correct. Neither is complete. A thorough accounting would require data that does not exist: how many projects were never proposed because the developer anticipated CEQA litigation, how many were redesigned to avoid triggering review, how many were moved to other states. The chilling effect of a law is invisible in the litigation statistics. It lives in the projects that were never filed.

The data also does not capture the settlements. Many CEQA lawsuits end in private cash payments from developer to petitioner, agreements that are not recorded in court filings and not reported to any public agency. The Hollywood hotel developer paid $5.5 million in settlements. How many other developers have paid how much to make CEQA claims go away is a number that no one tracks.

And the data says nothing about what the environment would look like without CEQA. California's air is cleaner, its water is less polluted, and its sensitive habitats are more protected than they were in 1970. How much of that improvement is attributable to CEQA versus the Clean Air Act, the Clean Water Act, the Endangered Species Act, and the state's own air quality and water quality regulations is a question the litigation data cannot isolate. CEQA operates in a regulatory ecosystem. Its contribution is real but unmeasurable in isolation.

---

Only 15 states have environmental review laws comparable to CEQA. Only three, California, Minnesota, and New York, require environmental review of private actions. Only California has built an entire litigation industry around the process. The law that Ronald Reagan signed to protect California's environment has become the law that prevents California from building its way out of a housing crisis, a climate crisis, and an infrastructure deficit, while environmental justice communities bear the burden of both the pollution the law was supposed to prevent and the housing shortage the law now perpetuates. The 2025 reforms acknowledge the problem. The 2026 ballot initiative would accelerate the fix. Whether either is sufficient depends on whether the political consensus holds, and whether 192 lawsuits per year remain the price California is willing to pay for the right of any resident to challenge any project for any reason, so long as the paperwork says "environment."
