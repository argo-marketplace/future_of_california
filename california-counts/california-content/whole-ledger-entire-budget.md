# California Counts: The Whole Ledger
## What $322 billion a year, $882 million a day, $8,250 per resident, 175 years of budget history from a $362,000 frontier government to the fourth-largest economy on Earth, a tax system balanced on the portfolios of 160,000 households, and one ordinary Tuesday of state spending reveal about the machine this series has spent 157 installments taking apart one gear at a time

This series has spent 157 installments examining California's government one piece at a time: a department here, a program there, a fund, a backlog, a caseload. This installment is the opposite exercise. Pull the camera back as far as it will go and look at the entire ledger at once. The State of California will spend approximately $322 billion in state funds in fiscal year 2025-26, comprising a General Fund of approximately $226 billion, special funds of approximately $80 billion, and bond funds making up the remainder. Add the federal money that flows through Sacramento and the total approaches $500 billion. The state that manages this ledger has 39 million residents, a $4.1 trillion economy that surpassed Japan in 2025 to become the fourth largest on Earth, and a budget larger than the national budgets of all but a handful of sovereign countries.

The numbers are too large to mean anything at annual scale. So this installment divides them three ways: by history, by day, and by resident.

---

## One Hundred Seventy-Five Years of Ledger

California's first state budget, adopted in 1850 when the non-native population was roughly 92,000 and the capital had not yet settled in Sacramento, appropriated approximately $362,000. The state now spends that amount roughly every 35 seconds. The table below traces the approximate size of the state budget across 175 years. Early figures are total appropriations; figures from 1970 onward are General Fund. All figures are nominal, which understates early budgets and overstates recent growth, but the shape of the curve survives any deflator.

| Year | Approximate Budget | Population | Context |
|------|-------------------|------------|---------|
| 1850 | $362,000 | ~92,000 | Statehood; gold rush government |
| 1900 | $10 million | 1.5 million | Progressive Era beginnings |
| 1930 | $120 million | 5.7 million | Highways, water, Depression onset |
| 1950 | $1 billion | 10.6 million | Postwar boom; budget crosses $1 billion |
| 1960 | $2.5 billion | 15.7 million | Pat Brown; Master Plan; State Water Project |
| 1970 | $6.5 billion | 20.0 million | Reagan governorship |
| 1980 | $21 billion | 23.7 million | Two years after Proposition 13 |
| 1990 | $51 billion | 29.8 million | Pre-recession peak |
| 2000 | $81 billion | 33.9 million | Dot-com surplus |
| 2010 | $87 billion | 37.3 million | Great Recession trough |
| 2020 | $147 billion | 39.5 million | Pre-pandemic peak |
| 2026 | $226 billion | 39.1 million | Post-COVID plateau |

Two things stand out. First, the budget roughly doubled every decade for a century and a half, through gold rushes, depressions, tax revolts, and recalls, with only two sustained interruptions: the decade after Proposition 13 in 1978 and the decade after the Great Recession. Second, the population stopped growing around 2020 (the state lost residents for the first time in its history between 2020 and 2023) while the budget grew approximately 54 percent over the same period. For 170 years, budget growth could be partially explained by more Californians. The last five years are the first period in state history where the ledger grew this fast while the population did not grow at all.

## One Day of California

Divide $322 billion by 365 and the State of California spends approximately $882 million every day, including weekends, holidays, and the days when the legislature is out of session. Here is what one ordinary Tuesday costs, using the major budget categories prorated to a single day.

| Category | Annual (State Funds) | Per Day |
|----------|---------------------|---------|
| K-14 education (Proposition 98) | ~$115 billion | ~$315 million |
| Health and human services | ~$84 billion | ~$230 million |
| Higher education (UC, CSU, aid) | ~$21 billion | ~$58 million |
| Corrections and rehabilitation | ~$14 billion | ~$38 million |
| Transportation (special funds) | ~$18 billion | ~$49 million |
| Debt service on bonds | ~$8 billion | ~$22 million |
| Everything else | ~$62 billion | ~$170 million |
| Total | ~$322 billion | ~$882 million |

By the time a Sacramento state worker finishes a morning coffee at 9:15 a.m., the state has spent roughly $340 million since midnight. The school day alone costs approximately $315 million, which works out to roughly $54 for each of the state's 5.8 million public school students, every day, whether or not the student shows up (and as installment #74 noted, increasingly the student does not show up, since chronic absenteeism still hovers near 25 percent). The state spends more per day servicing old bond debt (~$22 million) than it spends per day running the entire Legislature, Governor's Office, and judicial branch combined (~$17 million). One day of CDCR (~$38 million) costs more than the annual budgets of several state departments this series has covered in full installments.

## One Resident's Share

Divide $322 billion by 39.1 million residents and the state spends approximately $8,250 per Californian per year, or approximately $22.60 per resident per day. That figure is the state ledger only. Add the resident's share of federal pass-through spending and it approaches $12,800. Add city, county, school district, and special district spending (the 58 republics and 3,000-plus special districts of installments #87 and #12) and total government spending per Californian lands somewhere between $22,000 and $26,000 per year depending on the county, a range wide enough to deserve its own future installment.

But the per-resident figure hides the ledger's most extreme feature: who pays in. The personal income tax supplies approximately 60 percent of the General Fund, roughly $137 billion. The top 1 percent of filers, approximately 160,000 households out of 18 million returns, pay approximately 45 to 50 percent of that tax, meaning that something like $65 billion of the General Fund (enough to fund CDCR, the UC, the CSU, CalFire, the courts, and the entire state parks system combined, twice) depends on the annual investment returns, stock option exercises, and business sales of a group of people who would fit inside Dodger Stadium three times. Installment #39 named this the One Percent Budget. At full-ledger scale the consequence is sharper: the difference between a good year and a bad year in capital gains realizations (which have swung from under $80 billion to over $200 billion in realized gains within three years) moves the General Fund by $15 to $25 billion, which is larger than the entire budget of 20 other US states.

## The Ledger Among Nations

California's economy passed Japan's in 2025 to become the fourth largest in the world, behind only the United States, China, and Germany. The state budget invites the same comparison. At approximately $322 billion in state funds, California's budget exceeds the national government budgets of Sweden, Belgium, Poland, and roughly 170 other sovereign countries. It is comparable to the federal budget of Canada, a G7 nation with a comparable population. Among US states, no one else is close: Texas, with 78 percent of California's population, budgets roughly half as much; New York, the nearest per-capita rival, spends comparably per resident but on 20 million fewer people.

The comparison cuts both ways, and this series has spent 157 installments documenting how. A government with the budget of a G7 nation operates a high-speed rail project that has consumed 17 years without connecting two major cities (#17), an unemployment IT system that failed catastrophically when it was needed most (#38), a homelessness response that spent $24 billion while the unsheltered count rose (#18), and a DMV that needed the National Guard to process paperwork (#144). The same government operates the largest public university system in the world, the largest Medicaid program in the nation covering one in three residents (#20), the most aggressive climate program of any major economy (#10), and a food and agriculture engine that feeds the country (#51). The whole ledger contains all of it simultaneously. That is the point of looking at the whole ledger.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that the whole ledger describes a government that has decoupled spending from results at civilizational scale. The budget grew 54 percent in five years while the population fell, school enrollment declined, and residents' most-cited complaints (housing costs, homelessness, insurance, infrastructure) worsened. A budget that doubles every decade for 175 years is not evidence of ambition; it is evidence that the ledger only knows one direction, and that the political system that manages it treats each year's spending as the floor for the next. The revenue architecture compounds the problem: by balancing half the income tax on 160,000 households' investment income, the state has built a ledger that surges in bull markets (inviting permanent commitments) and craters in corrections (forcing cuts to the poor, as the SSP freeze of installment #149 showed), a boom-bust machine that Sacramento has studied, documented, lamented, and preserved for 25 consecutive years.

The generous read is that the whole ledger is what it costs to run the most ambitious public enterprise in the democratic world, and that the raw growth numbers mislead without context. Nominal budget growth over 175 years mostly tracks the compounding of population, prices, and wealth in what became the world's fourth-largest economy; measured as a share of state personal income, state spending has fluctuated within a relatively narrow band for five decades rather than exploding. The state absorbed 30 million new residents, built the water, university, and highway systems that made the economy possible, and now finances the nation's largest safety net while running budget reserves (installment #91) that did not exist a generation ago. The concentration of the tax burden on the wealthy is not a design flaw; it is the deliberate and repeatedly ratified choice of the electorate to fund a broad state with a progressive tax, and the volatility is the accepted price. Countries with California's GDP run governments twice this size relative to their economies. By international standards, the remarkable fact about the whole ledger is not how large it is but how small.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

Every figure in this installment is an approximation assembled from budget summaries, and the approximations compound when they are divided and compared. Historical budget figures are nominal and the early ones are not strictly comparable to modern General Fund accounting; the 1850 figure comes from state archival summaries, not an audited ledger. The per-day and per-resident divisions are arithmetic conveniences, not incidence analysis: the state does not actually spend $22.60 on each resident each day, and who receives the spending is as concentrated as who supplies the revenue. Most fundamentally, the whole ledger records inputs at the highest possible altitude, which is the altitude at which outputs are least visible. Whether $322 billion is buying a functioning state is not answerable from the ledger. It is only answerable one gear at a time, which is why this series exists.
