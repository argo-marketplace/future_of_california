# California Counts: The Governments You Didn't Know You Had
## What 3,300 special districts, 17,000 board seats, and $38 billion in annual spending reveal about California's hidden layer of government

You are governed by more governments than you think. If you live in an unincorporated part of a California county near the coast, you may be subject to the jurisdiction of the county, a water district, a fire protection district, a mosquito abatement district, a resource conservation district, a healthcare district, a cemetery district, a community services district, a flood control district, and possibly a geologic hazard abatement district. Each has its own board, its own budget, its own taxing authority or fee schedule, and its own elections, the ones you have never voted in. California has approximately 3,300 special districts. They collectively spend roughly $38 billion a year, employ more than 172,000 people, and receive 20 percent of all property tax revenue raised for local government. They are the largest category of government in California by count and the least known by every other measure.

---

## The Count: 3,300 Governments Hiding in Plain Sight

The 2022 Census of Governments counted 2,861 special districts in California, the second-highest total in the nation behind Illinois's 3,227. The State Controller's office, which uses a broader definition that includes both independent and dependent districts, collects financial reports from more than 4,800. The commonly cited figure of 3,300, drawn from the California Senate Governance and Finance Committee, splits the difference and includes both types.

About two-thirds are independent, meaning they have their own elected or appointed boards and operate autonomously. The remaining third are dependent, governed by an existing city council or county board of supervisors acting in a different capacity. The independent districts are the ones that matter most for accountability purposes, because they constitute an entire layer of government that most Californians do not know exists, cannot name, and have never voted for.

The count has remained roughly stable even as California's population nearly doubled from 22.4 million in 1977 to 39 million today. Districts are created and dissolved, but the net number barely moves. The system is durable. It is also, in the judgment of a UCLA political scientist writing in 1957, a "shadow government." That phrase has aged better than most academic coinages.

---

## What They Do: 60 Laws, 32 Services, 253 Cemeteries

Special districts exist because general-purpose governments, cities and counties, either cannot or choose not to provide a specific service within a specific boundary. California law provides roughly 60 different statutes under which a special district can be formed, each authorizing a different type of service. The result is a taxonomy that reads like a catalog of every public function anyone has ever thought to separate from general government.

The most common type is the county service area, with 895 statewide. Fire protection districts number 386. Community services districts, the Swiss Army knife of the category, number roughly 330 and are authorized to provide up to 32 different services, though most limit themselves to two or three. Cemetery districts number 253, nearly all in rural or formerly rural areas, governed by appointed trustees. Resource conservation districts, at least one per county, total 96. Mosquito and vector control agencies exceed 70. Healthcare districts number 77.

Nearly 85 percent of all special districts perform a single function. They exist to do one thing: deliver water, suppress fires, kill mosquitoes, maintain graves. The specificity is the point. A mosquito abatement district does not build parks. A cemetery district does not fight fires. The single-purpose design is what makes special districts efficient at their designated task and invisible to the public that funds them.

## The Money: $38 Billion and 20 Percent of Your Property Tax

The financial scale of California's special districts is large enough to rival many state agencies. The California Special Districts Association reports roughly $38 billion in combined annual spending. The Little Hoover Commission, examining only independent districts, found $21 billion in annual revenues. The State Controller's 2024 compensation data shows $12.66 billion in wages and $3.38 billion in health and retirement costs across 3,100 reporting districts.

The revenue structure divides along a clean line. Enterprise districts, roughly 27 percent of the total, operate like utilities: they charge fees for water, sewer, electricity, or transit and fund themselves primarily through user charges. Non-enterprise districts, the other 73 percent, rely heavily on property tax allocations. In FY2022-23, special districts received approximately $17.8 billion of the $89 billion in property tax revenue raised for local government, a 20 percent share. Schools received 53 percent. Counties received 14 percent. Cities received 12 percent.

The largest single district by budget is the Metropolitan Water District of Southern California, with a $2.3 billion operating budget for FY2026-27. MWD is the largest supplier of treated water in the United States, serving 19 million people across six counties through 26 member agencies, governed by a 38-member board of directors. SMUD, the Sacramento Municipal Utility District, operates a $2.3 billion budget as the sixth-largest community-owned electric utility in the nation. BART runs a multi-billion dollar transit operation. These are not small-bore local agencies. They are major infrastructure operators with budgets larger than those of most California state departments.

## The Democratic Deficit: Elections Nobody Votes In

Every independent special district has a governing board, typically five members, elected by the residents within the district's boundaries or appointed for fixed terms. Across roughly 2,100 independent districts, that translates to an estimated 16,000 to 17,000 board seats statewide, more than all the city council seats and county supervisor seats in California combined.

Almost nobody votes for them. Special district elections routinely see turnout below 5 percent. In many cases, no election occurs at all. When the number of candidates does not exceed the number of available seats, Government Code Section 1780 allows the existing board to simply appoint someone. In Santa Barbara County alone, more than 50 people were appointed to special district boards "in lieu of election" in a single cycle because no candidates or insufficient candidates filed. Statewide, 33 percent of all California elections are uncontested across all levels, and special district races are widely understood to be among the most frequently uncontested, though no single database tracks the rate.

The invisibility is partly structural. Before SB 929 was signed in 2018, fewer than half of California's special districts had websites. The law required all independent districts to maintain a website by January 2020, though it allowed hardship exemptions by board vote. Even now, the information available on most district websites is minimal. The State Controller publishes financial data for 4,800 districts online. A 2013 CALPIRG review of 79 special districts nationally, including 12 in California, found that 53 percent earned failing grades for spending transparency. Only 7 of 79 had detailed spending checkbooks online.

The combination of low turnout, frequent appointment in lieu of election, and limited public information creates what governance scholars call a democratic deficit: institutions that exercise real power, levy real taxes, and spend real money, but operate with minimal public scrutiny or accountability. The board members are not hiding. They hold public meetings, file financial reports, and comply with the Brown Act. But compliance with open-meeting laws is not the same as democratic engagement. A government that nobody watches is a government that governs itself.

## The Healthcare District Paradox

No category of special district better illustrates the tension between original purpose and current reality than healthcare districts. California has 77 of them. They were created, most of them in the mid-twentieth century, to build and operate hospitals in communities that the private market would not serve, primarily rural areas and small cities.

As of the most recent comprehensive data, 42 districts still operated 47 hospitals. Fifteen had leased or sold their hospitals to private health systems but continued providing health-related services. The remaining 20 or so operated no hospital, no clinic, and no ambulance service. They continued to levy property taxes.

California law requires that a healthcare district that does not operate a hospital, clinic, or ambulance service must spend at least 80 percent of its annual budget on community health grants and no more than 20 percent on administration. Some districts have embraced this model effectively. The Sequoia Healthcare District in San Mateo County, which transferred ownership of its hospital to what is now Dignity Health in 1996, distributes roughly $22 million annually through community health grants, school wellness programs, and clinic support, funded entirely by its 1.3 percent share of county property tax. The Desert Healthcare District in the Coachella Valley has allocated more than $114 million in total since its inception and operates as one of the largest community health funders in Riverside County.

Others are harder to defend. The Eden Township Healthcare District in Alameda County discontinued collecting property taxes in 1976 and now derives revenue from leasing three office buildings to healthcare providers. LAFCO has questioned whether it should continue to exist. The West Contra Costa Healthcare District filed for bankruptcy and closed its only hospital, Doctors Medical Center in San Pablo, in 2015, after years of financial distress.

The Little Hoover Commission's 2017 report focused specifically on healthcare districts that no longer operate hospitals, recommending that they evolve toward a preventive health model or face dissolution. Most have done neither. They persist, levying taxes for a purpose they no longer fulfill in the manner originally intended, governed by boards that few voters can identify, spending money that few residents know they are paying.

## The Scandals: What Happens Without Oversight

Low visibility and minimal scrutiny create conditions for mismanagement. The case studies are not hypothetical.

In Beaumont, a city in Riverside County, seven former officials of the Community Facilities District were charged with corruption spanning three decades. The schemes involved bond sales and development fees. $36.6 million in transportation mitigation fee revenue was collected but not released. The total loss was quantified at $42.96 million. Four officials pleaded guilty and agreed to $8.1 million in restitution. None received prison time.

The Tulare Local Healthcare District hired Healthcare Conglomerate Associates to operate the Tulare Regional Medical Center from 2014 to 2017 under CEO Yorai Benzeevi. The district filed for bankruptcy. Benzeevi was charged with 40 felonies and 6 misdemeanors, eventually accepting a plea deal for 8 counts and ordered to pay $2.4 million in restitution. The CFO pleaded to two felonies. No one went to prison.

At the Panoche Water District in San Benito County, former general manager Dennis Falaschi conspired to steal millions of gallons of water from the Central Valley Project's Delta-Mendota Canal beginning in 1992, a scheme that continued for over two decades. A State Controller audit found lax fiscal controls that allowed personal use of district credit cards for sports tickets, concert tickets, and slot machines. Falaschi pleaded guilty in federal court in May 2024.

The Central Basin Municipal Water District, serving 1.5 million residents in 24 cities, became entangled in the Calderon family corruption case. After leaving the Assembly, Tom Calderon's consulting firm received nearly $1 million over 10 years from Central Basin. The board created a secret $2.7 million fund for a water storage project the district had no legal right to build.

These are not representative of all 3,300 districts. Most operate competently and honestly. But the pattern is consistent: when a special district scandal emerges, it has often been running for years or decades before anyone noticed. The structural conditions, small boards, low visibility, minimal press coverage, infrequent contested elections, create an environment where mismanagement can compound without correction.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's special districts represent a failure of democratic design. The state has fragmented local governance into 3,300 entities, most performing a single function, each with its own board, budget, and taxing authority, creating a layer of government so diffuse that no voter, journalist, or elected official at any other level can meaningfully monitor it. The 58 LAFCOs charged with overseeing district formation and dissolution are, in the Little Hoover Commission's assessment, "not uniformly effective." Academic research on the Cortese-Knox-Hertzberg Act, the state's primary consolidation law, found that it reduced the number of local governments by 7 percent but produced no change in per capita spending or revenue: the surviving governments simply increased their budgets to offset the reduction. Consolidation did not generate the expected efficiency gains. The system resists reform because each district has a constituency, a board, and a statutory mandate, and dissolving any one of them requires overcoming the organized interests of the people it employs and the communities it serves, however small.

The generous read is that special districts are the most responsive form of local government California has. They are created by petition of the affected residents or by vote. They provide specific services to specific geographies at a scale that general-purpose government cannot match. A mosquito abatement district in the Sacramento Delta knows its wetlands better than the county public health department ever could. A community services district in a rural foothill community provides water, garbage collection, and fire protection that the county would otherwise have to deliver less efficiently from a distant seat. The Metropolitan Water District has engineered the water supply for 19 million people through a governance structure that gives its 26 member agencies a seat at the table. The low turnout in special district elections is not unique to special districts; it characterizes all local elections. And the $38 billion in annual spending, measured against the services delivered, represents a decentralized public infrastructure that works, quietly, in thousands of communities across the state.

Both readings describe the same system. The special districts are simultaneously too numerous to oversee and too embedded to remove. The data does not resolve whether the correct response is consolidation or appreciation. It clarifies that the question exists.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The State Controller collects financial reports from 4,800 districts. Sixty-eight failed to file compliant reports in the most recent cycle. The penalty for non-filing ranges from $1,000 to $5,000, an amount that may be less than the cost of compliance for the smallest districts. The data that is filed is accounting-level: revenues, expenditures, assets, and liabilities. It does not tell you whether the cemetery district is maintaining its graves, whether the water district is delivering clean water, or whether the healthcare district is improving community health outcomes. It tells you the money came in and the money went out.

The data also does not capture the cumulative governance burden on residents. A homeowner in an unincorporated coastal area may be subject to a dozen overlapping jurisdictions, each levying a tax or assessment, each holding elections, each issuing reports. The aggregate accountability cost of understanding one's own government is a form of friction that the data cannot measure but the voter experiences as disengagement. The shadow government is not hidden by design. It is hidden by complexity.

And the data says nothing about the districts that should exist but do not. In parts of rural California, the absence of a fire district, a water district, or a healthcare district means the absence of the service entirely. The special district system is not just a story about too many governments. In some places, it is a story about not enough.

---

California did not set out to build 3,300 special-purpose governments. It accreted them, one service at a time, over a century of population growth, geographic expansion, and legislative specificity. Each one was created because someone, somewhere, needed water, fire protection, mosquito control, or a place to bury the dead, and no existing government was willing or able to provide it. The system works in the sense that the water flows, the fires get fought, and the mosquitoes get sprayed. It fails in the sense that almost nobody knows who is doing it, who is paying for it, or who is responsible when it goes wrong. The 3,300 districts are not a bug in California's governance. They are a feature that has outlived the awareness of the people it serves.
