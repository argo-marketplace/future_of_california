# California Counts: The Backyard Revolution
## What 80,000 ADU permits since 2017, SB 9's duplex-by-right law, SB 10's upzoning option, and a 90 percent single-family zoning map reveal about the land-use reform California enacted from Sacramento after decades of local obstruction

California's residential land is approximately 90 percent zoned exclusively for single-family housing. In the state's major metropolitan areas, the share is even higher: approximately 75 percent in Los Angeles, 80 percent in San Jose, and 38 percent in San Francisco (where the lower figure reflects the city's geographic constraints and historical density). Single-family zoning, which prohibits multifamily housing on most residential land, is the primary regulatory mechanism that restricts housing supply, inflates land values, and produces the spatial segregation by income and race that characterizes California's metropolitan geography.

Beginning in 2016, California enacted a series of laws overriding local zoning to permit additional housing. AB 2299 and SB 1069 (2016) streamlined accessory dwelling unit (ADU) approvals. AB 68 and SB 13 (2019) further reduced barriers (eliminating parking requirements, owner-occupancy mandates, and impact fees for ADUs). SB 9 (2021) allowed lot splits and duplexes on single-family parcels statewide. SB 10 (2021) authorized cities to upzone parcels near transit to allow up to 10 units without CEQA review. The cumulative effect has been approximately 80,000 ADU permits issued statewide since 2017, with approximately 25,000 to 30,000 permits per year by 2024, making ADUs the fastest-growing housing type in California.

---

## The ADU Reality

The ADU boom is real but concentrated. Los Angeles County accounts for approximately 40 percent of all ADU permits statewide. The San Francisco Bay Area, San Diego, and Sacramento account for most of the remainder. Rural and inland counties have produced minimal ADU activity. The geographic concentration reflects land values (ADUs are economically viable where rents are high enough to justify the $150,000 to $400,000 construction cost), lot sizes (larger suburban lots accommodate ADUs more easily), and homeowner capacity (building an ADU requires capital, creditworthiness, and the ability to navigate construction).

SB 9's duplex-by-right provision has produced far fewer units than ADU legislation. In its first two years, SB 9 generated approximately 2,000 to 3,000 applications statewide, a fraction of the ADU volume. The gap reflects SB 9's practical limitations: lot splits require subdivision maps, construction of a second unit on a split lot requires full entitlements, and the economics of building a new unit for sale (versus an ADU for rent) are less favorable in most markets. Cities have also imposed design standards, setback requirements, and other conditions that comply with SB 9's letter while discouraging its use.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's zoning reforms are incremental additions to a fundamentally exclusionary system. Eighty thousand ADUs over seven years is meaningful production, but it represents approximately 2 percent of the state's housing deficit. SB 9 and SB 10 theoretically allow millions of new units on currently single-family land, but the gap between theoretical capacity and actual production is vast: permitting an ADU or duplex "by right" does not mean one gets built, because construction requires capital, labor, and materials that are scarce and expensive. The zoning reforms legalize housing without funding it, planning it, or building the infrastructure (water, sewer, transit, schools) to support it. The backyard revolution is producing backyard apartments for affluent homeowners in high-value neighborhoods. It is not producing affordable housing at the scale the state needs.

The generous read is that the zoning reforms represent the most significant land-use deregulation in California history, achieved through state preemption of local zoning authority that was previously considered politically untouchable. The 80,000 ADUs are 80,000 homes that would not exist under the prior zoning regime. Each ADU is a naturally affordable unit (median ADU rents are 20 to 40 percent below comparable apartment rents) that adds supply without public subsidy. SB 9 and SB 10 are frameworks whose full effects will emerge over decades as land values, construction costs, and development patterns adjust. The reforms changed the legal default from "no housing allowed" to "housing allowed." That shift, applied to 90 percent of California's residential land, is structural and irreversible.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

HCD tracks ADU permits through the Annual Progress Report system. It does not track ADU completions (permits issued versus units actually built), ADU rents, ADU occupant demographics, or the net effect of ADUs on neighborhood housing supply and prices. Whether ADUs are occupied by family members (adding no net housing supply), rented at market rates, or rented below market is not systematically measured.
