# California Counts: The Sovereign Bet
## What 76 tribal casinos, $12 billion in annual gaming revenue, 109 federally recognized tribes, and a $200 million campaign to block sports betting reveal about the economic sovereignty California's first people won through slot machines

California's 76 tribal casinos generated approximately $12 billion in gross gaming revenue in the most recent reporting year, making California the second-largest tribal gaming market in the nation (behind Oklahoma). The casinos employ approximately 65,000 workers and are operated by approximately 60 of California's 109 federally recognized tribes under gaming compacts negotiated with the state. The compacts, authorized by the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA, 1988) and approved by California voters through Propositions 5 (1998) and 1A (2000), grant tribes the exclusive right to operate slot machines and banked card games in California.

The exclusivity is the source of the revenue. California does not permit commercial (non-tribal) casinos, slot machines in card rooms, or legal sports betting. The absence of competition means that tribal casinos capture virtually all demand for casino-style gaming from California's 40 million residents and millions of annual tourists. A consumer in California who wants to play a slot machine or blackjack with a house bank has exactly one option: a tribal casino. The geographic distribution of tribal casinos, concentrated in San Diego County (approximately 12 casinos), Riverside County (approximately 8), and scattered across the rest of the state, means that many California consumers drive one to three hours to reach the nearest casino, a travel burden that limits casual participation but concentrates spending among committed players.

## The Revenue Distribution

Tribal gaming revenue is not uniformly distributed across California's 109 tribes. The largest tribal casinos, Morongo, Pechanga, San Manuel, Barona, Viejas, and Pala in Southern California, along with Thunder Valley and Cache Creek in Northern California, generate the majority of the $12 billion. A handful of tribes with large casinos in high-population areas generate hundreds of millions of dollars per year, producing per-capita distributions to tribal members that can exceed $100,000 annually. Other tribes, particularly those in remote rural areas with small casinos, generate modest revenue.

Approximately 40 of California's 109 federally recognized tribes do not operate casinos at all, either because they lack suitable land, lack the capital to build a casino, or have chosen not to pursue gaming. The Revenue Sharing Trust Fund (RSTF), established by the gaming compacts, requires casino-operating tribes to contribute a share of gaming revenue to non-gaming tribes. The RSTF distributes approximately $1.1 million per year to each eligible non-gaming tribe, a meaningful but modest amount that provides basic governmental services (tribal administration, social services, cultural programs) but does not approach the revenue available to large casino-operating tribes.

## Sports Betting: The $400 Million War

In November 2022, California voters rejected two competing sports betting ballot measures: Proposition 26, backed by tribal gaming interests, which would have legalized sports betting at tribal casinos and horse racetracks, and Proposition 27, backed by online sports betting companies (DraftKings, FanDuel, BetMGM), which would have legalized online sports betting operated by commercial companies in partnership with tribes. Combined spending on the two measures exceeded $400 million, making the 2022 California sports betting campaign the most expensive ballot measure campaign on any topic in any state in American history.

Both measures failed. Proposition 26 received 31 percent of the vote. Proposition 27 received 17 percent. The tribal gaming industry spent approximately $200 million to defeat Proposition 27, arguing that online commercial sports betting would undermine tribal sovereignty and divert gaming revenue from tribal communities. The commercial operators spent approximately $170 million promoting Proposition 27, arguing that legal sports betting would generate tax revenue for homelessness programs. The voters said no to both, leaving California as the largest state without legal sports betting.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that tribal gaming exclusivity has created a system in which a small number of geographically fortunate tribes have become extraordinarily wealthy while the majority of California's Native Americans remain among the most economically disadvantaged populations in the state. The $12 billion gaming market generates revenue that flows disproportionately to 10 to 15 tribes whose casinos are located near major population centers, while 40-plus tribes without casinos receive $1.1 million per year from the revenue sharing fund. The exclusivity is defended as sovereignty, but it functions as a monopoly that the tribes use their political power (and $200 million campaign expenditures) to maintain against competition. The rejection of sports betting, in a state where an estimated $12 billion to $15 billion is wagered illegally on sports each year, denies the state tax revenue while protecting the tribes' competitive position.

The generous read is that tribal gaming represents the most successful exercise of tribal sovereignty in California's history. For tribes that were dispossessed of their lands, subjected to termination policies, and reduced to the smallest, most remote reservations in the state, gaming has provided economic self-sufficiency that no federal program ever delivered. The casinos fund tribal government, healthcare, education, housing, cultural preservation, and per-capita distributions that have lifted tribal members out of multigenerational poverty. The exclusivity is not a monopoly granted by the state; it is a sovereign right exercised on sovereign land, negotiated through government-to-government compacts as IGRA intended. The tribes' defense of that exclusivity against commercial competition is the defense of their primary economic asset, an asset that exists because the tribes fought for it through decades of legal and political struggle.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

Tribal gaming revenue is self-reported by tribes to the National Indian Gaming Commission. Individual tribal casino revenue figures are not publicly disclosed; the $12 billion statewide figure is an aggregate. The per-capita distribution amounts paid by individual tribes to their members are also not publicly disclosed. The economic impact of tribal gaming on surrounding non-tribal communities (employment, sales tax from employee spending, infrastructure effects) has been studied in individual cases but not systematically measured statewide.
