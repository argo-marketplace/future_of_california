# California Counts: The Foster System's Quiet Expulsion
## What 53,000 children, a 43 percent reunification rate, $10.4 billion in annual spending, and a cliff at age 21 reveal about the state's obligation to the children it removes from their families

On any given day, approximately 53,000 children and youth in California are in foster care, removed from their families by juvenile court order because a social worker and a judge determined that remaining at home posed an unacceptable risk of abuse or neglect. California's foster care population is the largest in the nation, representing roughly 13 percent of the national total. The children are disproportionately Black (21 percent of foster children versus 5.5 percent of the state's child population), Native American (2.4 percent versus 0.4 percent), and from families living below the poverty line. They live in foster family homes (approximately 40 percent), with relatives or non-related extended family members in kinship placements (approximately 35 percent), in group homes and short-term residential therapeutic programs (approximately 8 percent), or in other arrangements including supervised independent living. Their average stay in care is approximately 19 months. Their average number of placements (different homes or settings) during a single episode is 2.3, meaning the typical foster child moves at least twice. Every move disrupts schools, relationships, therapy, and the fragile stability that children in crisis need most.

## The Spending: $10.4 Billion Across 58 Counties

California's child welfare system is county-administered and state-supervised, a structure that produces 58 different systems operating under a common legal framework with widely varying practice, resources, and outcomes. The total annual spending on child welfare services, including foster care payments, child protective services investigations, family maintenance and reunification services, adoptions, and administrative costs, is approximately $10.4 billion, combining federal (Title IV-E, Title IV-B, and other federal programs), state (realignment funds, general fund), and county contributions.

The per-child cost varies enormously by placement type. A foster family home placement costs the state approximately $1,100 to $2,600 per month in basic foster care payments, plus additional payments for specialized care needs. A kinship placement pays approximately $1,000 to $1,300 per month to relative caregivers, historically less than foster parents received for the same child, a disparity that AB 1811 (2018) and subsequent legislation have partially addressed. A Short-Term Residential Therapeutic Program (the successor to group homes under the Continuum of Care Reform) costs $8,000 to $25,000 per month depending on the level of care. Intensive Services Foster Care, for children with the most complex behavioral health needs, costs $5,000 to $10,000 per month.

The Continuum of Care Reform (CCR), implemented in 2017, was the most significant structural change to California's foster care system in a generation. CCR transformed group homes into Short-Term Residential Therapeutic Programs with a mandate to provide treatment rather than housing, limited STRTP placements to six months, and required counties to develop a continuum of family-based placements supported by intensive services. The reform was driven by research consistently showing that children in family-based placements achieve better outcomes than children in congregate care: lower rates of reentry into care, higher educational attainment, lower rates of justice system involvement, and better mental health outcomes.

The CCR implementation has been uneven. Some counties, particularly large urban counties with well-resourced child welfare agencies, have successfully reduced congregate care placements and expanded therapeutic foster care. Others, particularly small rural counties with limited foster family recruitment capacity, have struggled to find family-based placements for children with complex needs and have experienced placement disruptions, extended stays in emergency shelters, and in extreme cases, children sleeping in child welfare offices because no placement was available.

## The Pipeline: Who Enters and Why

Approximately 450,000 reports of suspected child abuse or neglect are made to California's county child welfare agencies each year through the Child Abuse Hotline system. Of those, approximately 55 percent (roughly 250,000) are screened in for investigation or assessment. Of investigated cases, approximately 25 to 30 percent result in a substantiated finding of abuse or neglect. Of substantiated cases, approximately 15 to 20 percent result in the child being removed from the home and placed in foster care. The funnel narrows dramatically at each stage: roughly one in every 15 to 20 hotline reports ultimately results in a foster care placement.

The reasons for removal are categorized by California's child welfare data system. Neglect (general and severe) accounts for approximately 60 to 65 percent of removals. Physical abuse accounts for approximately 10 to 15 percent. Sexual abuse accounts for approximately 5 to 8 percent. Caretaker absence or incapacity (including parental incarceration, hospitalization, or death) accounts for approximately 10 to 15 percent. Substance abuse by a parent, while not a separate removal category, is present as a contributing factor in approximately 40 to 50 percent of cases.

The correlation between poverty and child welfare involvement is the most consistent finding in child welfare research. Families living below the federal poverty line are approximately 22 times more likely to experience a child welfare investigation than families above the median income. The correlation does not mean that poverty causes abuse. It means that poverty creates conditions (housing instability, food insecurity, untreated mental health and substance use conditions, lack of childcare) that increase family stress and that trigger mandatory reporting by professionals (teachers, doctors, social workers) who interact with poor families at higher rates than with affluent ones. The child welfare system's front door is wider for poor families, and the services available to prevent removal (housing assistance, substance use treatment, mental health care, childcare) are chronically underfunded relative to the back-end costs of foster care.

## Outcomes: What Happens to Foster Children

The outcome data for children who age through California's foster care system is the most damning indictment of the system's failure. The CalYOUTH study, a longitudinal study conducted by the Chapin Hall center at the University of Chicago tracking California foster youth from ages 17 to 23, provides the most comprehensive outcome data available.

Educational attainment: approximately 65 percent of foster youth complete high school by age 19, compared to approximately 88 percent of the general California population. Approximately 8 percent of former foster youth complete a four-year college degree by age 26, compared to approximately 36 percent of the general population.

Employment: approximately 48 percent of former foster youth were employed at age 21, compared to approximately 65 percent of same-age peers. Median annual earnings for employed former foster youth at age 23 were approximately $16,000, below the poverty line for a single individual.

Housing: approximately 36 percent of former foster youth experienced homelessness by age 21. This rate is roughly 10 times the rate for same-age peers and makes former foster youth one of the highest-risk populations for homelessness in the state.

Justice system involvement: approximately 40 percent of male former foster youth and 15 percent of female former foster youth had been incarcerated by age 21. The foster-to-prison pipeline is one of the most documented and least addressed system failures in California social policy.

Mental health: approximately 33 percent of former foster youth met criteria for PTSD at age 21, a rate comparable to combat veterans. Depression, anxiety, and substance use disorders are significantly elevated relative to same-age peers.

The 43 percent reunification rate, meaning 43 percent of children who enter foster care are eventually returned to their families, is both the system's primary goal and its most contested metric. Reunification is successful when families receive the services they need to address the conditions that led to removal. It is unsuccessful when children are returned to unsafe homes and reenter care, a pattern called reentry that occurs for approximately 10 to 12 percent of reunified children within 12 months.

## The Cliff: Aging Out at 21

California extended foster care from age 18 to 21 through AB 12 (2010), implementing the federal Fostering Connections to Success Act. Extended foster care allows youth to remain in supervised placements (apartments, shared housing, college dormitories) while attending school or working, with continuing access to case management, transitional housing, and supportive services. The extension was a significant improvement over the previous system, in which foster youth were abruptly discharged at 18 with minimal transition support.

Approximately 3,500 to 4,000 youth age out of extended foster care each year in California, most at age 21. The transition is a cliff. On the day before their 21st birthday, they have a case worker, a housing placement, access to mental health services, and a support network however imperfect. On the day after, they have none of these unless they have independently secured them. The state's Transitional Housing Program provides limited post-emancipation housing for former foster youth ages 18 to 24, serving approximately 2,000 youth at any time against a need that is multiples larger.

The Independent Living Program (ILP) provides life skills training, employment assistance, educational support, and financial literacy to foster youth ages 16 to 21 (and to former foster youth up to age 23). The program serves approximately 18,000 youth per year across 58 counties. Participation is voluntary, and engagement rates vary by county. The Chafee Education and Training Voucher program provides up to $5,000 per year for post-secondary education, and the state's Foster Youth Higher Education Access Act provides tuition waivers at community colleges and priority registration at CSU and UC. These programs are meaningful for the small percentage of foster youth who pursue post-secondary education. They do not address the majority who do not.

The state spends approximately $10.4 billion per year on the child welfare system. It spends approximately $200 million per year on transition services for the youth it discharges. The ratio, roughly 50 to 1, reflects a system designed to manage the crisis of removal and placement rather than the longer-term challenge of producing functional adults. The system succeeds at protection, its core mandate. It fails at preparation, a mandate it has never been adequately resourced to fulfill.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California removes 53,000 children from their families, places them in a system that moves them an average of 2.3 times per episode, graduates them with a 65 percent high school completion rate, discharges them at 21 into a housing market they cannot afford, and watches 36 percent become homeless and 40 percent of the men become incarcerated, all at a cost of $10.4 billion per year. The system is expensive and its outcomes are catastrophic. The Continuum of Care Reform reduced group home placements without adequately building the therapeutic foster family capacity to replace them, leaving children in limbo. The 58-county administration produces radical variation in practice and outcomes: a foster child's experience in San Francisco bears little resemblance to a foster child's experience in Kern County, despite operating under the same legal framework. The front-end prevention services that could keep families together, housing assistance, substance use treatment, mental health care, childcare, are funded at a fraction of the back-end removal and placement costs. California spends $10.4 billion reacting to family crisis and a fraction of that preventing it. The foster system does not fail despite the money spent. It fails because the money is spent in the wrong place.

The generous read is that California has enacted more foster care reforms in the past decade than any other state, driven by evidence and implemented with genuine commitment. The extension of foster care to 21, a policy adopted by only a handful of states, reduced homelessness and improved outcomes for youth who participate. The Continuum of Care Reform is moving the system toward the family-based model that research supports. The state's investment in Resource Family Approval, which created a unified approval process for all family-based caregivers, simplified a bureaucratic nightmare that deterred potential foster parents. Kinship care payments have been equalized, recognizing that relatives who step up to care for children deserve the same financial support as strangers. And the CalYOUTH study itself, funded by the state, represents a rare commitment to measuring outcomes rather than outputs, providing the data that drives further reform. The foster care system will never produce outcomes equal to intact families, because the children who enter it arrive with trauma, disrupted attachments, and accumulated disadvantage that no placement can fully repair. The question is whether the system makes things better or worse relative to the alternative of leaving children in the homes from which they were removed. The evidence, for all its limitations, suggests that for the children who need it most, the system makes things better. Imperfectly, inadequately, and at tremendous cost. But better.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

California's Child Welfare Services/Case Management System (CWS/CMS), the legacy data system used by county child welfare agencies, tracks placements, case status, demographics, and disposition outcomes. It does not track long-term outcomes: what happens to children after their cases close. The CalYOUTH study provides longitudinal outcome data for a cohort of youth, but it is a research study, not a system feature. California does not routinely track whether former foster children complete college, earn a living wage, maintain stable housing, or avoid incarceration. The system monitors itself while children are in it and loses sight of them when they leave.

The data does not capture the experience of the 55 percent of hotline reports that are screened out without investigation. Some of those reports involve families that need services but do not meet the threshold for child welfare intervention. Others involve malicious or mistaken reports. The screening decision, made by a hotline worker based on a phone call, determines whether a family enters the system at all. Variation in screening practices across counties means that the same report might be investigated in one county and screened out in another. The data records the decision. It does not record whether the decision was correct.

The data also does not capture the racial disproportionality in full dimension. Black children are represented in California's foster care system at approximately four times their share of the child population. Native American children at approximately six times. The causes of disproportionality are debated: differential rates of poverty and associated risk factors, differential reporting by mandated reporters who interact more frequently with families of color, and differential decision-making by social workers and judges at each stage of the process. Each explanation implies a different remedy. The data shows the disparity. It does not explain it, because explaining it requires disentangling the effects of poverty, racism, and system design, a task that no dataset can perform alone.

---

California removes children from their families to protect them, then places them in a system that moves them, medicates them, educates them sporadically, and discharges them at 21 into an economy that has no place for a young adult with a trauma history, a fragmented education, and no family to fall back on. The $10.4 billion annual cost is the price of a system designed to manage the acute crisis of child abuse and neglect. It is not the price of a system designed to produce adults who can support themselves, maintain relationships, and avoid the institutions, jails, shelters, emergency rooms, that become the foster system's default aftercare. The children did not choose to enter care. They did not choose the placements, the moves, the schools, or the discharge date. Every decision was made for them, by a system that is well-intentioned, chronically overwhelmed, and structurally unable to deliver what it promises: a safe childhood, a prepared adulthood, and a fair start. The data shows 53,000 children in care. It does not show what they lose on the way through.
