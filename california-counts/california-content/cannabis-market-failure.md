# California Counts: The Legal Market That Isn't Winning
## What $7.9 billion in tax revenue, a 75 percent illicit market share, and 8,000 shuttered licenses reveal about California's experiment in regulated cannabis

California legalized recreational cannabis in November 2016 when voters approved Proposition 64 by a margin of 57 to 43 percent. The measure promised a regulated market that would eliminate the black market, generate substantial tax revenue, repair the harms of the War on Drugs, and create a legitimate industry. Nine years later, the legal market has generated approximately $7.9 billion in cumulative state and local tax revenue. It has not eliminated the black market. Estimates from the Department of Cannabis Control and academic researchers suggest that illicit cannabis sales in California still account for 60 to 75 percent of total transactions by volume. The state licensed approximately 12,000 cannabis businesses at the peak in 2022. By early 2026, roughly 8,000 licenses have been surrendered, suspended, or not renewed. The legal market is shrinking while the illegal market endures. The most populous state in the nation, the first to legalize medical cannabis in 1996 and among the first to legalize recreational use, has produced a regulatory framework that is losing to its unregulated competitor.

---

## The Tax Architecture

Proposition 64 created a multi-layered tax structure. The state imposed a 15 percent excise tax on retail cannabis purchases, calculated on the average market price. It imposed a cultivation tax of $9.25 per ounce on flowers and $2.75 per ounce on leaves, adjusted annually for inflation. Local governments were permitted to impose their own taxes on top, with no cap. And the standard state and local sales tax of 7.25 to 10.75 percent applied to cannabis as to any other retail good.

The combined tax burden on legal cannabis in California ranges from 35 to 80 percent of retail price depending on the jurisdiction. In Los Angeles, a consumer purchasing cannabis at a licensed dispensary pays the 15 percent state excise tax, the standard 9.5 percent sales tax, and a 10 percent local cannabis tax, for a combined rate of approximately 34.5 percent before accounting for the cultivation tax embedded in the wholesale price. In some jurisdictions, the combined effective rate exceeds 45 percent. In Oakland, combined rates have reached 50 percent or more.

The cultivation tax was eliminated effective July 1, 2022, after years of industry lobbying. Cultivators argued the per-ounce tax was unsustainable when wholesale prices collapsed from over $1,500 per pound in 2018 to below $400 per pound in 2022 due to oversupply. The excise tax was simultaneously restructured from a wholesale-price basis to a point-of-sale calculation to simplify compliance.

Even after the cultivation tax elimination, the total tax burden on legal cannabis significantly exceeds that of any comparable consumer product. Alcohol excise taxes in California amount to approximately 3 to 5 percent of retail price. Tobacco taxes are high in percentage terms but operate in a market without a significant untaxed competitor. Cannabis competes directly with an untaxed illicit product of comparable or superior quality available through delivery services, social media, and unlicensed storefronts operating openly in many jurisdictions.

---

## The Revenue Reality

Tax revenue from cannabis has consistently underperformed projections. The Legislative Analyst's Office estimated in 2016 that cannabis taxes would generate approximately $1 billion per year in state revenue within a few years of legalization. The first full year of legal sales, 2018, produced approximately $635 million in state cannabis tax revenue. Revenue grew to $817 million in 2019, $1.03 billion in 2020, peaked at approximately $1.3 billion in 2021, then declined to approximately $1.1 billion in 2022 and $900 million in 2023. The 2024 and 2025 figures show continued decline, with full-year state revenue falling below $800 million.

The decline reflects multiple factors. Wholesale cannabis prices collapsed as legal cultivation flooded the market, reducing the excise tax yield per transaction. The elimination of the cultivation tax removed a revenue stream. Consumer traffic shifted partially back to illicit sources as legal prices remained high relative to street prices. And the number of licensed retailers declined as businesses closed, reducing the taxable transaction base.

Local cannabis tax revenues have followed similar trajectories. The City of Los Angeles projected $50 million annually from its cannabis tax. Actual collections have ranged from $44 million to $62 million, below what was projected once the market matured. Some smaller jurisdictions that anticipated cannabis taxes as a budget lifeline have collected a fraction of expectations.

The total cumulative state and local cannabis tax revenue from legalization through early 2026 is approximately $7.9 billion. That is real money. It funds youth drug prevention programs, environmental cleanup of illegal grow sites, law enforcement, and community reinvestment grants. But it is roughly half of what a fully functioning legal market of California's size would have generated, the difference being the revenue lost to the illicit market.

---

## The Illicit Market: Why It Persists

California's illicit cannabis market did not exist because cannabis was illegal. It existed because California had 20 years of quasi-legal medical cannabis from 1996 to 2016, which built a massive cultivation, distribution, and retail infrastructure that did not disappear when recreational legalization created a parallel regulated system. The Emerald Triangle counties of Humboldt, Mendocino, and Trinity had thousands of cultivation operations that supplied the medical and gray market. Many never transitioned to the legal market because the costs of compliance, including licensing fees, testing requirements, environmental regulations, track-and-trace systems, local permits, and taxes, exceeded what they had been paying as nominally medical growers.

The price differential between legal and illicit cannabis is substantial. A legal eighth-ounce (3.5 grams) retails for $35 to $60 at a licensed dispensary in a major metropolitan area, after taxes. The same quantity from an unlicensed source costs $20 to $35. For price-sensitive consumers, which includes much of the cannabis market, the legal product offers no quality advantage sufficient to justify a 50 to 100 percent price premium.

Unlicensed dispensaries operate openly in many California cities. A 2023 investigation by the Department of Cannabis Control identified more than 2,500 unlicensed retail operations statewide. Los Angeles alone had an estimated 1,200 unlicensed dispensaries operating at various points, outnumbering the roughly 200 licensed ones. Local law enforcement has limited capacity and sometimes limited will to shut them down. When closed, many reopen within weeks under new names.

The illicit cultivation market has also expanded, not contracted, since legalization. Environmental regulators and law enforcement report that illegal grow operations on public lands, in residential neighborhoods, and in warehouses have proliferated, often operated by organized groups exploiting California's legal cover. Illegal grows on national forest land increased from approximately 200 identified sites in 2015 to over 600 in 2022, with significant environmental damage from pesticides, water diversions, and deforestation. Indoor grows in residential rentals have caused fires, structural damage, and electrical grid strain.

## The Licensing Collapse

California's cannabis licensing system was designed with the complexity of the state's regulatory environment. Businesses needed both a state license from the Department of Cannabis Control (or its predecessors, three separate agencies consolidated in 2021) and local approval from the city or county where they operated. Many jurisdictions banned cannabis businesses entirely: as of 2025, approximately 62 percent of California's cities and 73 percent of its counties do not permit retail cannabis sales. The result is a legal desert across much of the state, where consumers have no licensed option within a reasonable driving distance and default to delivery from unlicensed sources.

The provisional licensing system, intended as a bridge while businesses completed full environmental review, became a long-term limbo. Thousands of businesses operated on provisional licenses for years, unable to secure final licenses due to CEQA compliance costs, local permitting delays, and regulatory uncertainty. When the state set deadlines for provisional-to-annual license conversion, hundreds of businesses could not meet the requirements and closed.

Peak active licenses reached approximately 12,000 in 2022. By early 2026, the Department of Cannabis Control reports approximately 8,600 active licenses, a decline of nearly 30 percent. The losses are concentrated in cultivation, where wholesale price collapse made small and medium operations economically unviable, and in retail, where competition from the illicit market and high operating costs drove closures. Many closures are in equity applicants, the social equity program participants who received priority licensing but lacked the capital to sustain operations through years of below-break-even revenue.

---

## The Social Equity Gap

Proposition 64 allocated 60 percent of cannabis tax revenue above a threshold to youth drug prevention, treatment, and education; 20 percent to environmental cleanup; and 20 percent to law enforcement. It did not include provisions for equity licensing or reparative justice for communities targeted by cannabis criminalization. Those provisions came later, through legislation and local programs.

The state and individual cities created social equity programs that offered fee waivers, technical assistance, and priority processing to applicants from communities disproportionately affected by cannabis enforcement, generally defined as people with prior cannabis convictions, residents of neighborhoods with high arrest rates, or low-income individuals. San Francisco, Oakland, Los Angeles, and Sacramento all launched equity programs.

The results have been disappointing. A 2024 report from the Department of Cannabis Control found that equity licensees were significantly more likely to surrender their licenses than non-equity licensees. The reasons were financial: equity applicants received licensing advantages but not capital. Starting a cannabis business in California requires $500,000 to $2 million in startup capital for retail operations, including build-out, inventory, security, compliance systems, and working capital to survive the months or years before profitability. Fee waivers worth $5,000 to $20,000 did not close this gap. Several jurisdictions offered low-interest loans or grants, but the amounts were insufficient relative to total startup costs.

Meanwhile, the corporate consolidation of the legal market has proceeded. Multi-state operators and well-capitalized companies have acquired licenses, brands, and retail locations from struggling smaller operators, including equity licensees. The legal cannabis market in California is increasingly dominated by larger companies with access to capital markets, while the small farmers and community operators that legalization was supposed to empower have been squeezed out by the same regulatory costs that were intended to professionalize the industry.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California designed a regulatory system so expensive, so complex, and so burdened by taxation that it cannot compete with the illegal market it was supposed to replace. The state imposed a tax burden of 35 to 80 percent on a product available tax-free from an unlicensed competitor, then expressed surprise when consumers chose the cheaper option. It created a licensing system requiring CEQA compliance, local permits, state permits, track-and-trace systems, and mandatory testing, driving compliance costs to levels that small operators could not sustain. It allowed 62 percent of cities and 73 percent of counties to ban legal sales, then wondered why the illicit market thrived in those areas. It promised social equity but delivered fee waivers to people who needed capital. Nine years after legalization, the illegal market still holds 60 to 75 percent market share, legal businesses are closing by the thousands, tax revenue is declining, and the environmental damage from illegal grows is worse than before legalization. The experiment has not failed because legalization is a bad idea. It has failed because California made legal cannabis more expensive and harder to access than illegal cannabis, and consumers responded rationally.

The generous read is that California is nine years into a multi-decade market transition, and the trajectory, while imperfect, is directional. Tax revenue of $7.9 billion is real money funding real programs. The legal market did not exist before 2018 and now generates nearly a billion dollars annually in state revenue alone. The cultivation tax was eliminated when it proved counterproductive, demonstrating regulatory adaptability. The Department of Cannabis Control's consolidation of three agencies into one reduced bureaucratic complexity. Enforcement against unlicensed operators has intensified, with DCC conducting hundreds of operations annually and seizing millions in illicit product. The social equity programs, despite their limitations, represent a genuine attempt at reparative policy that no other industry has undertaken. And the 60 to 75 percent illicit market share, while high, represents a market that was 100 percent illicit nine years ago. The legal share is growing, slowly, and as illicit enforcement increases and tax reform reduces the price differential, the transition will accelerate. No jurisdiction that has legalized cannabis has achieved full market capture in less than a decade. California's timeline is within international norms, even if its regulatory complexity is not.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Department of Cannabis Control's track-and-trace system, METRC, captures all legal cannabis transactions from seed to sale. It does not capture the illicit market. The estimates of 60 to 75 percent illicit market share are derived from surveys, consumption models, and comparisons between legal sales volume and estimated total consumption based on population use rates. These estimates carry significant uncertainty. Some researchers believe the illicit share is declining faster than surveys suggest because respondents may not distinguish between licensed and unlicensed delivery services.

The data does not capture the labor market effects of the transition. The legal cannabis industry employed approximately 80,000 to 100,000 workers in California at its peak. Many were previously employed in the unregulated market and transitioned to legal employment with benefits, worker protections, and stable wages. As licenses decline, those workers are displaced, and some return to the illicit market where labor protections do not exist.

The data also does not capture the environmental comparison between legal and illegal cultivation. Legal cultivators must comply with water diversion limits, pesticide restrictions, waste management requirements, and energy efficiency standards. Illegal cultivators comply with none of these. To the extent that high regulatory costs drive production from the legal to the illegal market, the environmental outcome is worse than if standards were lower but compliance was broader. This tradeoff, between rigorous standards with low compliance and moderate standards with high compliance, is the central design challenge that California's cannabis regulatory reform has not resolved.

---

California legalized cannabis to end the war on drugs, create a legitimate industry, and generate tax revenue. It achieved the last of these partially. The first two remain works in progress. The legal market exists but is not winning. Nine years after voters approved Proposition 64, the majority of cannabis consumed in California is still grown, sold, and purchased outside the regulatory framework the state spent hundreds of millions to build and staff. The gap between the legal market and the illegal one is not a law enforcement problem. It is a price problem. Legal cannabis costs 50 to 100 percent more than illegal cannabis because of taxes, testing, compliance, and regulatory overhead. Until that gap narrows, through tax cuts, enforcement, or both, the legal market will remain a minority player in its own state. The question California has not answered in nine years of legalization is simple: is the purpose of cannabis regulation to raise revenue, to eliminate the black market, or to professionalize the industry? Because at current tax rates and compliance costs, the state cannot achieve all three simultaneously. It may not be achieving any of them adequately.
