# California Counts: The Right to Know
## What 10 days to respond, 14 categorical exemptions, zero enforcement penalties for noncompliance, and a requestor who must sue to compel disclosure reveal about the public records law California enacted to ensure transparency and administers to ensure delay

The California Public Records Act (CPRA), enacted in 1968 and recodified in 2022 (SB 900, Government Code sections 7920-7931), establishes the public's right to inspect and copy records maintained by state and local government agencies. The law requires agencies to respond to requests within 10 calendar days (with a possible 14-day extension for "unusual circumstances"), to disclose all responsive records unless a specific exemption applies, and to justify any withholding with a written explanation identifying the applicable exemption. The CPRA includes 14 categorical exemptions covering personnel records, law enforcement investigations, attorney-client privilege, deliberative process, trade secrets, and several other categories.

California's open-records framework is, on paper, among the strongest in the nation. In practice, compliance is inconsistent, enforcement is absent, and delay is the dominant strategy of agencies that prefer not to disclose. There is no oversight body responsible for monitoring CPRA compliance. There is no administrative appeal process: a requestor whose request is denied or delayed has no remedy short of filing a lawsuit in superior court. The litigation option is theoretically available but practically accessible only to news organizations, law firms, and advocacy groups with the resources to pursue it. An ordinary citizen who is told "we need more time" or "the records are exempt" has no affordable mechanism to challenge the agency's determination.

## The Compliance Gap

Investigative journalism organizations, academic researchers, and open-government advocacy groups consistently report that CPRA compliance varies dramatically by agency and that delay is the norm for sensitive requests. The First Amendment Coalition has documented response times ranging from days (for routine requests from friendly agencies) to years (for politically sensitive requests from agencies with institutional reasons to delay). Common delay tactics include overly broad claims of exemption, requests for "clarification" of clear requests, claims that responsive records cannot be located, and responses that provide partial records while withholding others without adequate justification.

SB 1421 and SB 16 (the police transparency laws) provide a case study: despite clear statutory mandates to disclose specific categories of peace officer records, multiple law enforcement agencies delayed or refused disclosure for months or years, requiring litigation to compel compliance. The city of Inglewood spent approximately $1 million in legal fees fighting disclosure of police shooting records that the law clearly required it to release. The agency faced no penalty for the delay; the requestor (a news organization) bore the cost of litigation, and the records were eventually released years after they were due.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that the CPRA is an accountability law without accountability. The law declares a right to records but provides no enforcement mechanism other than litigation, which is expensive, slow, and available only to well-resourced requestors. The 10-day response deadline is routinely ignored without consequence. The exemptions are broad enough that a creative attorney can construct a plausible argument for withholding almost any politically sensitive record. The absence of an oversight body (analogous to a state ombudsman or information commissioner, as exists in many other states and countries) means that no institution monitors compliance, identifies patterns of noncompliance, or imposes consequences on agencies that systematically obstruct disclosure. The CPRA protects the government from accountability for failing to comply with the accountability law.

The generous read is that the CPRA has produced enormous public benefit over its 57-year history: it is the legal foundation for investigative journalism, government accountability research, community access to decision-making documents, and the open-data movement that has made California's fiscal, environmental, criminal justice, and regulatory data publicly accessible. The fee-shifting provision (Government Code section 7923.115) allows prevailing requestors to recover attorney's fees, creating an economic incentive for pro bono and contingency representation. The 2022 recodification (SB 900) modernized the law and clarified ambiguities. The law works imperfectly but continuously: thousands of CPRA requests are fulfilled every day across California's thousands of government agencies, providing the public with the information that democratic governance requires.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

No California agency tracks CPRA compliance statewide: the number of requests received, response times, rates of full disclosure versus partial disclosure versus denial, exemptions invoked, or litigation filed. The fundamental performance question (is the CPRA working?) cannot be answered because no one measures it. The law mandates disclosure but does not mandate measurement of whether disclosure occurs.
