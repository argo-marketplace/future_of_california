# California Counts: The Golden Economy
## What $150 billion in annual visitor spending, 1.2 million tourism jobs, a $12 billion state and local tax contribution, and a post-pandemic recovery that left business travel permanently reduced reveal about the industry California promotes relentlessly and protects minimally

California's tourism and hospitality industry generates approximately $150 billion in annual visitor spending, supports approximately 1.2 million direct jobs, and produces approximately $12 billion in state and local tax revenue (hotel taxes, sales taxes on visitor spending, and various local assessments). California is the most-visited state in the nation by domestic travelers and the second-most-visited by international travelers (after New York). Approximately 270 million person-trips per year include at least one overnight stay or a day trip of 50 miles or more.

Visit California, the state's tourism marketing organization, operates on an annual budget of approximately $90 million funded by a voluntary assessment on the tourism industry (not general fund tax revenue). The organization runs the "Visit California" and "Dream Big" campaigns targeting domestic and international travelers. California's tourism product is unusually diverse: coastal beaches, national parks (nine), urban entertainment (Los Angeles, San Francisco), wine regions (Napa, Sonoma, Paso Robles), ski resorts (Tahoe), desert landscapes (Death Valley, Joshua Tree), and theme parks (Disneyland, Universal Studios) create year-round demand across geographies.

## The Post-Pandemic Structure

The pandemic permanently altered California's tourism economy. Business travel (conventions, corporate meetings, trade shows) declined approximately 30 percent from 2019 levels and has not recovered. Business travelers spend approximately twice as much per trip as leisure travelers (higher hotel rates, meal expenses, conference fees), meaning that the 30 percent reduction in business travel volume represents a disproportionate revenue loss. San Francisco, which depended heavily on convention and business travel, has been particularly affected: hotel occupancy rates in 2025 remain approximately 15 to 20 percent below 2019 levels.

Leisure travel, by contrast, recovered to pre-pandemic levels by 2023 and has exceeded 2019 levels since. The shift from business to leisure changes the geography of tourism (leisure travelers favor coastal, outdoor, and entertainment destinations over urban convention centers), the seasonality (leisure travel is more concentrated in summer and holidays), and the employment structure (leisure hospitality jobs tend to be more seasonal, lower-wage, and less likely to include benefits than convention-center and business-hotel employment).

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's $150 billion tourism economy rests on a workforce of 1.2 million people in the lowest-paid sector of the state's economy. Median wages in accommodation and food service are approximately $18 to $22 per hour (depending on the subsector), well below the living wage in every California county. The workers who make tourism function (housekeepers, servers, cooks, drivers, retail clerks, park workers) cannot afford to live in the tourist destinations they serve (Napa, Monterey, Lake Tahoe, coastal Los Angeles). Housing costs in tourism-heavy regions are inflated by vacation rentals and second homes that reduce housing supply for local workers. The industry extracts $150 billion in visitor spending while externalizing the cost of housing its workforce onto workers themselves, public assistance programs, and communities that absorb long commuters.

The generous read is that tourism generates $12 billion in tax revenue that funds schools, roads, parks, and services for all Californians, not just those in the tourism industry. The 1.2 million jobs, while lower-wage than the state average, provide employment for workers (immigrants, young people, workers without college degrees) who face barriers in other sectors. Hotel and restaurant unions (UNITE HERE, local affiliates) have secured wages of $25 to $30 per hour plus benefits in unionized properties in Los Angeles, San Francisco, and other major markets. The industry's recovery from the pandemic, despite the loss of business travel, demonstrates the resilience and global appeal of California as a destination. Tourism is one of the few industries that distributes economic activity (visitor spending) across the state's geography rather than concentrating it in Silicon Valley and Los Angeles.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

Visit California and the EDD publish tourism spending estimates, employment figures, and tax revenue calculations. They do not publish data on the housing status of tourism workers (housing cost burden, commute distance, use of public assistance), the displacement effects of tourism on local housing markets, or the net fiscal impact of tourism by community (whether visitor-generated tax revenue in a given jurisdiction exceeds the public costs of serving the visitor economy). The $150 billion in spending is measured. Its distribution (who benefits, who bears the costs) is not.
