# California Counts: The Late Count
## What a 37 percent turnout, a 30,000-vote swing from election night to final count, a debunked "zero votes" claim, a guilty plea for paying Skid Row residents to register, and a presidential accusation of a "rigged election" reveal about the 2026 Los Angeles mayoral primary and the collision between California's ballot-counting mechanics and a political culture primed to see fraud everywhere

On June 3, 2026, Los Angeles held its mayoral primary election. Incumbent Karen Bass finished first with approximately 275,992 votes (34.3 percent). City Councilmember Nithya Raman finished second with approximately 229,576 votes (28.6 percent). Reality television personality and entrepreneur Spencer Pratt finished third with approximately 207,757 votes (25.8 percent). Under the city's top-two system, Bass and Raman advanced to the November general election. Pratt was eliminated by approximately 21,800 votes, roughly 2.7 percentage points behind Raman.

The result was not what election night suggested. When the first round of results posted on the evening of June 3, Pratt led Raman by approximately 20,000 votes and six percentage points. Over the following days, as the Los Angeles County Registrar-Recorder processed mail ballots, drop-off ballots, and provisional ballots, Raman steadily closed the gap. By Sunday, June 8, Raman overtook Pratt (27.1 percent to 26.6 percent, a margin of 3,113 votes). The gap continued to widen through the final count. Pratt conceded on June 10. The headline in Variety read: "You Have No Idea How Bad Things Are About to Get."

The late shift from Pratt to Raman was not anomalous. It was the standard California pattern.

## The Turnout

Approximately 2.2 million of Los Angeles County's 5.8 million registered voters cast ballots, a turnout of roughly 37.7 percent. This is high by the standards of recent LA mayoral primaries.

| Year | Turnout (%) | Context |
|------|-------------|---------|
| 2013 | 23% | Garcetti vs. Greuel (primary) |
| 2017 | 20.1% | Garcetti incumbent (primary) |
| 2022 | 43.9% | Bass vs. Caruso (general, on November ballot) |
| 2026 | 37.7% | Bass vs. Raman vs. Pratt (primary) |

The 2022 election benefited from being consolidated with the November general election, which inflated turnout beyond the typical standalone mayoral contest. The 2026 primary, held as a standalone June election, drew nearly double the participation of the 2017 primary. Pratt's celebrity candidacy and the national attention it attracted almost certainly contributed to the higher engagement.

## The Mail Ballot Shift

California's vote-counting process is designed to prioritize accuracy over speed. On election night, county registrars report results from ballots already processed: early vote-by-mail ballots received and verified before election day and, in some counties, in-person votes cast at vote centers. Ballots dropped off on election day, ballots arriving by mail after election day (California accepts mail ballots postmarked by election day and received up to seven days later), and provisional ballots are counted in the days and weeks following the election. Final certification can take up to 30 days.

The ballots counted after election night skew systematically. Voters who mail their ballots late or drop them off on election day tend to be younger, more urban, and more progressive than voters who return ballots early or vote in person. This pattern has been documented in every major California election of the past decade. In the 2024 presidential election, Democratic candidates gained an average of three to five percentage points statewide between election night and final certification. In competitive Los Angeles races, the shift has been larger.

The Pratt-to-Raman shift in the 2026 mayoral primary followed this pattern precisely. Pratt's election night lead reflected the composition of the early-counted vote. Raman's overtaking reflected the composition of the late-counted vote. The outcome was not a reversal; it was the predictable completion of a count that was always going to shift in one direction.

## The "Zero Votes" Claim

On June 6, a viral post on X alleged that a batch of approximately 24,000 ballots had been reported with zero votes for Spencer Pratt. The claim spread rapidly, generating millions of impressions and prompting accusations of ballot manipulation. The implication was that someone had manufactured or altered ballots to exclude Pratt.

The claim was false. The Los Angeles County Registrar-Recorder confirmed that "Spencer Pratt received votes in every official election results update." The apparent anomaly was an artifact of the Associated Press data feed: the electronic update pulled vote totals for Bass and Raman first, and then, exactly one minute later, pulled Pratt's votes from the same batch. Anyone watching the AP feed in real time saw a momentary gap that did not exist in the actual count. PolitiFact, Poynter, Snopes, and Media Bias/Fact Check all independently verified that the claim was baseless. Federal prosecutor Bill Essayli, not typically aligned with the fact-checking establishment, also confirmed the claim was false.

## The Skid Row Videos

Beginning on June 9, videos circulated on X showing individuals in the Skid Row neighborhood of downtown Los Angeles claiming they had been paid two to five dollars to vote for Bass or Raman. The videos were recorded by activists and amplified by accounts sympathetic to Pratt's candidacy and to broader election fraud narratives.

Journalists who investigated the claims found that the evidence did not support the allegations. One woman featured in the most widely shared video was registered to vote in Inglewood, not in the City of Los Angeles. She did not return her ballot. She could not have cast a vote in the LA mayoral race. The remaining claims, that unspecified individuals were paid to vote, have not been independently verified by any news organization, law enforcement agency, or election oversight body.

The Skid Row allegations intersected with a real federal case, but the connection was thinner than it appeared. In May 2025, Brenda Lee Brown Armstrong, a 64-year-old Marina del Rey resident and professional petition circulator, pleaded guilty in federal court to paying Skid Row residents two to three dollars each to register to vote and sign petitions. Armstrong used her own address on registration forms when individuals did not have a fixed address. The case was exposed by an undercover investigation by the O'Keefe Media Group and resulted in federal charges carrying up to five years in prison. Armstrong was the first person charged, and the investigation is ongoing.

The Armstrong case is real and serious. It is also a petition signature and voter registration fraud case, not a vote-buying case. Armstrong was not accused of paying anyone to vote for a particular candidate. She was accused of paying people to register and sign petitions, inflating her per-signature compensation from petition campaigns. The distinction matters: paying someone to register is a federal crime, but it does not produce fraudulent ballots. A person who registers under someone else's address still has to request, receive, and return a ballot to cast a vote, and there is no evidence that the registrations Armstrong facilitated resulted in fraudulent votes in the 2026 mayoral election.

## The Presidential Response

On June 9, President Donald Trump posted on Truth Social: "No way this could have happened. Rigged Election!" In a subsequent post, he wrote: "Not possible for Spencer Pratt to have lost the L.A. runoffs after the big lead he had." Trump did not cite specific evidence of fraud or irregularities. He did not reference the Registrar-Recorder's data, the AP feed timing, the Armstrong case, or any other factual basis for the claim. The posts were consistent with Trump's pattern of alleging fraud in elections where candidates he favors lose ground in late-counted ballots, a pattern that dates to the 2020 presidential election and has recurred in multiple subsequent contests.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that the Skid Row allegations and the Armstrong guilty plea, while distinct, point to genuine vulnerabilities in the election system. Paying people to register to vote is a federal crime because it introduces registrations that do not reflect genuine voter intent. The fact that a professional petition circulator was convicted of doing exactly this in the same neighborhood where paid-voting allegations later emerged is not a coincidence; it reflects an ecosystem of low-level electoral manipulation that thrives in communities with high concentrations of vulnerable people. The 30,000-vote shift from election night to final count, even if structurally predictable, is difficult for voters to understand and trust. A system that routinely produces election-night results that reverse over the following week is a system that invites suspicion, regardless of whether the suspicion is warranted.

The generous read is that every specific fraud claim in the 2026 LA mayoral primary has been investigated and debunked. The "zero votes" batch was a data feed timing artifact. The key individual in the most viral Skid Row video was not registered in the city and did not vote. The Armstrong case predates the election and involves petition signatures, not vote-buying. The mail ballot shift is a documented, structural feature of California elections that has been explained by election officials, academics, and journalists in every cycle for the past decade. The LA County Registrar-Recorder runs one of the most transparent ballot-counting operations in the country, publishing regular updates with batch-level detail. The result, Bass first, Raman second, Pratt third, reflects the preferences of 2.2 million voters counted under rules that have been in place for years. The fraud narrative is not a response to evidence; it is a template applied to any election where the late count shifts the result.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Los Angeles County Registrar-Recorder publishes vote totals by candidate and update batch. It does not publish batch-level demographic or geographic breakdowns that would allow independent analysts to verify the composition of early-counted versus late-counted ballots. The Skid Row paid-voting allegations remain uninvestigated by law enforcement as of this writing; the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but it is also not evidence of fraud. The Armstrong case is ongoing, and the scope of the registration fraud scheme (how many registrations, how many resulted in ballot requests, how many resulted in returned ballots) has not been publicly disclosed. The question of whether any fraudulent votes were cast in the 2026 LA mayoral primary cannot be answered with certainty from currently available data, though no evidence that any were has been produced.
