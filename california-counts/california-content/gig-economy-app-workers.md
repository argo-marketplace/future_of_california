# California Counts: The App Workers
## What 1.4 million gig workers, a $200 million ballot measure, an AB 5 law the voters overrode, and a 72 percent earnings penalty reveal about the labor classification fight California started and then lost

California has approximately 1.2 million to 1.4 million workers who perform gig or platform-based work as a primary or significant source of income, according to estimates by the UC Berkeley Labor Center and the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Contingent Worker Survey. The category includes app-based ride-hail drivers (Uber, Lyft), delivery drivers (DoorDash, Instacart, Amazon Flex), freelance professionals (Upwork, Fiverr), and platform-based care workers, cleaners, and maintenance providers. An additional several million workers perform occasional gig work to supplement traditional employment.

AB 5 (2019), authored by Assemblywoman Lorena Gonzalez, codified the "ABC test" from the California Supreme Court's Dynamex decision (2018), establishing a three-part standard for determining whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor. Under the ABC test, a worker is an employee unless the hiring entity demonstrates that the worker (A) is free from the control and direction of the hiring entity, (B) performs work outside the usual course of the hiring entity's business, and (C) is customarily engaged in an independently established trade, occupation, or business. The law was designed to reclassify gig workers as employees, entitling them to minimum wage, overtime, workers' compensation, unemployment insurance, and employer-provided benefits.

## Proposition 22: The Override

The gig companies responded to AB 5 with Proposition 22 (November 2020), a ballot measure that exempted app-based transportation and delivery companies from AB 5 and created an alternative benefits framework for their drivers. The campaign for Proposition 22 cost approximately $200 million, the most expensive ballot measure campaign in California history, funded almost entirely by Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, and Instacart. The opposition campaign spent approximately $20 million. Proposition 22 passed with 59 percent of the vote.

Proposition 22 guarantees app-based drivers a minimum earnings floor of 120 percent of the local minimum wage for "engaged time" (the time between accepting a ride or delivery request and completing it), a healthcare stipend for drivers who work more than 15 hours per week of engaged time, occupational accident insurance, and protection against discrimination and sexual harassment. The measure does not provide unemployment insurance, workers' compensation, paid sick leave, or employer contributions to Social Security and Medicare.

The earnings floor is calculated on engaged time only, not total time worked. A driver who spends 10 hours logged into the app but only 6 hours on engaged time (waiting for requests accounts for the other 4 hours) receives the minimum earnings guarantee for 6 hours, not 10. A UC Berkeley Labor Center analysis estimated that the effective hourly wage under Proposition 22, accounting for waiting time, vehicle expenses, and the absence of employer-side payroll taxes, was approximately $5.64 per hour, roughly one-third of the state minimum wage. The gig companies disputed this analysis, citing their own calculations showing average earnings of $25 to $30 per hour before expenses.

## The Legal Aftermath

In August 2021, an Alameda County Superior Court judge ruled Proposition 22 unconstitutional, finding that it improperly limited the legislature's authority over workers' compensation. The California Court of Appeal reversed the decision in 2023, upholding the measure's constitutionality. The California Supreme Court declined to review the appellate decision, leaving Proposition 22 in effect.

The broader impact of the AB 5/Proposition 22 saga extends beyond gig work. AB 5 included numerous exemptions for specific professions (real estate agents, insurance agents, physicians, attorneys, accountants, direct sales representatives, commercial fishermen, and dozens more), carved out through intense lobbying by industries that did not want their independent contractor relationships disrupted. The exemption process revealed the political economy of labor classification: industries with effective lobbyists obtained exemptions, while industries without lobbying capacity (trucking, janitorial, construction subcontracting) were subject to the law's full application.

The trucking industry's challenge produced federal preemption litigation that reached the U.S. Supreme Court (California Trucking Association v. Bonta), which declined to hear the case, allowing AB 5 to apply to trucking. The application of AB 5 to owner-operator truck drivers has produced ongoing enforcement disputes, with the California Attorney General bringing actions against trucking companies that classify drivers as independent contractors.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California attempted the most significant labor reclassification in a generation, then allowed the affected companies to purchase an exemption for $200 million. Proposition 22 created a third category of worker, one who is neither a full employee with benefits nor a true independent contractor with entrepreneurial autonomy, but a platform-dependent piece worker with a minimum earnings guarantee that does not account for unpaid waiting time, vehicle depreciation, gas, insurance, or self-employment taxes. The 59 percent vote in favor was purchased by the most expensive ballot campaign in history, in which the gig companies outspent labor unions 10 to 1. The result is a workforce of hundreds of thousands of drivers who bear the full costs of vehicle ownership and maintenance, receive no employer retirement contributions, and earn an effective hourly rate that the UC Berkeley Labor Center estimates at one-third of the minimum wage. California invented the gig economy, tried to regulate it, and was overridden by the companies that profit from it.

The generous read is that Proposition 22 reflected a genuine preference among many gig workers for flexibility over traditional employment. Surveys conducted by the gig companies and by independent researchers consistently find that a majority of gig workers value the ability to set their own hours, work for multiple platforms simultaneously, and avoid the fixed schedules and managerial oversight of traditional employment. The 59 percent vote was not solely the product of spending; it reflected the votes of millions of Californians who use gig platforms as consumers and who understood that reclassifying drivers as employees would increase prices and reduce availability. The earnings debate is genuinely contested: the effective wage depends on assumptions about vehicle costs, engaged time definitions, and whether gig work should be compared to full-time employment or to the part-time, flexible work arrangements that many drivers prefer. Proposition 22 is an imperfect compromise. It provides a benefits floor that did not exist before and preserves a flexibility that many workers value.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

Neither the gig companies nor the state publishes comprehensive data on gig worker earnings, hours, expenses, or demographics. The companies treat driver-level data as proprietary. The state does not collect it because gig workers are classified as independent contractors who do not report through employer payroll systems. The estimates of effective hourly wages (from $5.64 to $30, depending on the source) are derived from surveys, driver self-reports, and modeling assumptions that are contested by all parties. The fundamental empirical question, what do gig workers actually earn after all expenses, is not answerable from publicly available data.
