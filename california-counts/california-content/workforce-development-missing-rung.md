# California Counts: The Missing Rung
## What 90,000 active apprentices, a $1.2 billion workforce development budget, 30 percent of jobs requiring middle skills, and a 4-to-1 return on apprenticeship investment reveal about the career pathways California has not built between high school and the university

California's workforce development system is a fragmented collection of programs, agencies, and funding streams that collectively spend approximately $1.2 billion per year preparing workers for employment. The system includes the Employment Development Department's labor exchange and unemployment insurance services, the California Workforce Development Board's oversight of 45 local workforce development boards, the community college system's career technical education programs, the Division of Apprenticeship Standards' registered apprenticeship programs, and dozens of state-funded job training grants administered through multiple departments. The fragmentation is the system's defining feature: a worker seeking training must navigate multiple agencies, eligibility criteria, and application processes with no single point of entry.

Registered apprenticeship, the training model that combines on-the-job learning with classroom instruction and produces workers who earn while they learn, serves approximately 90,000 active apprentices in California. The number has grown approximately 40 percent in the past decade, but the growth is concentrated in the building trades (electricians, plumbers, carpenters, ironworkers), which account for roughly 70 percent of all apprenticeships. The remaining 30 percent includes healthcare, technology, public safety, and advanced manufacturing, sectors that have been slower to adopt the apprenticeship model despite evidence of its effectiveness.

## The Middle-Skill Gap

Approximately 30 percent of California jobs are "middle-skill" positions that require more than a high school diploma but less than a four-year degree: registered nurses, dental hygienists, electricians, paralegals, IT technicians, medical assistants, and similar occupations. These jobs pay median wages of $45,000 to $80,000 per year, placing them squarely in the middle class in most California regions (though below the middle class in the highest-cost coastal areas). The supply of workers qualified for middle-skill positions has not kept pace with demand: California produces approximately 180,000 associate degrees and vocational certificates per year from its community colleges, below the estimated 250,000 needed to fill middle-skill openings.

The gap is particularly acute in healthcare (registered nurses, respiratory therapists, radiology technicians), skilled trades (electricians, plumbers, HVAC technicians), and information technology (cybersecurity analysts, network administrators, software quality assurance). These are the occupations that appear simultaneously on "most in-demand" and "hardest to fill" lists published by the EDD and the California Workforce Development Board. The demand is driven by demographic replacement (baby boomer retirements in the trades), industry growth (healthcare expansion under the ACA and Medi-Cal), and technological change (cybersecurity needs growing with digital transformation).

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has a $1.2 billion workforce development system that does not produce enough qualified workers for the jobs the economy offers. The fragmentation across agencies means that no single entity is responsible for outcomes, no single data system tracks whether training produces employment, and no single point of entry exists for the worker who needs training. The 90,000 apprentices are a fraction of the potential: Germany, with a population roughly double California's, has 1.3 million apprentices. California's apprenticeship system is dominated by construction trades unions, which operate effective programs for their members but have not scaled the model to healthcare, technology, or the service sector. The community college career technical education programs, which serve hundreds of thousands of students, operate independently of the workforce development boards, which operate independently of the apprenticeship system, which operates independently of the EDD. The system is a collection of programs. It is not a system.

The generous read is that California's apprenticeship growth of 40 percent in a decade, the expansion of apprenticeship into healthcare and technology through the California Apprenticeship Initiative, and the creation of earn-and-learn models in community colleges represent genuine progress toward a workforce system that provides alternatives to the four-year degree. The return on investment in apprenticeship, estimated at $4 in increased earnings and reduced public assistance for every $1 invested, makes it one of the most cost-effective education and training interventions available. The workforce development system, however fragmented, serves approximately 1 million workers per year through some combination of training, job placement, or supportive services. The trajectory is toward greater coordination, driven by federal Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA) requirements and state legislation mandating collaboration between agencies.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The EDD publishes labor market data, the CWDB publishes workforce program participation data, and the Division of Apprenticeship Standards publishes apprenticeship enrollment data. No single state data system tracks a worker from training through employment and measures the earnings impact of workforce development programs across all funding streams. The return-on-investment estimates for apprenticeship and other training programs are derived from academic studies, not from administrative data linking program participation to employment outcomes at scale.
