# California Counts: The Unfunded Mandate
## What $1 billion in annual mandate costs, a constitutional reimbursement requirement that the state ignores, 2,500 pending mandate claims, and the SB 90 process that turns local compliance into a decade-long fight for payment reveal about the mandates California imposes on local governments and the funding it promises but does not deliver

The California Constitution (Article XIII B, Section 6) requires the state to reimburse local governments and school districts for the costs of complying with state-mandated programs. When the legislature enacts a law that requires cities, counties, or school districts to perform a new function or provide an expanded level of service, the state must either fund the mandate or allow local agencies to opt out. The reimbursement process is administered by the Commission on State Mandates, a quasi-judicial body that determines whether a state law constitutes a mandate, calculates the reimbursement amount, and submits claims to the legislature for appropriation.

The process is, in practice, a multi-year ordeal. A local government that incurs costs from a state mandate must file a test claim with the Commission on State Mandates. The commission takes an average of five to seven years to issue a decision on the test claim. If the mandate is approved, the commission develops parameters and guidelines for calculating costs, which takes an additional one to three years. Local agencies then file annual reimbursement claims based on the approved methodology. The legislature appropriates funds for approved claims in the annual budget, but frequently defers or reduces appropriations, creating a backlog. As of 2025, the state has approximately $1 billion in unpaid mandate claims and approximately 2,500 pending claims at various stages of the process.

---

## The Workarounds

The state has developed several mechanisms to avoid paying mandates. SB 90 allows the legislature to repeal or modify the mandated program, eliminating the reimbursement obligation retroactively. The legislature can also suspend a mandate (declaring that local compliance is optional during the suspension period), which defers costs without reimbursement. And the state can "fund the mandate" by including the estimated cost in a block grant or property tax allocation, claiming that the general funding substitutes for specific reimbursement.

For school districts, Proposition 98 (1988) provides a separate mechanism: the state can claim that the Proposition 98 minimum funding guarantee includes mandate reimbursement, folding the mandate cost into the base allocation rather than providing supplemental funding. This effectively makes school mandates free to the state by defining the existing funding level as sufficient. Local governments have no equivalent mechanism and must either absorb mandate costs from existing budgets or defer compliance and hope the state does not enforce.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that the mandate reimbursement system is a constitutional promise the state breaks routinely. The five-to-seven-year processing time means that local governments absorb mandate costs for the better part of a decade before even receiving a determination that reimbursement is owed. The $1 billion backlog represents services that cities, counties, and school districts have provided at local taxpayer expense because the state imposed the requirement and did not pay for it. The suspension mechanism is particularly cynical: the legislature mandates a service, local governments build the infrastructure to provide it, the legislature suspends the mandate during a fiscal downturn (relieving itself of the reimbursement obligation), and the local government must decide whether to continue providing the service without state funding or eliminate a service that residents have come to expect.

The generous read is that the mandate reimbursement system, despite its delays and backlogs, establishes a constitutional principle that constrains state overreach. The system's existence forces the legislature to consider the fiscal impact of proposed laws on local government, and the Commission on State Mandates provides an independent adjudication of whether costs are legitimately reimbursable. The state has addressed the backlog through periodic lump-sum settlements (including a $1.2 billion payment in 2018-19 that cleared several years of accumulated claims) and has improved processing times through staffing increases and procedural reforms. The system is slow but functional: it provides a mechanism for local governments to recover costs that most states do not offer.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Commission on State Mandates publishes data on pending and decided claims. It does not publish a comprehensive inventory of all active state mandates, their annual costs to local government, compliance rates, or the total cumulative unpaid balance by mandate and jurisdiction. The question of how much the state owes local governments for mandated services (past, present, and future) is not answered by any published data source.
