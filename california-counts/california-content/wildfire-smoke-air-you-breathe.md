# California Counts: The Air You Breathe
## What 30 to 50 smoke days per year in fire-prone regions, a PM2.5 exposure that undoes decades of Clean Air Act progress, 1,200 to 3,000 smoke-attributable deaths per year, and an N95 mask as the new seasonal accessory reveal about the air quality crisis California's fire seasons create

California's wildfire smoke exposure has increased dramatically over the past two decades. Residents of the Sierra Nevada foothills, Sacramento Valley, Northern California, and parts of the Central Valley now experience approximately 30 to 50 days per year with smoke-impacted air quality (PM2.5 concentrations above the "unhealthy for sensitive groups" threshold of 35.5 micrograms per cubic meter). During major fire seasons (2017, 2018, 2020, 2021), smoke events have blanketed the entire state, including coastal areas that historically experienced minimal smoke. The August-September 2020 fire complex (multiple simultaneous fires burning over 4 million acres) produced the worst air quality ever recorded in San Francisco, with PM2.5 concentrations exceeding 200 micrograms per cubic meter for multiple days.

The health effects of wildfire smoke exposure are well documented and severe. PM2.5 particles (fine particulate matter with diameter less than 2.5 micrometers) penetrate deep into the lungs and enter the bloodstream. Short-term exposure increases rates of asthma attacks, heart attacks, stroke, emergency department visits, and death. Chronic exposure (repeated seasonal smoke over years) is associated with increased rates of lung disease, cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, and low birth weight. Stanford University researchers estimate that wildfire smoke exposure causes approximately 1,200 to 3,000 premature deaths per year in California, a mortality impact that exceeds the direct deaths from the fires themselves.

## The Progress Reversal

California has spent 50 years reducing PM2.5 pollution from industrial sources, vehicles, and power plants under the federal Clean Air Act and state regulations. The South Coast Air Quality Management District (covering the Los Angeles basin) reduced PM2.5 concentrations by approximately 50 percent between 1990 and 2015, producing measurable improvements in respiratory health, life expectancy, and childhood asthma rates. The Central Valley air districts achieved similar reductions, though from higher baseline levels.

Wildfire smoke is reversing those gains. During smoke events, PM2.5 concentrations in California's cleanest cities exceed levels that the EPA considers hazardous, erasing in days the improvements that decades of regulation achieved. Unlike regulated sources (which can be controlled through emissions standards, permits, and enforcement), wildfire smoke is uncontrolled: there is no permit to revoke, no smokestack to retrofit, no fuel to reformulate. The only "control" for wildfire smoke is preventing or reducing the fires themselves (through forest management, prescribed burns, development restrictions, and fire suppression), a set of interventions that operates on timescales of years and decades, not the hours and days that air quality emergencies require.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has created a seasonal public health emergency through 100 years of fire suppression (which allowed fuel to accumulate in forests), development in the wildland-urban interface (which creates ignition risk and requires aggressive suppression that prevents natural fire), and climate change (which produces hotter, drier conditions that increase fire severity). The smoke is the atmospheric expression of land-use and forest-management decisions that prioritized short-term development over long-term ecosystem health. The response (N95 masks, air purifiers, "spare the air" days, indoor air filtration) treats the symptom (bad air) without addressing the cause (too much fuel, too much fire, too much development in fire-prone areas). The air quality crisis will get worse, not better, as the climate warms and the fuel loads remain unmanaged.

The generous read is that California is the only state aggressively tackling the wildfire-smoke nexus through coordinated policy: increased prescribed burning (from approximately 30,000 acres per year in 2018 to a target of 400,000 acres per year by 2025), the Wildfire and Forest Resilience Action Plan, investments in forest health and fuels reduction (approximately $2 billion in dedicated funding since 2021), and building code reforms that harden the WUI. The air quality monitoring network (the most extensive in the nation) provides real-time data that enables protective action during smoke events. The public health system has developed smoke-event response protocols (school closures, outdoor activity restrictions, distribution of N95 masks and HEPA filters to vulnerable populations). The smoke problem is worsening because the fire problem is a century in the making. The solution, a restored fire-adapted landscape that burns at lower intensity and produces less catastrophic smoke, will take a generation to build.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CARB and the local air districts publish real-time PM2.5 data from monitoring stations. The data captures ambient outdoor concentrations but not individual exposure (which depends on time spent outdoors, indoor filtration, mask use, and personal activity level). The health impact estimates (1,200 to 3,000 deaths per year) are modeled from population exposure and dose-response relationships, not counted from death certificates. The actual health toll of wildfire smoke in California is not known with precision because smoke-caused deaths are not identified as such in vital statistics.
