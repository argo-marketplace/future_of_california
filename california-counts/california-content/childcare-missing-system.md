# California Counts: The Missing System
## What $22,000 per child per year, a 63 percent supply gap, 57,000 childcare workers earning $15.50 per hour, and a universal TK promise reveal about the infrastructure California never built for its youngest residents

Childcare in California costs approximately $17,000 to $27,000 per year per child for center-based care, depending on the age of the child and the region. Infant care, the most expensive category, averages approximately $22,000 per year statewide and exceeds $27,000 in the Bay Area and Southern California coastal markets. For a family with two children under five, childcare costs can exceed $40,000 per year, more than tuition at the University of California and more than the median annual rent in many California communities. Childcare is the largest single expense for many young families, exceeding housing in some cases, and is the primary reason that one parent (disproportionately mothers) reduces work hours or exits the labor force entirely.

The supply gap is equally stark. California has licensed childcare capacity for approximately 1.3 million children ages 0 to 5. The population of children in that age range is approximately 3.5 million. Accounting for children who do not need or want formal childcare (those cared for by a parent at home, by relatives, or in license-exempt arrangements), the estimated unmet demand for licensed childcare is approximately 63 percent of families who want or need it. The gap is widest for infant and toddler care (ages 0 to 2), where the ratio of licensed spaces to children is approximately 1 to 5 in many counties, and in low-income communities where the subsidy system that makes childcare affordable is itself capacity-constrained.

## The Workforce Crisis

California has approximately 57,000 licensed childcare center workers and an additional estimated 35,000 licensed family childcare providers. Their median hourly wage is approximately $15.50, barely above the state minimum wage of $16.00 and below the living wage in every California metropolitan area. Annual earnings for full-time childcare workers average approximately $32,000, placing them in the bottom 15 percent of the state's income distribution. Approximately 20 percent of childcare workers qualify for public assistance (Medi-Cal, CalFresh, or subsidized housing), meaning the state is simultaneously underpaying the workers who care for its children and subsidizing their poverty-level income through the social safety net.

The wage depression is structural, not accidental. Childcare is labor-intensive (ratios of 1 adult to 3 infants, 1 to 4 toddlers, 1 to 12 preschoolers are required by licensing), and the revenue source (parent fees and government subsidies) is constrained by what families can afford and what the state is willing to pay. The reimbursement rate for state-subsidized childcare, set by the Regional Market Rate survey, was historically set at the 75th percentile of market rates but was funded at lower percentiles for years. AB 92 (2021) and subsequent legislation raised the reimbursement rate and committed to reaching the 75th percentile by 2025, but the increase, while meaningful, brings rates to approximately $12,000 to $16,000 per year per child for full-time care, an amount that does not cover the cost of providing quality care at living-wage compensation for workers.

The result is a market failure. Parents cannot afford to pay more. Workers cannot afford to earn less. Providers cannot operate at current rates without paying poverty wages. And the state cannot or will not appropriate the subsidy required to close the gap. The childcare system runs on the willingness of workers, disproportionately women of color, to accept wages below what their skills, training, and emotional labor warrant.

## Universal Transitional Kindergarten

California's most ambitious early education initiative is the expansion of Transitional Kindergarten (TK) to all four-year-olds by the 2025-26 school year. TK, which was previously available only to children turning five between September and December, is being expanded to serve all children turning four by September 1, adding approximately 250,000 children to the K-12 system at full implementation. The expansion was funded in the 2021-22 budget at an estimated $2.7 billion per year at full implementation.

TK is administered through school districts, staffed by credentialed teachers (with an assistant in each classroom), and funded through the LCFF formula. The model has significant advantages: stable funding through Proposition 98, credentialed staff, integration with the K-12 system, and no cost to families. The disadvantages are equally significant: TK operates on a school-day schedule (typically 8 a.m. to 2:30 p.m.), which does not cover a full working day; districts vary in their capacity to provide before- and after-school care; the expansion competes for classroom space, teacher hires, and school facilities with existing K-12 programs; and TK serves only four-year-olds, leaving the 0-to-3 age range, where the childcare crisis is most acute, entirely unaddressed.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has no childcare system. It has a patchwork of programs, subsidy streams, licensing requirements, and market failures that collectively fail to provide affordable, accessible, quality care for the majority of children who need it. The $22,000 annual cost of infant care is a tax on parenthood paid disproportionately by women. The $15.50 hourly wage for childcare workers is an exploitation of care labor paid disproportionately by women of color. The 63 percent supply gap means that two-thirds of families who need childcare cannot find it. Universal TK is a significant investment that serves one age cohort for six hours a day and does nothing for infants, toddlers, or the hours between 2:30 p.m. and 6 p.m. The state that spends $135 billion on K-12 education has never treated the years before kindergarten with comparable seriousness, despite research showing that the return on investment in early childhood education exceeds that of any subsequent educational intervention.

The generous read is that California is building the early education infrastructure that no previous generation invested in, and the trajectory is positive. Universal TK will provide a free year of public education to 250,000 four-year-olds, the largest expansion of public early education since the creation of Head Start. The rate increases for subsidized childcare represent the most significant investment in the childcare workforce in state history. The Expanded Learning Opportunities Program, which funds before- and after-school care at Title I schools, provides the wraparound hours that TK alone does not cover. The Master Plan for Early Learning and Care, adopted in 2020, articulates a vision for a unified early childhood system. The system does not yet exist. The components are being assembled.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Department of Social Services publishes licensed childcare capacity by county. It does not publish real-time vacancy data: a licensed center with 50 spaces may have 5 openings or none, and the licensing data does not distinguish. The actual supply-demand gap is estimated from capacity data and demographic projections, not from direct measurement of available slots.

The data does not capture the quality of childcare. Licensed capacity includes everything from high-quality, accredited centers with degreed teachers to minimally compliant facilities with high staff turnover. The Quality Rating and Improvement System (QRIS), which assesses quality on a tiered scale, covers only a fraction of providers. Whether a child receives high-quality early education or custodial care depends on which provider their family can access and afford, a lottery that the data does not describe.
