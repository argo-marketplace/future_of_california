# California Counts: The Edge of Access
## What 1,100 miles of coastline, a 12-member commission, 1,500 permit decisions per year, and a 50-year fight over who gets to reach the water reveal about the public trust doctrine California enshrined in its constitution and enforces through a perpetual land-use war

The California Coastal Commission regulates development along the state's 1,100-mile coastline within a zone extending approximately 1,000 yards inland. Created by voter initiative in 1972 (Proposition 20) and made permanent by the California Coastal Act of 1976, the commission reviews approximately 1,500 coastal development permit applications per year, enforces public access requirements, and plans for sea-level rise, habitat protection, and coastal hazards. The commission has 12 voting members: six appointed by the Governor, three by the Senate Rules Committee, and three by the Speaker of the Assembly. It operates on an annual budget of approximately $25 million with approximately 200 staff.

The commission's central mandate is public access. Article X, Section 4 of the California Constitution declares that access to navigable waters is a public right. The Coastal Act requires that new development not interfere with public access to the shoreline and that development permits include conditions ensuring access (public trails, stairways, lateral easements along the beach). The commission has secured approximately 1,700 public access easements since its creation, though many remain unopened due to lack of funding for improvements, adjacent landowner resistance, or incomplete legal documentation.

---

## The Access Wars

The tension between public access and private property defines the commission's work. Coastal land in California is among the most valuable real estate in the world: median home prices in Malibu exceed $3 million, in Carmel exceed $2 million, and in La Jolla exceed $2.5 million. Property owners along the coast have strong economic incentives to limit public access to adjacent beaches, and they pursue that interest through permit conditions, private security, signage, landscaping that obscures access paths, and litigation challenging commission authority.

The commission's enforcement budget is approximately $3 million per year, sufficient to pursue approximately 150 to 200 active enforcement cases at any time. The backlog of unresolved violations (unpermitted development, blocked access, failure to comply with permit conditions) exceeds 2,000 cases. The most common violation is unpermitted construction (seawalls, fences, landscaping, structures) that blocks or impairs public access. The commission can issue cease-and-desist orders, restoration orders, and administrative penalties of up to $15,000 per day of violation, but enforcement actions average two to five years from initiation to resolution due to legal challenges.

---

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that the Coastal Commission is a regulatory body with a constitutional mandate (public access), a democratic origin (voter initiative), and insufficient resources to fulfill either. The 2,000-case enforcement backlog means that wealthy property owners can block public access for years with near impunity, paying fines that represent a fraction of the property value they protect. The commission's 12-member structure, with appointees from three different political authorities, produces inconsistent decision-making influenced by the political composition of the appointing bodies. The commission's $25 million budget to regulate 1,100 miles of the most valuable and contested coastline in the world is a resource mismatch that guarantees selective enforcement: some violations are pursued, most are not, and the selection is influenced by political visibility rather than systematic prioritization.

The generous read is that the Coastal Commission has preserved public access to California's coastline for 50 years against the relentless economic pressure of coastal property owners to privatize it. The 1,700 public access easements, the permit conditions requiring public amenities, and the commission's willingness to deny permits for development that impairs access have maintained a public coastline in a state where land values would otherwise have produced complete privatization. The commission is underfunded and its enforcement is slow, but the constitutional and statutory framework it enforces is the reason California's beaches remain public when comparable coastlines (the Hamptons, the French Riviera) have been largely privatized. The commission's existence, not just its actions, deters the most extreme privatization attempts.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Coastal Commission publishes permit decisions, enforcement actions, and access inventories. It does not publish a comprehensive assessment of which public access points are actually usable (open, maintained, signed, reachable by public transit), which are blocked or degraded, and which communities have adequate coastal access versus which are effectively excluded. The question of equitable coastal access, whether low-income communities and communities of color have the same practical access to the coast as affluent communities, is not systematically measured.
