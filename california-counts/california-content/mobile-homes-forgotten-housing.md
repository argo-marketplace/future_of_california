# California Counts: The Forgotten Housing
## What 500,000 mobile home units, 4,800 mobile home parks, a median resident age of 62, and a land-lease structure that makes homeowners into tenants reveal about the affordable housing California already has and systematically fails to protect

California has approximately 500,000 mobile and manufactured home units in approximately 4,800 mobile home parks, housing an estimated 1.2 million residents. Mobile homes represent approximately 4 percent of California's housing stock and constitute the largest source of unsubsidized affordable housing in the state. The median sale price of a mobile home in California is approximately $80,000 to $120,000 (versus $750,000 for a site-built single-family home), making mobile home ownership the only pathway to homeownership for hundreds of thousands of low-income Californians, particularly seniors, farmworkers, and workers in inland communities.

The defining feature of California mobile home parks is the split-ownership structure: residents own their homes (the manufactured unit) but rent the land beneath them (the lot or "space"). Monthly space rent averages $800 to $1,500 in coastal areas and $400 to $800 inland. The land is owned by the park operator, who may be an individual, a family, or increasingly a corporate investor (private equity firms and REITs have acquired approximately 20 percent of California's mobile home parks since 2015). The resident owns a depreciating asset (the home) on land controlled by someone else, creating a power imbalance that defines the mobile home resident experience.

## The Rent and Closure Problem

Approximately 100 California cities and counties have mobile home park space rent control ordinances, limiting annual rent increases (typically to 50 to 75 percent of CPI). The remaining jurisdictions have no rent protections, and space rents in uncontrolled parks have increased by 50 to 100 percent over the past decade in high-demand areas. Unlike apartment rent control (where a tenant can leave and find another apartment), mobile home residents who face unaffordable rent increases cannot easily move: relocating a manufactured home costs $10,000 to $50,000, and many older homes cannot survive transport. The resident's choice is often to pay the increase, sell the home at a loss (because high space rent reduces the home's resale value), or abandon the home entirely.

Park closures represent an existential threat. When a park owner decides to close a park (typically to redevelop the land for higher-value use), residents must relocate or lose their homes. The Mobilehome Residency Law requires park owners to provide 12 months' notice and relocation assistance (typically $5,000 to $15,000 per household, far below actual relocation costs). Approximately 10 to 20 park closures per year displace hundreds of residents, disproportionately seniors and low-income families. The closure process is technically regulated but practically devastating: residents receive compensation that does not cover their losses and face a housing market they cannot afford.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that mobile home parks are California's exploitation architecture: a housing form that sells the illusion of homeownership (you own the home) while delivering the vulnerability of tenancy (you rent the land, and the landowner controls your costs, your conditions, and ultimately your displacement). The split-ownership structure means that residents bear the costs of ownership (maintenance, depreciation, property tax on the unit) without the benefits (equity appreciation, which accrues to the land, not the structure). Corporate acquisition of parks has intensified the extraction: private equity firms purchase parks, raise rents to market levels (where not controlled), defer maintenance, and eventually close or redevelop. The 1.2 million residents in this system are disproportionately elderly, low-income, and have no alternative housing options in the California market.

The generous read is that mobile home parks house 1.2 million Californians affordably without a dollar of public subsidy, providing homeownership (however imperfect) to people who would otherwise be renters or homeless. The parks exist. They function. Their residents report high levels of community satisfaction (neighbor relationships, quiet, safety, green space) despite the structural vulnerabilities. Rent control ordinances protect approximately 60 percent of park residents. AB 978 (2023) strengthened disclosure requirements for park sales, giving residents first right of refusal to purchase their park as a cooperative. Resident-owned cooperatives (approximately 150 in California) eliminate the land-lease vulnerability entirely. The path forward is not eliminating mobile home parks but converting them to resident ownership, preserving the housing while eliminating the extractive structure.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Department of Housing and Community Development licenses and inspects mobile home parks but does not publish comprehensive data on space rents, rent increases, park ownership changes, park closures, or resident demographics. The mobile home population is largely invisible in housing policy discussions because it falls between rental housing (tracked by Census and HCD) and site-built ownership (tracked by real estate data). The 1.2 million Californians living in mobile home parks are statistically and politically invisible.
