# California Counts: The Runaway Production
## What a $330 million tax credit program, a 40 percent decline in on-location filming since 2005, 700,000 entertainment industry jobs, and a $72 billion annual economic impact reveal about the industry California invented and is losing to Georgia, New Mexico, and the cloud

California's entertainment industry, encompassing motion pictures, television, streaming content, music, video games, and digital media, employs approximately 700,000 workers and generates an estimated $72 billion in annual economic impact, according to the Motion Picture Association and the Los Angeles Economic Development Corporation. The industry is the cultural export that defines California's global brand: Hollywood is synonymous with the state in a way that no other industry-place association matches.

The industry is leaving. On-location film and television production in Los Angeles County, measured by permitted shooting days, has declined approximately 40 percent since 2005. The decline is driven by production tax incentives offered by competing states and countries: Georgia offers a 20 to 30 percent tax credit, New Mexico offers 25 to 35 percent, the United Kingdom offers 25 to 40 percent, and Canada offers federal and provincial credits that effectively subsidize 20 to 30 percent of production costs. The credits have transformed Georgia into the second-largest production center in North America (Marvel films, "Stranger Things," "The Walking Dead"), and New Mexico into a major production hub (Netflix's Albuquerque studios, "Better Call Saul," "Oppenheimer").

California's Film and Television Tax Credit Program, established in 2009 and expanded multiple times, currently offers tax credits of 20 to 25 percent on qualified production expenditures, with an annual cap of approximately $330 million. The program is competitive and oversubscribed: applications regularly exceed available credits by 3 to 4 times. The $330 million annual cap is dwarfed by the incentives offered by competing jurisdictions: Georgia's uncapped program distributed approximately $1.3 billion in credits in fiscal year 2023 alone. California's program retains some productions that would otherwise leave but cannot match the fiscal firepower of states that have made production subsidies a centerpiece of their economic development strategy.

## The Streaming Disruption

The shift from theatrical and broadcast production to streaming has changed the economics of "runaway production." Streaming companies (Netflix, Amazon, Apple, Disney+) produce content at a volume that exceeds what the theatrical and broadcast systems generated, but they are more price-sensitive on per-title budgets and more willing to produce anywhere in the world where costs are lower and tax incentives are available. Netflix's largest production facility outside of Los Angeles is in Albuquerque. Amazon produces globally. Apple's "Foundation" films in Ireland and Malta. The geographic freedom of streaming production, which does not require proximity to network headquarters or studio lots, has accelerated the dispersion of production away from California.

The AI and virtual production revolution adds another layer of disruption. The increasing use of virtual backgrounds (LED volume stages), AI-generated visual effects, and virtual production techniques reduces the need for on-location shooting. A production that would have required a week of location filming in Los Angeles can be shot in a virtual production stage anywhere in the world. The technology is locationally neutral, and its adoption favors jurisdictions where stage space, labor, and living costs are lower than California.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California invented the entertainment industry, then allowed other states to buy it away with tax credits that California cannot match and will not match. The $330 million annual cap on California's tax credit program is a rounding error compared to the $1.3 billion Georgia distributes. Every production that leaves California takes with it not just direct employment (actors, crew, craftspeople) but the ecosystem of post-production, visual effects, scoring, editing, marketing, and distribution services that is the industry's deepest competitive advantage. The runaway production phenomenon is a slow-motion hollowing of an industry that took a century to build and that no tax credit, however generous, can fully reconstruct once the ecosystem fragments. California is losing its signature industry to states that are willing to write larger checks.

The generous read is that California's entertainment industry is far more than on-location production, and the metrics that show "decline" capture only one dimension of a multidimensional industry. The management, creative development, financing, and distribution functions of the global entertainment industry remain overwhelmingly concentrated in Los Angeles. The streaming companies are headquartered in California. The talent agencies, law firms, music labels, and game studios that constitute the entertainment ecosystem have not relocated. And the $72 billion annual economic impact, while it includes the production that has left, also includes the digital entertainment, video game, and streaming platform operations that are growing. California's entertainment industry is evolving from a production-centered model to an intellectual property and platform model, a transition that the tax credit debate, focused on physical production, does not capture.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

FilmLA publishes permitted shooting days for Los Angeles County. It does not publish data on the total economic output, employment, or tax revenue generated by productions that remain in California versus those that relocate. The California Film Commission publishes data on tax credit recipients and their estimated California spending, but does not publish a counterfactual analysis estimating how much production would leave absent the credit.
