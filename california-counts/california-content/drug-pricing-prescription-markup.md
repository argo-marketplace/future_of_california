# California Counts: The Prescription Markup
## What $38 billion in annual drug spending, CalRx's state-label insulin, a 300 percent markup on generic drugs, and Medi-Cal's 14 million beneficiaries who need prescriptions filled reveal about the pharmaceutical costs California is trying to circumvent by becoming a drug manufacturer

California spends approximately $38 billion per year on prescription drugs across all payers: Medi-Cal (approximately $14 billion), private insurance (approximately $16 billion), Medicare Part D (approximately $5 billion), and out-of-pocket (approximately $3 billion). Drug spending has grown at approximately 5 to 8 percent per year over the past decade, outpacing both inflation and wage growth. The drivers are specialty drugs (biologics, oncology, immunology) that can cost $50,000 to $500,000 per patient per year and constitute approximately 2 percent of prescriptions but 50 percent of total drug spending.

In 2022, Governor Newsom launched CalRx, a program under which the state contracts with generic drug manufacturers to produce prescription drugs under a California state label and sell them at near-cost. The first CalRx product, insulin (biosimilar insulin glargine), became available in early 2025 at a price of approximately $30 per vial, compared to branded insulin at $100 to $300 per vial. CalRx does not manufacture drugs directly; it contracts with Civica Rx, a nonprofit generic manufacturer, to produce the drugs and distributes them through retail pharmacies. The program's initial product list includes insulin, naloxone, and fluticasone (a common asthma inhaler).

## The Pharmacy Desert

California has approximately 6,500 retail pharmacies, but their distribution is uneven. Low-income communities, rural areas, and communities of color have significantly fewer pharmacies per capita than affluent areas. Research by USC has identified approximately 700 "pharmacy deserts" (census tracts where residents must travel more than one mile in urban areas or 10 miles in rural areas to reach a pharmacy). The pharmacy desert problem intensified after 2020 as independent pharmacies closed (approximately 300 independent pharmacy closures between 2019 and 2024) due to declining reimbursement rates from pharmacy benefit managers, competition from mail-order pharmacies, and rising operational costs.

Medi-Cal pharmacy reimbursement is a structural driver of pharmacy closures in low-income areas. Medi-Cal reimburses pharmacies at the National Average Drug Acquisition Cost (NADAC) plus a dispensing fee of approximately $10 to $13 per prescription. Independent pharmacies report that Medi-Cal reimbursement does not cover their costs for approximately 20 to 30 percent of prescriptions dispensed, creating a cross-subsidy from privately insured customers that pharmacies in predominantly Medi-Cal communities cannot sustain.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that CalRx is a demonstration project, not a systemic solution. Three drugs (insulin, naloxone, fluticasone) out of the approximately 4,000 generic and branded drugs in common use represent a fraction of a fraction of California's $38 billion drug spending problem. The fundamental driver of drug costs, patent-protected specialty drugs priced at monopoly levels, is not addressed by CalRx because biosimilar manufacturing of complex biologics costs hundreds of millions in development and FDA approval. California cannot manufacture its way out of pharmaceutical pricing when the most expensive drugs are protected by patents, regulatory exclusivities, and manufacturing complexity that take decades and billions to replicate. CalRx is a political message (the state can compete with pharma) attached to a programmatic reality (the state label covers three cheap generics).

The generous read is that CalRx represents a proof of concept that state governments can enter the pharmaceutical market as buyers with manufacturing leverage rather than as passive purchasers accepting industry pricing. The insulin program alone could save California consumers and Medi-Cal approximately $50 million to $100 million per year if adopted at scale. The model can expand: Civica Rx is developing approximately 50 additional generic formulations, and CalRx's contract can add products as they become available. The combination of CalRx (cheap generics), Medi-Cal's Rx contract (bulk purchasing), and California's support for federal drug pricing negotiation (the Inflation Reduction Act's Medicare negotiation provisions) creates a multi-channel strategy for reducing drug costs. No single channel solves the problem. Together, they create pricing pressure from multiple directions.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Department of Health Care Services publishes Medi-Cal pharmacy utilization data (prescriptions filled, total spending by drug category). It does not publish data on pharmacy access by geography, the number of beneficiaries who fail to fill prescriptions due to access barriers, or the dispensing-fee adequacy for independent pharmacies. Whether Medi-Cal beneficiaries face medication access barriers comparable to the food deserts documented in nutrition research is not systematically measured at the state level.
