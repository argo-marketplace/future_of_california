# California Counts: The Aqueduct
## What 700 miles of canals, 34 storage facilities, 4 million acre-feet of annual allocation, and a 1960s-era system delivering water to 27 million people through a Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta that is sinking and may catastrophically fail reveal about the water infrastructure California built for 20 million people and now runs for 40 million

The State Water Project (SWP), approved by voters in 1960 and operational since 1973, is the largest state-built, state-operated water and power development and conveyance system in the United States. The SWP stores water in Oroville Dam (the tallest dam in the U.S.) and conveys it approximately 700 miles south through the California Aqueduct to 29 water agencies serving approximately 27 million people in the San Joaquin Valley, the Bay Area, the Central Coast, and Southern California. The SWP has a maximum annual delivery capacity of approximately 4.2 million acre-feet, though actual deliveries vary dramatically with hydrology: in wet years, the SWP delivers 60 to 80 percent of contracted amounts, and in dry years (2014, 2015, 2021), deliveries have been reduced to 5 to 15 percent of contract amounts.

The SWP is funded entirely by its 29 contracting water agencies (not by the state General Fund), which pay approximately $3 billion to $4 billion per year in fixed and variable charges. The charges cover the SWP's debt service (the original construction bonds), operations and maintenance, power costs (the SWP is the largest single consumer of electricity in California, using approximately 5,000 gigawatt-hours per year to pump water over the Tehachapi Mountains), and capital rehabilitation. The state water contractors pass these costs to their retail customers, adding approximately $200 to $500 per year to a typical Southern California household water bill.

## The Delta Vulnerability

The Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta, the hub through which SWP water passes from Northern California to the California Aqueduct, is the system's critical vulnerability. The Delta is a network of islands and channels formed by peat levees, many built in the 19th century by hand. The peat soils have subsided (compressed and oxidized) since they were drained for agriculture, and many Delta islands now sit 10 to 25 feet below sea level, held above water only by aging levees. A major earthquake on the nearby Hayward or San Andreas faults could cause multiple simultaneous levee failures, flooding dozens of islands and pulling saltwater from the San Francisco Bay into the Delta, contaminating the freshwater that the SWP and the Central Valley Project pump south.

The Department of Water Resources estimates that a catastrophic Delta failure would interrupt water deliveries to 27 million people and 750,000 acres of farmland for 12 to 24 months. The economic cost has been estimated at $15 billion to $40 billion. The Delta Conveyance Project (a proposed tunnel under the Delta to bypass the levee-dependent channel system) has been in planning for over 15 years, at an estimated cost of $16 billion to $20 billion. The project has been delayed by litigation, environmental review, cost escalation, and political opposition from Delta communities and Northern California interests who oppose additional water exports.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's water system is a mid-20th-century infrastructure operating on borrowed time. The SWP was designed for a state of 20 million people with a stable climate. It now serves 40 million people in a climate that is producing more extreme droughts, more extreme floods, reduced snowpack, and earlier snowmelt. The Delta, the system's chokepoint, is an engineered landscape whose physical foundations (peat soils, 19th-century levees) are deteriorating. The state knows the Delta is vulnerable (every assessment since the 1980s has reached the same conclusion) and has spent 15 years failing to build the fix (the Delta tunnel). California is running a system it knows can fail catastrophically, supplying water to 27 million people through a conveyance it knows is fragile, while deferring the infrastructure investment it knows is necessary.

The generous read is that the SWP is one of the engineering achievements of the 20th century, delivering water reliably across 700 miles for 50 years despite droughts, earthquakes, floods, and regulatory constraints. The system's flexibility (variable deliveries based on hydrology, conjunctive use with local groundwater, interconnections with the federal Central Valley Project) has allowed it to function even in extreme drought years. The Delta Conveyance Project, while delayed, is progressing through environmental review and may begin construction in the late 2020s. The SWP contractors' willingness to fund the project (through their water rates, not the General Fund) demonstrates that the beneficiaries of the system value it enough to invest in its modernization. The system is old. It is not broken. And its rehabilitation is underway, if slowly.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

DWR publishes SWP delivery data, contractor allocations, and Delta water quality monitoring. It does not publish a comprehensive risk assessment of the probability and consequences of a catastrophic Delta failure in a format accessible to the 27 million people who depend on the system. The public understands that water comes from the tap. It does not understand that the tap depends on 19th-century peat levees in a seismic zone.
