# California Counts: The Train That Time Forgot
## What $33.6 billion to $128 billion, 119 miles under construction, and a 2033 opening date reveal about the most expensive infrastructure project in American history

California voters approved Proposition 1A in November 2008 by a margin of 52.7 to 47.3 percent, authorizing $9.95 billion in general obligation bonds for a high-speed rail system connecting San Francisco to Los Angeles in two hours and forty minutes at speeds exceeding 200 miles per hour. The ballot measure promised a system that would cost $33.6 billion to build, begin operating by 2020, and require no ongoing public subsidy. Eighteen years later, no segment carries passengers. The estimated cost has risen to $128 billion in the most recent business plan revision. The opening date for a partial segment in the Central Valley, carrying trains at reduced speeds between Merced and Bakersfield, is projected for 2030 at the earliest, though the California High-Speed Rail Authority's 2024 project update report lists 2030 to 2033 as the operational window. The full San Francisco to Los Angeles system has no funded completion date.

---

## The Cost Escalation: A Timeline

The cost history of California High-Speed Rail reads as a study in progressive revelation. The 2008 business plan, the document voters relied upon, estimated the full Phase 1 system from San Francisco to Anaheim at $33.6 billion in year-of-expenditure dollars. The 2009 stimulus bill, the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, provided $3.3 billion in federal funds for the project, requiring California to begin construction in the Central Valley, where the terrain was flat, the right-of-way acquisition was relatively straightforward, and the political support was strongest.

The 2012 business plan revised the cost to $68.4 billion. The jump reflected updated engineering, changed alignment decisions, inflation adjustments, and the inclusion of costs previously deferred or excluded. The legislature authorized construction to proceed on the initial Central Valley segment, appropriating the bond funds and cap-and-trade revenues.

The 2016 business plan revised the cost to $64.2 billion, a number achieved through scope reductions and a "blended" approach in the Bay Area and Los Angeles Basin that would share existing Caltrain and Metrolink tracks rather than building dedicated high-speed track. The blended approach reduced cost but also reduced speed: trains on shared track cannot achieve the 220 mph design speed of dedicated track.

The 2018 business plan revised the cost to $77.3 billion. Then in 2019, Governor Newsom announced the state would "right-size" the project by focusing on the 171-mile Central Valley segment from Merced to Bakersfield, a decision widely interpreted as a soft acknowledgment that the full system was unaffordable. He later walked back the characterization, insisting the state remained committed to the full build.

The 2022 business plan revised the cost to $105 billion for the full Phase 1 system. The 2024 project update report placed the range at $100 billion to $128 billion, depending on scope decisions yet to be made. The Central Valley segment alone, the 119 miles currently under active construction between Madera and the Kern County line, is estimated at $33.2 billion, essentially the original cost of the entire system now assigned to one-sixth of the route.

## What Is Actually Built

As of early 2026, the California High-Speed Rail Authority has approximately 119 miles of active construction in the Central Valley, organized into construction packages that involve building the guideway infrastructure: bridges, viaducts, grade separations, trenches, and earthwork. The work includes 36 grade separations, meaning locations where the rail line passes over or under existing roads. Multiple construction packages are complete or nearing completion. The structures are visible from Highway 99 and from the agricultural roads of the San Joaquin Valley: concrete piers rising from almond orchards, bridge decks spanning irrigation canals, and cleared right-of-way corridors stretching toward the horizon.

What is not built: track, stations, signals, electrification, rolling stock, maintenance facilities, or any of the connecting infrastructure needed to actually operate trains. The current construction is the civil works, the bones of the system. Laying track, stringing catenary wire, installing signaling systems, and commissioning the electrical systems are separate contracts not yet fully awarded. The trains themselves have not been procured. The stations at Merced, Fresno, and Bakersfield exist as design concepts, not construction projects.

The authority has spent approximately $11.5 billion through 2025, drawn from a combination of Proposition 1A bond funds, federal grants, and California's cap-and-trade auction revenues. Annual cap-and-trade appropriations for high-speed rail have ranged from $500 million to $1.6 billion per year, making the project the single largest recipient of greenhouse gas reduction fund dollars, a fact that environmental groups have alternately praised as climate investment and criticized as diverting funds from more cost-effective emissions reductions.

## The Comparison Problem

China's high-speed rail network, the world's largest at more than 42,000 kilometers, was built at an average cost of approximately $17 million to $21 million per kilometer. Spain's AVE system averages roughly $25 million to $35 million per kilometer. France's TGV lines average $20 million to $30 million per kilometer. Japan's recent Chuo Shinkansen maglev, admittedly a more advanced technology, is projected at roughly $130 million per kilometer.

California's Central Valley segment, at $33.2 billion for 119 miles (192 kilometers), works out to approximately $173 million per kilometer. That figure includes only the flat agricultural terrain of the Central Valley, not the mountain crossings required to reach Los Angeles or San Francisco. The Pacheco Pass crossing into the Bay Area and the Tehachapi crossing into Southern California are the most geologically and engineering-challenging segments of the route and are not yet funded or under construction.

The cost differential between California and peer nations reflects multiple factors. American environmental review requirements under CEQA and NEPA add years and hundreds of millions in compliance costs. Prevailing wage requirements, project labor agreements, and Buy America provisions increase construction costs by 20 to 40 percent relative to international benchmarks. Right-of-way acquisition in California requires fair market compensation and extensive relocation assistance. Litigation by affected landowners, local governments, and environmental groups has added years to the schedule. The authority's own organizational challenges, including high executive turnover, consultant dependence, and changing scope and alignment decisions, have contributed to cost growth.

But the comparison to other American projects is equally unfavorable. The Northeast Corridor between Washington and Boston, the only existing high-speed rail corridor in the United States, carries Acela trains at average speeds below 90 mph on track shared with commuter and freight rail. Amtrak's Gateway Program, a two-track tunnel beneath the Hudson River in New York, is projected at $16 billion for 2.4 miles, or roughly $6.7 billion per mile. By that measure, California's per-mile cost is not anomalous for American megaprojects. The anomaly is attempting to build 500 miles of it.

## The Funding Gap

The authority's current capital plan identifies approximately $30 billion in available or committed funding for the project: $9.95 billion in Prop 1A bonds (of which approximately $4.1 billion remains unallocated), $3.5 billion in federal grants received to date, $750 million in additional federal funds from the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, and approximately $12 to $15 billion projected from future cap-and-trade revenues through 2030. An additional $3.4 billion in federal funding was awarded in 2024 for the Merced-to-Bakersfield segment.

Against the $100 billion to $128 billion cost of the full system, identified funding covers less than one-third of the total. The gap is approximately $70 billion to $100 billion. No plan exists to close it. The authority's business plans describe potential funding sources including additional federal grants, future bond measures, and private investment in the operating phase, but none of these are committed or even formally proposed. The state's general fund, running structural deficits of $30 billion to $45 billion in recent years, is in no position to absorb additional capital commitments of this magnitude.

The authority has argued that the project should be understood as a multi-generational investment, comparable to the Interstate Highway System, which took 35 years to complete and was funded through a combination of federal and state gasoline taxes. The analogy is imperfect. The Interstate System generated dedicated revenue from the moment the first segments opened, through the fuel taxes that funded it. California High-Speed Rail generates no revenue until it carries passengers, which it cannot do until a segment is complete and operational. The project's financial structure is front-loaded cost with back-loaded revenue, the inverse of the Interstate model.

## The Ridership Question

Proposition 1A included a requirement that the system operate without ongoing public subsidy. The 2008 ridership projections estimated 39 million annual riders by 2020 and 94 million by 2035. Those projections were produced by Cambridge Systematics using the authority's ridership model and were criticized at the time by the Legislative Analyst's Office and the Institute of Transportation Studies at UC Berkeley as optimistic.

The 2022 business plan reduced ridership projections to 28.8 to 44.6 million annual riders for the full Phase 1 system at maturity. An independent peer review commissioned by the legislature in 2023 suggested the lower end of that range was more defensible, particularly given the post-pandemic shift toward remote work and the competitiveness of airline travel on the Los Angeles to San Francisco corridor at current ticket prices of $50 to $150 one way.

The authority's revenue projections assume an average ticket price of approximately $86 to $93 for a one-way trip from San Francisco to Los Angeles. At those prices and the lower ridership projections, operating revenues would cover operating costs but generate limited surplus for capital cost recovery or debt service. The system would not lose money operationally, but it would not generate enough revenue to have justified its capital investment on a pure financial return basis.

The comparison to international systems is instructive. France's TGV was profitable within five years of opening its first line. Japan's Shinkansen generated operating surpluses from its first full year. Spain's AVE, despite lower ridership density, covers its operating costs. All of these systems, however, were built at one-fifth to one-tenth of California's per-mile cost, meaning their capital investment was recoverable at fare levels comparable to what California projects.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California High-Speed Rail is a monument to institutional dysfunction. The state asked voters to approve a specific project at a specific cost with a specific timeline, then changed all three while continuing to spend. The cost has quadrupled. The timeline has slipped by a decade or more. The scope has shrunk from a full system to a Central Valley stub that connects two cities no one would choose as the endpoints of a high-speed rail line. The $11.5 billion spent to date has produced concrete structures in the middle of farmland with no track, no trains, and no opening date. The project has consumed the majority of California's cap-and-trade revenues, diverting greenhouse gas reduction funds to a project that will not reduce a single ton of carbon until it carries its first passenger, a date that has receded with every successive business plan. The authority has cycled through multiple CEOs, multiple board chairs, and multiple strategic resets. The project is the textbook case of the sunk cost fallacy applied at state scale: too much has been spent to stop, but not enough to finish.

The generous read is that California is building something no other American state has attempted: a true high-speed rail system on dedicated track, designed to 220 mph standards, through one of the most litigious and environmentally regulated jurisdictions on Earth. The Central Valley construction is real and substantial: 119 miles of guideway infrastructure that will carry 220 mph trains once complete. The cost escalation, while enormous, reflects honest accounting that previous estimates lacked: real environmental compliance, real prevailing wages, real community mitigation, real inflation. The Merced-to-Bakersfield segment, when operational, will provide the first 200 mph passenger rail service in the Western Hemisphere, a proof of concept that could catalyze the full system build and federal investment in national high-speed rail. Every great infrastructure project in American history, the transcontinental railroad, the Panama Canal, the Interstate Highway System, exceeded its initial cost estimate and timeline. The question is not whether California High-Speed Rail costs more than projected. The question is whether, fifty years from now, a state of 50 million people connected by 220 mph trains will consider the investment worthwhile.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The authority's project update reports provide detailed cost estimates for individual construction packages, but they do not provide a transparent reconciliation between prior estimates and current ones. The reasons for cost growth are described in general terms, inflation, scope changes, market conditions, but the specific decisions that added billions are difficult to trace through the public record. The peer review process is extensive, with multiple independent reviews commissioned by the legislature, but the authority is not required to adopt reviewer recommendations.

The data also does not capture the opportunity cost. The $11.5 billion spent on high-speed rail, plus the approximately $12 to $15 billion in future cap-and-trade revenues committed to it, represents money that could have funded housing, transit, water infrastructure, or direct emissions reduction. Whether high-speed rail is the best use of those funds depends on assumptions about future ridership, future funding, and whether the full system will ever be built. If the project remains a Central Valley stub connecting Merced to Bakersfield, the investment case collapses. If it eventually reaches San Francisco and Los Angeles, the investment case may be vindicated. The data cannot answer that question because it depends on political decisions not yet made by officials not yet elected.

The numbers also omit the regional economic effects of construction itself. The authority reports approximately 13,000 construction jobs created in the Central Valley, a region with unemployment rates persistently above the state average. Whether those jobs represent net economic gain or simply displacement from other construction activity is debatable. What is clear is that the Central Valley communities along the route, Fresno, Madera, Kings, Tulare, and Kern counties, have experienced the disruption of construction, including property acquisition, road closures, and noise, for more than a decade. They have not yet experienced any of the benefits of operation.

---

The most expensive infrastructure project in American history has no trains, no passengers, and no funded completion date. It has 119 miles of concrete structures rising from the floor of the San Joaquin Valley, $11.5 billion in sunk costs, a cost estimate that has quadrupled in eighteen years, and a series of business plans that project a different future every two years. It also has something that no other state or country has built in the Western Hemisphere: the physical infrastructure for 220 mph passenger rail, under construction, in the ground, real. Whether California finishes what it started or abandons it as the most expensive white elephant in transportation history depends on a single variable that no spreadsheet can forecast: political will sustained across decades. The data can tell you what the project costs. It cannot tell you what it is worth.
