# California Counts: The Pendulum Swings
## What Proposition 47's reclassification of theft, Proposition 36's reversal, a 70 percent vote to re-felonize, and $950 as the most politically consequential dollar amount in California reveal about the criminal justice reform the public embraced and then rejected

In November 2024, California voters approved Proposition 36 with approximately 70 percent of the vote, the largest margin for a criminal justice ballot measure since the Three Strikes law of 1994. Proposition 36 reversed key provisions of Proposition 47 (2014), which had reclassified certain nonviolent offenses, including theft of property valued under $950 and simple drug possession, from felonies to misdemeanors. Proposition 47 had passed with 60 percent of the vote a decade earlier. The reversal, from 60 percent support for reform to 70 percent support for counter-reform, represents the most dramatic swing in criminal justice public opinion in California's modern political history.

Proposition 36 reinstates felony charges for serial theft (two or more prior theft convictions regardless of the dollar amount stolen), organized retail crime, and fentanyl possession in combination with a loaded firearm or large quantities. It creates a new category of "treatment-mandated felony" for drug possession, allowing courts to require substance use treatment as an alternative to incarceration, with the threat of a felony sentence if the defendant fails to complete treatment. The measure was supported by a coalition of retailers, law enforcement, and district attorneys who argued that Proposition 47 had created a de facto legalization of shoplifting by making theft under $950 a non-jailable offense that police did not prioritize. The opposition, led by Governor Newsom, criminal justice reform organizations, and public defenders, argued that re-felonizing theft and drug possession would increase incarceration without reducing crime.

## The $950 Number

The $950 felony threshold, established by Proposition 47, became the symbol of the reform's perceived failure. Under Proposition 47, theft of property valued at $950 or less was a misdemeanor, regardless of the offender's criminal history. Critics argued that the threshold created an incentive for serial theft: a person could steal $949 in merchandise, receive a misdemeanor citation (often not prosecuted), and return to steal again the next day. Viral videos of brazen retail theft, particularly in San Francisco, amplified the narrative. Organized retail crime, in which groups systematically steal merchandise for resale, was attributed (accurately or not) to the reduced penalties.

The data on whether Proposition 47 actually caused an increase in property crime is contested. UCR and NIBRS data show that property crime in California declined overall between 2014 and 2019, increased during the pandemic, and has declined since. Shoplifting specifically increased in some jurisdictions and decreased in others. The relationship between the $950 threshold and actual theft behavior has been studied by the Public Policy Institute of California, Stanford, and UC Berkeley with mixed results: some studies found modest increases in property crime attributable to Proposition 47, while others found no statistically significant effect. The public perception of a theft crisis, however, was not contested: polls consistently showed that 60 to 70 percent of Californians believed crime had increased, even during periods when crime data showed declines.

## The Incarceration Cost

The Legislative Analyst's Office estimated that Proposition 36 will increase the state prison population by approximately 3,000 to 5,000 inmates and the county jail population by an additional 5,000 to 10,000, at an annual cost of approximately $500 million to $1.5 billion. The cost includes incarceration ($130,000 per year per state prison inmate, $75,000 to $150,000 per year per county jail inmate), court processing, prosecution, and public defense. The treatment-mandated felony provisions will require additional investment in substance use treatment capacity, which is already insufficient in most counties.

Proposition 47 had generated approximately $100 million to $150 million per year in savings from reduced incarceration, which was deposited into the Safe Neighborhoods and Schools Fund and distributed to victim services, mental health, and juvenile justice prevention programs. Proposition 36's reversal will reduce or eliminate those savings, redirecting the funds from prevention to incarceration.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that Proposition 36 represents a triumph of narrative over evidence. The viral shoplifting videos that drove the ballot measure depicted a real phenomenon (brazen retail theft in specific locations) but generalized it into a statewide crisis that the aggregate crime data did not support. The 70 percent vote reflected public frustration with visible disorder, homelessness, and the perception that the justice system had abandoned accountability, rather than a careful assessment of whether re-felonizing theft and drug possession would reduce crime. The incarceration costs ($500 million to $1.5 billion per year) will consume resources that could fund the treatment, prevention, and intervention programs that actually reduce recidivism. The pendulum swung because the public experienced insecurity, and the ballot measure offered a simple answer (more punishment) to a complex problem (addiction, poverty, mental illness, housing instability).

The generous read is that democratic self-governance means the public gets to change its mind, and 70 percent is a mandate. Proposition 47's theory, that reclassifying offenses would not increase crime because the threat of a felony does not deter, was tested and, in the public's experience, found wanting. The $950 threshold may or may not have caused more theft. It clearly communicated that California did not take theft seriously, and the communication itself eroded public trust in the justice system. Proposition 36's treatment-mandated felony provisions attempt to split the difference: holding drug users accountable while directing them toward treatment rather than incarceration. Whether the treatment infrastructure will be built to support the mandate is an open question. But the voters' decision to prioritize accountability over the reforms of the previous decade was informed, deliberate, and overwhelming.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Department of Justice publishes crime data through the OpenJustice portal. The data does not isolate the causal effect of Proposition 47 or Proposition 36 on crime rates, because crime is influenced by dozens of variables (economic conditions, policing levels, drug markets, demographic shifts, weather, housing policy) that change simultaneously. The before-and-after crime rate comparisons cited by both proponents and opponents of Proposition 36 are correlational, not causal. Whether re-felonizing theft reduces theft, whether treatment-mandated felonies produce treatment, and whether the incarceration increase produces public safety returns commensurate with its cost are empirical questions that will take years to answer and may never be answered definitively.
