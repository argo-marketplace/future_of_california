# California Counts: The Circular Lie
## What a 42 percent recycling rate, 40 million tons of waste per year, China's import ban, and an SB 54 mandate to eliminate single-use plastics reveal about the recycling system California built on a market that collapsed

California generates approximately 77 million tons of waste per year, of which approximately 42 percent (roughly 32 million tons) is diverted from landfills through recycling, composting, and other recovery methods. The remaining 58 percent, approximately 45 million tons, is landfilled. The 42 percent diversion rate is below the state's longstanding goal of 75 percent diversion (established by AB 341 in 2011) and represents a plateau that has persisted for more than a decade. The rate peaked at approximately 50 percent in 2014 and has since declined, driven by the collapse of export markets for recyclable materials and the contamination of curbside recycling streams.

The collapse was triggered by China's "National Sword" policy (2018), which effectively banned the importation of most recyclable materials from the United States by imposing a contamination standard of 0.5 percent, a threshold that virtually no American recycling facility could meet. Before National Sword, California exported approximately 30 percent of its collected recyclables, primarily to China, where they were processed into raw materials for manufacturing. When China closed its doors, the materials had nowhere to go. Recycling facilities stockpiled bales of plastic, paper, and mixed materials. Some materials were diverted to landfills. Processing costs increased as domestic facilities absorbed volume they were not designed to handle. Municipal recycling programs, which had been revenue-positive when export markets were strong, became cost centers.

## The Bottle Bill

California's Beverage Container Recycling Program, authorized by the California Redemption Value (CRV) system, is the most visible recycling program in the state. Consumers pay a CRV deposit ($0.05 for containers under 24 ounces, $0.10 for larger containers) and redeem the deposit by returning containers to certified recycling centers. The program processes approximately 18 billion beverage containers per year, with a redemption rate of approximately 68 percent.

The recycling center infrastructure has been in decline. The number of certified recycling centers in California has fallen from approximately 2,500 in 2015 to fewer than 1,200, driven by rising real estate costs (recycling centers occupy land that landlords can lease more profitably to other uses), low processing margins, and regulatory compliance costs. The closures have reduced consumer access to redemption, particularly in urban areas where real estate pressure is highest. CalRecycle has responded with mobile recycling programs and retailer in-store redemption requirements, but the convenience gap between the declining recycling center network and the ubiquitous retail locations where containers are purchased is widening.

## SB 54: The Plastics Mandate

SB 54 (2022), the Plastic Pollution Prevention and Packaging Producer Responsibility Act, is the most ambitious plastics reduction law in the nation. The law requires that by 2032, all single-use packaging and plastic food service ware sold in California must be recyclable or compostable, and that the amount of single-use plastic waste must be reduced by 25 percent from 2023 levels. The law also establishes a producer responsibility organization funded by the packaging industry, with producers contributing an estimated $5 billion over 10 years to fund recycling infrastructure, litter cleanup, and the transition to recyclable packaging.

The law shifts the cost of managing packaging waste from municipalities and consumers to the companies that produce the packaging, a "producer responsibility" model used widely in Europe but novel at this scale in the United States. The implementation is complex: defining what counts as "recyclable" requires CalRecycle to determine which materials are actually recycled (not just theoretically recyclable) in practice, a determination that depends on the existence of end markets, processing capacity, and collection infrastructure that does not yet exist for many packaging types.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's recycling system was built on the assumption that someone else would process the materials, and when that someone else (China) stopped accepting them, the system revealed itself as a sorting operation, not a recycling operation. Curbside recycling collects and sorts materials. Whether those materials are actually recycled depends on whether end markets exist, whether the materials are clean enough to process, and whether the economics of processing are favorable. For many materials, particularly mixed plastics, the answer is no: the recycling symbol on a plastic container does not mean the container will be recycled. It means the container is made of a material that could theoretically be recycled if a facility existed to process it, if the material were clean, and if the cost of processing were below the cost of virgin material. For most mixed plastics, none of these conditions hold. California's 42 percent diversion rate includes materials that were "diverted" from landfills to recycling facilities and then landfilled anyway because no market existed for them.

The generous read is that SB 54 represents a fundamental rethinking of the recycling model, shifting from consumer responsibility (sort your waste correctly and hope someone recycles it) to producer responsibility (design your packaging to be recyclable and pay for the infrastructure to recycle it). The $5 billion in producer funding will build domestic processing capacity that reduces dependence on export markets. The 25 percent plastic waste reduction mandate will force packaging redesign that reduces the volume of materials entering the waste stream in the first place. California's recycling rate may be stagnant, but SB 54 changes the rules of the game: for the first time, the companies that create packaging waste are financially responsible for managing it. The transition will take a decade. It is the right structural reform.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CalRecycle publishes diversion rate data, facility throughput data, and CRV redemption data. It does not publish tracking data showing what happens to materials after they are collected for recycling: what percentage is actually processed into new products, what percentage is exported, what percentage is landfilled after collection, and what percentage is incinerated or otherwise disposed of. The "recycling rate" measures collection and sorting, not actual recycling. The gap between the two is the measurement that would reveal whether California's recycling system produces recycling or performs a sorting ritual that ends in a landfill.
