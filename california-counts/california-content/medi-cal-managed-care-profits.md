# California Counts: The Managed Care Margin
## What 14 million enrollees, capitation rates paid to 20 private plans, and 5 to 10 percent profit margins reveal about who benefits when California outsources its public health program

Fourteen million of California's 15 million Medi-Cal enrollees receive their care not from the state directly but from approximately 20 private managed care plans that contract with the state under a capitation arrangement: a fixed monthly payment per enrolled member, regardless of how much care that member actually uses. The plans take the money, assume the risk, and keep the margin between payments received and care delivered. In 2021 through 2023, several of those plans reported profit margins of 5 to 10 percent on their Medi-Cal business. The state was, in the same period, cutting some benefits to balance its budget. The math of managed care margins in a public program is not complicated. It is, however, rarely presented plainly.

---

## How Medi-Cal Managed Care Works

The capitation model was introduced in California Medicaid beginning in the 1970s and expanded substantially in the 1990s. The theory was straightforward: by paying a fixed per-member-per-month rate rather than reimbursing each service individually, the state could give managed care plans an incentive to keep enrollees healthy and to provide preventive care that avoids more expensive downstream treatment. A plan that prevents a hospitalization keeps more of its capitation revenue. A plan that manages chronic disease well avoids expensive emergency care. The incentive structure, in theory, aligns plan financial interest with patient health outcomes.

California's Department of Health Care Services sets capitation rates through an actuarial process, working with contracted actuarial firms to model the expected healthcare costs of the enrolled population and setting rates that, in the actuarial standard of adequacy, allow a well-managed plan to cover expected costs and maintain a reasonable margin for risk and operating expenses. The rates vary by county, by population category (children, adults, seniors, people with disabilities), and by service package. In aggregate, California pays its managed care plans approximately $75 to $90 billion per year in total capitation, funded by the combination of state general fund, federal matching funds, and various provider taxes.

California law requires that managed care plans spend at least 85 percent of premium revenues on medical care for enrollees, the medical loss ratio (MLR) requirement. The remaining 15 percent may be retained for administrative costs, quality improvement activities, and profit. Plans that fall below the 85 percent medical loss ratio are required to rebate the difference to the state. DHCS has clawback authority and has used it.

## The Profit Margin Question

The 85 percent medical loss ratio floor implies a maximum theoretical profit margin (before administrative expenses) of 15 percent of revenue. Actual profit margins vary substantially by plan, year, and population served.

Several of California's larger Medi-Cal managed care plans reported profit margins of 5 to 10 percent on their Medi-Cal business in 2021 through 2023. This range is derived from plan financial statements filed with the California Department of Managed Health Care, which require health plans to report revenues, medical expenses, administrative expenses, and net income. The filings are public but require combining data across plan affiliates to produce consolidated figures.

Centene Corporation, which administers Medi-Cal through its California subsidiary Health Net, is the largest single Medi-Cal managed care contractor. Centene is a publicly traded company that reports earnings quarterly. In fiscal year 2022, Centene's California Medicaid business (primarily Medi-Cal) contributed substantially to the company's nationwide reported profit, with analysts estimating California-specific margins in the 5 to 8 percent range. Centene's overall Medicaid managed care margins are disclosed in quarterly earnings calls at the national level; California-specific margins require estimation from partial data.

The L.A. Care Health Plan, which is the largest publicly operated Medi-Cal plan, serving approximately 2.7 million enrollees in Los Angeles County, is not a for-profit entity and operates with lower margins. Several county-organized health systems, which administer Medi-Cal in some counties, similarly operate on narrower margins than commercial plans.

The variation between for-profit commercial plans and county-organized or public plans is significant. For-profit plans have investors expecting returns. County-organized plans return surplus to the health system for reinvestment in services. Whether the capitation rate is the same or different across these plan types varies by county and by contract.

## CalAIM: More Capitation for More Services

The 2023 CalAIM (California Advancing and Innovating Medi-Cal) reform substantially expanded the scope of what managed care plans are responsible for providing and, correspondingly, increased capitation rates to fund those expanded responsibilities. Under CalAIM, plans now bear capitation covering Enhanced Care Management for high-risk enrollees, Community Supports services (including housing deposits, medically supportive food, and other social needs interventions), and broader population health management functions.

The additional capitation for CalAIM services added billions in total payments to managed care plans. For plans that successfully build the infrastructure to deliver the new services, the additional capitation represents revenue matched by real service delivery. For plans that receive the additional capitation but are slow to build the new service networks, the additional revenue may be flowing in before the corresponding services are flowing out, creating a transitional margin improvement that is not the intended purpose of the rate increase.

DHCS has acknowledged that CalAIM implementation is uneven across plans and counties. Some plans had robust community partnerships and were positioned to deliver Community Supports quickly. Others were building from near-zero capacity. The actuarial rates for CalAIM services were set based on projected utilization of the new services; if actual utilization comes in below projection (because plans have not yet built the capacity to deliver the services), plans collect the full capitation without incurring the projected costs.

DHCS's clawback authority applies to medical loss ratio shortfalls: if a plan's medical spending falls below 85 percent of capitation, the state can recover the difference. Whether CalAIM community support expenditures count toward the medical loss ratio calculation, or whether they are treated as administrative expenses, is a methodological question with significant financial consequences that DHCS has addressed in guidance but that advocates have identified as an area of ongoing ambiguity.

## Who the Plans Are

California's approximately 20 Medi-Cal managed care plans include a mix of organizational types that produce different financial dynamics.

Large commercial insurers, including Centene's Health Net, Anthem Blue Cross's Medi-Cal subsidiary, Molina Healthcare, and Kaiser Permanente, operate statewide or multi-county plans. These entities have shareholders or, in Kaiser's case, a nonprofit structure with substantial reserves. Their financial performance is reported in public filings.

County-organized health systems, including L.A. Care, Community Health Plan of Imperial Valley, and similar entities, are organized as public or quasi-public entities that direct surpluses toward service expansion rather than investor returns.

Small local plans, organized around specific geographic regions or populations, include entities like CenCal Health in Santa Barbara and San Luis Obispo counties and several other regional plans. These smaller plans are more financially constrained and operate with thin margins.

The plan market has consolidated over time. Several plans that operated in the 1990s and 2000s have been acquired, merged, or had their contracts terminated by DHCS. The surviving plans are on average larger and more financially sophisticated than the plan universe of a decade ago. DHCS periodically re-procures managed care contracts through competitive processes; the most recent reprocurement occurred in 2023 through 2024 in some regions.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California outsources the delivery of healthcare to 14 million of its poorest residents to a market of private plans, several of which are for-profit, and pays them capitation rates that produce margins of 5 to 10 percent while simultaneously imposing provider reimbursement rates so low that physicians refuse to accept Medi-Cal patients. The same system that struggles to ensure access to a specialist appointment is generating double-digit investment returns for Centene shareholders. The medical loss ratio requirement ensures that plans spend at least 85 cents of every dollar on care, but 85 cents of an actuarially derived capitation rate is not the same as adequate care for every enrollee: if the capitation is set too high, 85 percent of an inflated base still leaves room for margin that the state could have used to increase provider rates or improve benefits. CalAIM's expansion of capitation for social services adds new layers of complexity to the medical loss ratio calculation and potentially new opportunities for plans to collect capitation for services that are slow to materialize in practice.

The generous read is that the managed care model has enabled California to cover 15 million Medi-Cal enrollees at a per-capita cost substantially below what fee-for-service coverage of the same population would require, because plans have financial incentives to prevent expensive hospitalizations, manage chronic disease, and coordinate care in ways that the old fee-for-service model did not. The 85 percent medical loss ratio requirement is among the most stringent in the country for Medicaid managed care, and DHCS's clawback authority has been exercised. Plan profits represent a small share of the total capitation flow, and those profits are the compensation for bearing actuarial risk: when a major disease outbreak, a natural disaster, or a sudden change in the Medi-Cal population drives utilization above projections, plans absorb the loss rather than the state. CalAIM's expansion of managed care to social determinants represents the most ambitious redesign of Medicaid in any state and was possible precisely because the managed care infrastructure existed. The alternative, returning to fee-for-service for 14 million enrollees, would cost more and coordinate less. The question is not whether managed care is imperfect but whether the available alternatives are better.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

Plan financial statements filed with DMHC show revenue and spending at the plan level. They do not disaggregate by enrollee population, geography, or service type in a way that allows analysis of where, specifically, the gap between capitation received and care delivered is widest. A plan could be overpaying for services in one county and underproviding in another, and the aggregate financial statement would not reveal it.

The medical loss ratio calculation, while standardized, includes methodological choices about which expenses count as medical costs. Quality improvement expenditures, care management staff, and some administrative costs can be classified differently across plans, affecting the reported ratio. DHCS has issued guidance on classification but auditing every plan's classification choices is not routinely done.

DHCS does not publish a consolidated annual report on managed care plan financial performance in a format designed for public accountability. The data that would allow systematic analysis of profit margins across all Medi-Cal managed care plans is available in plan filings and in DHCS's own actuarial reports, but it is not assembled into a single publicly accessible summary. Researchers, advocates, and journalists who want to examine managed care plan margins must assemble that picture from multiple sources, a task that requires actuarial literacy and access to financial filings that are technically public but practically obscure.

---

Fourteen million people, twenty plans, capitation rates set by actuaries, and margins of 5 to 10 percent flowing to some of the plans at the end of each quarter. The managed care model is the mechanism through which California delivers healthcare to more people than it could cover any other way, and the mechanism through which private entities extract returns from a public program. Both things are true. The question that neither the financial statements nor the enrollment data can answer is whether the 14 million enrollees are getting the care the capitation was designed to buy for them. That answer lives in access data, utilization data, and health outcomes data that DHCS collects in fragments and has not yet assembled into a clear public picture. Until it does, the margin is known. The care it was supposed to produce is not.
