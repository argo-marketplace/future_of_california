# California Counts: The Other Debt
## What $20 billion in accumulated child support debt, 1.8 million open cases, a 60 percent collection rate, and a system that imprisons parents for the inability to pay reveal about the child support enforcement California runs as a debt collection agency when it was designed as a child welfare program

California's child support program, administered by the Department of Child Support Services (DCSS), manages approximately 1.8 million open cases and collects approximately $4 billion per year in child support payments. The program serves primarily low-income families: approximately 70 percent of child support cases involve a custodial parent receiving or formerly receiving CalWorks (cash assistance) or Medi-Cal. The program's dual purpose is to ensure that children receive financial support from both parents and to reimburse the state for public assistance provided to the custodial household.

The total accumulated child support debt (arrears) in California is approximately $20 billion, among the highest of any state. The arrears represent the gap between what was ordered and what was paid, accumulated over years and sometimes decades. Interest accrues on unpaid child support at 10 percent per year (one of the highest rates in the nation), and the interest itself constitutes a significant portion of the $20 billion balance. Approximately 60 percent of current support is collected; the collection rate for arrears (past-due amounts) is approximately 5 percent, meaning that the overwhelming majority of the $20 billion in accumulated debt will never be collected.

---

## The Poverty Trap

The child support system creates a particular trap for low-income noncustodial parents (predominantly fathers). Support orders are set based on imputed income (what the court determines the parent could earn, not necessarily what they do earn), and parents who lose jobs, become incarcerated, or experience disability do not receive automatic order modifications. The arrears accumulate during periods of nonpayment, interest accrues, and by the time the parent is employed again, the total debt may be tens of thousands of dollars. Enforcement tools include wage garnishment, tax refund interception, drivers' license suspension, passport denial, and civil contempt (incarceration for willful failure to pay).

Drivers' license suspension is particularly counterproductive: a parent who loses their license for nonpayment may lose the job that would enable payment, deepening the cycle of debt and noncompliance. Approximately 150,000 California drivers' licenses are currently suspended for child support nonpayment. The contempt power (jailing parents for nonpayment) is used primarily against parents who have the ability to pay but refuse; however, the determination of "ability to pay" is made by a judge in a hearing that many low-income parents do not understand and cannot afford legal representation to contest. The system cannot effectively distinguish between "will not pay" and "cannot pay," and the enforcement tools punish both equally.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's child support system has devolved from a child welfare program into a government debt collection agency. The $20 billion in arrears is largely uncollectible (owed by parents who are incarcerated, unemployed, disabled, or earning poverty wages), and the enforcement tools (license suspension, incarceration, tax interception) impoverish the noncustodial parent without enriching the child. When the custodial parent receives public assistance, child support payments are intercepted by the state to reimburse the public assistance cost, meaning the child receives nothing. The system extracts money from poor fathers to repay the state for benefits provided to poor mothers and children, cycling poverty between households rather than alleviating it. The 10 percent annual interest rate on arrears is a punitive policy that inflates uncollectible debt and creates a permanent underclass of parents who owe more than they can ever repay.

The generous read is that child support is a child's right: both parents have a legal and moral obligation to support their children, and the enforcement system ensures that obligation is met. The $4 billion collected annually provides direct financial support to 1.8 million children, many of whom would be in deeper poverty without it. DCSS has implemented reforms: the Compromise of Arrears Program allows parents who pay current support to have accumulated arrears reduced, and AB 2356 (2016) allows parents to petition for relief from interest on arrears. The system's fundamental challenge is that it operates at the intersection of poverty, family law, and public assistance, serving a population for whom the financial obligations the court imposes may genuinely exceed the financial capacity the economy provides.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

DCSS publishes collection rates, caseload data, and arrears totals. It does not publish data on the economic circumstances of noncustodial parents (income, employment, incarceration status, disability), the outcomes of enforcement actions (whether license suspension or contempt produces payment or deeper noncompliance), or the amount of child support that actually reaches children versus the amount intercepted by the state for public assistance reimbursement. The fundamental performance question, whether the child support system improves children's economic well-being, is not measured by collection statistics that track money movement between adults and government.
