# California Counts: The Algorithm
## What SB 1047's vetoed safety framework, 40 percent of U.S. AI companies headquartered in the state, 300,000 AI-related jobs, and the tension between regulating an industry and hosting it reveal about the artificial intelligence policy California has not yet decided on

California is home to approximately 40 percent of U.S. artificial intelligence companies, including the dominant firms (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta AI) and thousands of startups. Approximately 300,000 California workers are employed in AI-related positions (a definition that spans researchers, engineers, data scientists, product managers, and supporting roles). The San Francisco Bay Area alone hosts approximately 60 percent of global venture capital investment in AI. California is to artificial intelligence what Detroit was to automobiles in 1920: the geographic center of an industry that will reshape the economy, and the jurisdiction that must decide first whether and how to regulate it.

Governor Newsom vetoed SB 1047, the Safe and Secure Innovation for Frontier Artificial Intelligence Models Act, in September 2024. The bill would have required developers of large AI models (those costing more than $100 million to train) to implement safety protocols, conduct pre-deployment risk assessments, and maintain the ability to shut down models that pose catastrophic risks. The veto message cited concerns about the bill's scope (applying to all large models regardless of actual risk), its potential to drive AI companies out of California, and the absence of a federal regulatory framework within which state regulation could be situated. The veto left California without a comprehensive AI safety law, despite being the state where the most powerful AI systems are being developed.

## The Regulatory Landscape

In the absence of comprehensive legislation, California's AI regulation has proceeded through targeted bills addressing specific applications. AB 2013 (2024) requires disclosure of training data for generative AI models. SB 942 (2024) requires AI-generated content to be labeled with provenance information. AB 1008 and subsequent legislation restrict the use of AI in employment decisions (hiring, firing, performance evaluation) and require human oversight. The California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA) has proposed regulations on automated decision-making technology that would give consumers the right to opt out of AI-based profiling.

The enforcement landscape is fragmented: the CPPA enforces privacy-related AI rules, the Department of Fair Employment and Housing addresses AI discrimination in employment, the Department of Insurance regulates AI in underwriting, and no single agency has cross-cutting authority over AI safety, bias, or transparency. The absence of a lead agency means that AI regulation in California is reactive (responding to specific harms after they occur) rather than proactive (establishing safety standards before deployment).

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has chosen to host the most transformative and potentially dangerous technology in a generation while refusing to regulate it comprehensively. The SB 1047 veto was a capitulation to industry lobbying: AI companies argued that safety regulation would stifle innovation and drive companies to other states, and the governor accepted the argument despite the absence of evidence that other states were prepared to absorb California's AI industry. The targeted bills (transparency labels, training data disclosure) are disclosure requirements, not safety requirements. They tell the public what AI does without constraining what AI may do. California is allowing the AI industry to self-regulate during the critical window when foundational norms and practices are being established, repeating the pattern (social media, gig economy, cryptocurrency) of deferring regulation until the harms are entrenched.

The generous read is that the SB 1047 veto reflected a legitimate assessment that the bill was poorly designed for the problem it addressed. Regulating AI by training cost (a proxy for model capability) is imprecise: a $100 million model used for weather prediction poses different risks than a $100 million model used for bioweapons research. The targeted approach (regulating specific applications in employment, insurance, privacy) addresses known harms with proportionate interventions, rather than imposing a framework designed for hypothetical catastrophic risks that may not materialize. California's AI companies are subject to existing law (product liability, anti-discrimination, consumer protection), and the state's regulatory capacity is better deployed enforcing those laws against AI-specific harms than creating a new bureaucratic framework for an industry whose capabilities change faster than regulation can adapt. The correct federal-state division is federal safety standards (which the EU AI Act and potential U.S. legislation may provide) plus state enforcement of consumer protection and civil rights.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

No California agency tracks the deployment of AI systems in the state: how many AI models are in commercial use, what decisions they influence (hiring, lending, insurance, criminal justice, healthcare), what populations are affected, and what outcomes they produce. The fundamental question (what is AI doing in California today?) cannot be answered from any public data source because AI deployment is not reported, registered, or inventoried.
