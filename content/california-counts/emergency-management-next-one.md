# California Counts: The Next One
## What 6,000 declared disasters since 1950, a $3 billion Cal OES budget, 40 million people in multi-hazard zones, and a statewide alert system that 30 percent of residents cannot act on reveal about California's preparedness for the catastrophe that statistics guarantee

California is, by geological and meteorological measures, the most hazard-exposed state in the nation. It sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire's most active fault systems. It experiences the largest and most destructive wildfires in the country. Its topography channels atmospheric rivers into floods that can inundate entire valleys. Its coastline is exposed to tsunamis. Its droughts are the most severe and economically destructive in the nation. Since 1950, the state has received approximately 300 federal disaster declarations and thousands of state and local emergency proclamations. In an average year, the Governor's Office of Emergency Services (Cal OES) manages responses to 15 to 25 declared emergencies, from wildfires and floods to hazardous material spills and public health emergencies.

Cal OES operates on an annual budget of approximately $3 billion, of which roughly 70 percent passes through as federal disaster recovery funds (FEMA Public Assistance, Hazard Mitigation Grant Program, Emergency Management Performance Grants) to local governments and tribes. The agency's operational budget for state-level emergency coordination, the State Operations Center, the mutual aid system, and the California Earthquake Early Warning system (ShakeAlert), is approximately $800 million. The agency employs approximately 1,200 staff and coordinates with 58 county offices of emergency services, hundreds of city emergency managers, and thousands of first responder agencies.

## The Mutual Aid System

California's mutual aid system, formalized by the California Disaster and Civil Defense Master Mutual Aid Agreement (1950), is the backbone of the state's emergency response. Under the system, when a jurisdiction's resources are overwhelmed by a disaster, it requests mutual aid from neighboring jurisdictions, which are obligated to respond. If regional resources are insufficient, the request escalates to the state, which coordinates deployment from across California. If state resources are insufficient, the request goes to FEMA for federal assistance.

The system has been tested by every major California disaster and has generally performed well: mutual aid deployments during the 2017-2018 fire sieges, the 2023 atmospheric river floods, and the 2025 Los Angeles wildfires demonstrated the state's ability to move thousands of firefighters, law enforcement officers, and emergency medical personnel across jurisdictional boundaries within hours. The system's weakness is its dependence on surplus capacity: mutual aid works when some jurisdictions have resources to share. During simultaneous multi-hazard events (a major earthquake during a severe fire season, for example), every jurisdiction may be fully committed, leaving no surplus to share.

## The Preparedness Gap

Household preparedness for disaster in California lags significantly behind the state's hazard exposure. Cal OES surveys and academic research consistently find that approximately 40 to 50 percent of California households have an emergency supply kit, approximately 30 percent have an emergency plan, and fewer than 20 percent have practiced an evacuation route. The preparedness rates are lowest in low-income communities, communities of color, and communities with limited English proficiency, the same communities that are disproportionately exposed to hazards (flood zones, wildfire-urban interface, industrial areas) and disproportionately lack the resources (savings, transportation, social networks) to respond to and recover from disasters.

The Wireless Emergency Alert system and the Earthquake Early Warning system (ShakeAlert) can deliver alerts to cell phones within seconds of an earthquake or minutes before a flood, fire, or other event. The alerts assume that the recipient has a smartphone, can read and understand the alert (which is in English), has the physical and financial capacity to act on it (evacuate, shelter in place, move to higher ground), and has somewhere to go. For the estimated 30 percent of California residents who lack one or more of these capacities, the alert is information without action.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California manages emergencies well and prepares for them poorly. The mutual aid system, the Cal OES coordination capacity, and the state's first-responder infrastructure are among the best in the nation. The preparedness infrastructure, the systems that would reduce harm before disaster strikes, is underfunded and inequitably distributed. The communities most exposed to hazards have the least preparedness capacity. The state's disaster response demonstrates institutional competence. The state's disaster mitigation demonstrates institutional neglect: the seismic retrofits not funded, the flood levees not strengthened, the wildfire fuel not reduced, the evacuation routes not built, the community resilience plans not developed. California excels at responding to catastrophe. It does not invest comparably in preventing it.

The generous read is that Cal OES and the mutual aid system represent one of California's genuine institutional achievements: a coordination framework that has been tested by hundreds of emergencies and has never failed catastrophically. The ShakeAlert earthquake early warning system, deployed statewide since 2019, is the most advanced in the Western Hemisphere. The Listos California program, a culturally and linguistically targeted preparedness campaign, has reached millions of non-English-speaking residents. And the scale of the challenge, preparing 40 million people across 164,000 square miles for earthquakes, wildfires, floods, tsunamis, and pandemics simultaneously, exceeds the institutional capacity of any government. California does not have a preparedness problem. It has a multi-hazard problem at a scale that no preparedness program can fully address.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

Cal OES publishes disaster declaration data, mutual aid deployment data, and grant program expenditure data. It does not publish a comprehensive, publicly accessible statewide hazard exposure assessment that quantifies, at the census tract level, the combined probability of earthquake, wildfire, flood, and other hazard exposure. Individual hazard assessments exist (CalFire's Fire Hazard Severity Zones, FEMA flood maps, USGS seismic hazard maps), but no integrated multi-hazard assessment allows residents or policymakers to understand the total risk profile of a specific location.
