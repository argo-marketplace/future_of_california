# California Counts: The Other Epidemic
## What 3,500 gun deaths per year, a 37 percent decline in the firearm death rate, the strictest gun laws in the nation, and a 4.5 million concealed carry ruling reveal about California's war with its own gun culture

California experiences approximately 3,500 firearm deaths per year: roughly 2,300 suicides, 1,000 homicides, and 200 from other causes (accidental discharge, law enforcement, undetermined). The state's firearm death rate is approximately 8.5 per 100,000 population, well below the national average of approximately 14.7 and roughly half the rate of states with the least restrictive gun laws (Mississippi at 33.9, Louisiana at 29.1, Alabama at 26.4). California's rate has declined approximately 37 percent since 1993, when it was approximately 13.5, a decline that has occurred alongside the enactment of what the Giffords Law Center ranks as the strictest gun laws in the nation.

California has approximately 4.2 million registered firearms and an estimated 20 to 25 percent gun ownership rate (the percentage of households with at least one firearm), compared to a national average of approximately 32 percent. The state bans assault weapons (as defined by the Roberti-Roos Assault Weapons Control Act and subsequent legislation), limits magazine capacity to 10 rounds, requires universal background checks for all firearm transfers (including private sales), imposes a 10-day waiting period on all purchases, requires a Firearm Safety Certificate for purchasers, bans the sale of handguns not on the Department of Justice's roster of approved models, and prohibits the carrying of firearms in most public places.

## The Legal Battlefield

California's gun laws are under sustained legal attack following the U.S. Supreme Court's decision in New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v. Bruen (2022), which established that firearms regulations must be consistent with the nation's "historical tradition of firearm regulation" to survive constitutional challenge. The Bruen framework has been used to challenge California's assault weapons ban, magazine capacity limit, concealed carry restrictions, and the approved handgun roster.

The most consequential ruling to date is the Ninth Circuit's decision in May v. Bonta, which struck down California's restrictions on concealed carry permits. The decision required county sheriffs to issue concealed carry permits to applicants who pass background checks and complete training, eliminating the "good cause" requirement that had allowed sheriffs to deny permits based on the applicant's stated reason for wanting to carry. The ruling approximately doubled the number of active concealed carry permits in California, from approximately 250,000 to an estimated 450,000 to 500,000.

SB 2 (2023) responded to the Bruen decision by designating a list of "sensitive places" where firearms may not be carried even with a concealed carry permit: schools, government buildings, parks, places of worship, financial institutions, and other locations. The law was immediately challenged and partially enjoined by federal courts applying the Bruen historical-tradition test. The legal battle over the scope of permissible regulation continues, with cases working through the Ninth Circuit and potentially returning to the Supreme Court.

## The Geography of Gun Violence

Gun violence in California is not distributed evenly. Firearm homicide is concentrated in a small number of neighborhoods within a small number of cities. Oakland, Stockton, San Bernardino, Fresno, and specific neighborhoods in Los Angeles (Watts, Compton, South Central, parts of the San Fernando Valley) account for a disproportionate share of the state's approximately 1,000 annual firearm homicides. Within those cities, gun violence is further concentrated: a 2019 study of Oakland found that approximately 60 percent of shootings occurred in census tracts containing less than 15 percent of the city's population.

The concentration is racial. Black Californians, who constitute approximately 6 percent of the state's population, account for approximately 30 percent of firearm homicide victims. Latino Californians account for approximately 40 percent. The firearm homicide rate for Black males ages 15 to 34 in California is approximately 60 per 100,000, roughly 15 times the overall state firearm death rate.

Firearm suicide, which accounts for the majority of gun deaths, follows a different geographic and demographic pattern. Suicide by firearm is most prevalent in rural areas, where gun ownership rates are highest and access to mental health services is most limited. White males over 45 are the demographic group most likely to die by firearm suicide. The rural-urban divide in gun death is a divide in the type of death: urban gun deaths are predominantly homicides, rural gun deaths are predominantly suicides.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's gun laws, however strict, have not eliminated gun violence; they have reduced it to a level that remains catastrophic for the communities where it concentrates. The 3,500 annual gun deaths are the equivalent of a mass casualty event occurring continuously, invisibly, predominantly in Black and Latino neighborhoods that receive sustained attention only when a mass shooting makes national news. The legal framework that made those laws possible is being dismantled by federal courts applying Bruen's historical-tradition test, which treats 18th-century regulatory practice as the benchmark for 21st-century firearm policy. And the gun violence prevention infrastructure, the Violence Interrupter programs, the hospital-based intervention programs, the community organizations that mediate disputes before they become shootings, is funded at a fraction of the level of law enforcement despite evidence that community-based violence intervention is more cost-effective per shooting prevented.

The generous read is that California's gun laws work. The state's firearm death rate is 42 percent below the national average. The 37 percent decline since 1993 has saved an estimated 15,000 to 20,000 lives over three decades. The universal background check, waiting period, assault weapons ban, and magazine limit constitute a regulatory framework that reduces access to the most lethal weapons and creates friction in the acquisition process that studies have shown reduces impulsive violence (particularly suicide). The legal challenges are real but have not dismantled the core framework. And the state's investment in community-based violence intervention, approximately $200 million through the California Violence Intervention and Prevention (CalVIP) program since 2017, represents the largest state-level investment in violence prevention in the nation. California's gun violence rate is too high. It would be dramatically higher without the laws the state has enacted.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Department of Justice publishes firearm death data through the OpenJustice portal, including counts by county, type (homicide, suicide, accidental), and demographics. It does not publish data on nonfatal gunshot injuries, which outnumber deaths by approximately 3 to 1. The total burden of gun violence, deaths plus nonfatal injuries, is approximately 14,000 incidents per year, a figure that changes the scale of the crisis but is not captured in the mortality data. Hospital discharge data captures nonfatal gunshot injuries but is not integrated with DOJ firearms data in a publicly available format.
