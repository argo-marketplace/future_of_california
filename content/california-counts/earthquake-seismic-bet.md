# California Counts: The Seismic Bet
## What a 72 percent probability, 1.5 million unreinforced buildings, $5 trillion in exposed property, and a 13 percent insurance rate reveal about the catastrophe California has agreed to forget

The U.S. Geological Survey estimates a 72 percent probability that one or more earthquakes of magnitude 6.7 or greater will strike the San Francisco Bay Area in the next 30 years. The same models estimate a 60 percent probability for the Los Angeles region. The last magnitude 6.7 or greater earthquake to hit a major California metropolitan area was Northridge in 1994, which struck at 4:31 a.m. on January 17 beneath the San Fernando Valley, killed 57 people, injured more than 8,700, and caused approximately $44 billion in damage (roughly $95 billion in 2025 dollars). That was 32 years ago. In those 32 years, California added 8 million residents, built millions of housing units and commercial structures of varying seismic resilience, constructed critical infrastructure including the new eastern span of the Bay Bridge and retrofitted hundreds of highway bridges, and allowed its earthquake insurance participation rate to decline from roughly 33 percent of homeowners in 1996 to approximately 13 percent today. The state has simultaneously become more prepared and less insured, more structurally resilient in new construction and more vulnerable in its existing building stock, more scientifically informed about the risk and more complacent about acting on that information.

---

## The Faults: A Catalog of Certainty

California sits astride the boundary between the Pacific and North American tectonic plates, which grind past each other at approximately 46 millimeters per year. The San Andreas Fault, the primary plate boundary, runs 800 miles from the Salton Sea to Cape Mendocino and is capable of producing earthquakes exceeding magnitude 8.0. The last great rupture of the southern San Andreas was in 1857 (estimated magnitude 7.9 at Fort Tejon). The last great rupture of the northern segment was in 1906 (magnitude 7.9, which destroyed much of San Francisco and killed an estimated 3,000 people). Both segments have accumulated sufficient strain for major rupture.

The San Andreas is the most famous but not the most immediately threatening fault for California's major cities. The Hayward Fault runs directly beneath the East Bay cities of Oakland, Berkeley, Hayward, and Fremont, through some of the densest urban development in Northern California. The USGS considers it one of the most dangerous faults in the nation. Its last major rupture was in 1868 (estimated magnitude 6.8 to 7.0). The probability of a magnitude 6.7 or greater earthquake on the Hayward Fault in the next 30 years is approximately 33 percent.

In Southern California, the Puente Hills Thrust Fault lies directly beneath downtown Los Angeles, invisible at the surface. A 2005 study estimated that a magnitude 7.5 earthquake on the Puente Hills fault could kill 3,000 to 18,000 people and cause $250 billion in damage, making it the costliest disaster scenario in American history. The Hollywood Fault, the Santa Monica Fault, the Newport-Inglewood Fault, and the Elsinore Fault each present significant hazards to densely populated areas.

The Cascadia Subduction Zone, which runs from Cape Mendocino northward through Oregon and Washington, is capable of producing magnitude 9.0 or greater earthquakes. A full Cascadia rupture would generate a tsunami affecting the Northern California coast within 15 to 20 minutes. The last full rupture was in 1700, documented by Japanese tsunami records and Indigenous oral histories. The estimated recurrence interval is 200 to 600 years.

The seismic hazard is not a prediction. It is an actuarial certainty distributed across a range of possible events. The question is not whether a major earthquake will strike a California city. It is which city, which fault, and whether the buildings, bridges, water systems, and hospitals will perform as designed when it does.

## The Building Stock: What Stands and What Falls

California's seismic building codes are the most stringent in the nation, and they have been progressively strengthened after each major earthquake: the 1933 Long Beach earthquake led to the Field Act (requiring seismic standards for schools), the 1971 Sylmar earthquake led to major code revisions, the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake exposed vulnerabilities in soft-story buildings and unreinforced masonry, and the 1994 Northridge earthquake revealed failures in steel moment-frame connections. Each earthquake taught lessons that were incorporated into the next code cycle.

The problem is that building codes apply to new construction. California's existing building stock includes an estimated 1.5 million buildings that predate modern seismic codes, many of which are vulnerable to collapse or severe damage in a major earthquake. The most dangerous categories include:

Unreinforced masonry buildings (URMs): approximately 26,000 statewide, concentrated in historic downtowns and older commercial districts. URMs are brick or stone buildings without steel or concrete reinforcement. They are the most likely to collapse in a strong earthquake. The 1933 Long Beach earthquake destroyed hundreds of URMs and prompted the first seismic code. Ninety-three years later, thousands remain standing, many serving as housing, shops, and restaurants in communities that value their historic character.

Soft-story buildings: multi-story wood-frame buildings with large openings on the ground floor (parking garages, storefronts) that create structural weakness. An estimated 16,800 soft-story apartment buildings exist in Los Angeles alone, housing hundreds of thousands of renters. San Francisco had approximately 6,000 before its mandatory retrofit ordinance, adopted in 2013, required strengthening. The San Francisco program has achieved approximately 70 percent compliance. Los Angeles adopted a similar ordinance in 2015, with compliance rates improving but not yet universal.

Non-ductile concrete buildings: reinforced concrete structures built before 1980 that lack the ductile detailing required to flex rather than shatter during shaking. An estimated 1,500 non-ductile concrete buildings exist in Los Angeles, including hospitals, offices, and residential towers. The City of Los Angeles included non-ductile concrete in its 2015 seismic retrofit ordinance, giving building owners 25 years to comply, a timeline that extends to 2040.

The retrofitting cost is substantial. Soft-story retrofits average $60,000 to $130,000 per building. URM retrofits range from $100,000 to $500,000 or more. Non-ductile concrete retrofits can exceed $1 million per building. The total cost of retrofitting California's vulnerable building stock is estimated at $15 billion to $30 billion, a figure that no level of government has committed to funding. Building owners bear the cost, which they pass to tenants through rent increases where rent control permits it, or defer indefinitely where it does not.

## The Insurance Gap

The California Earthquake Authority, a publicly managed but privately funded organization created in 1996 after the Northridge earthquake nearly bankrupted the private insurance market, provides earthquake insurance to California homeowners and renters. The CEA's current take-up rate is approximately 13 percent: only one in eight California homeowners carries earthquake coverage. The rate has declined from approximately 33 percent in the years immediately following Northridge to its current level, a decline driven by premium costs, high deductibles, and the fading memory of the last damaging earthquake.

CEA policies carry deductibles of 5 to 25 percent of the dwelling value. For a home insured at $800,000, a 15 percent deductible means the first $120,000 of earthquake damage is paid by the homeowner. Premiums range from $400 to $3,000 or more per year depending on location, construction type, and foundation. The cost-deductible combination has proven unattractive to many homeowners who perceive earthquake risk as low or believe they can self-insure.

The CEA's claim-paying capacity is approximately $22 billion, funded by policyholder premiums, reserves, reinsurance, and a post-event assessment mechanism. The USGS ShakeOut scenario, which models a magnitude 7.8 rupture on the southern San Andreas, estimates total losses of $200 billion to $300 billion. A Hayward Fault scenario produces similar estimates for the Bay Area. The CEA's $22 billion capacity would cover roughly 8 to 11 percent of projected losses, and only for the 13 percent of homeowners who carry policies. The remaining 87 percent of homeowners would receive nothing from insurance and would depend on FEMA disaster assistance (maximum of $42,500 per household for housing), SBA disaster loans, or personal savings.

The arithmetic is bleak. In a major earthquake, the majority of affected homeowners will have no earthquake insurance, will owe their full mortgage on a damaged or destroyed home, will receive federal assistance insufficient to rebuild, and will face a housing market in which thousands of comparable homes are simultaneously damaged. The economic literature on post-earthquake recovery consistently finds that uninsured homeowners are the slowest to recover, the most likely to default on their mortgages, and the most likely to leave the region permanently. The 13 percent insurance rate is not a policy failure that California is unaware of. It is a collective decision to accept catastrophic financial risk rather than pay annual premiums to mitigate it. The decision is rational at the individual level (the premium is expensive and the earthquake may not come in one's lifetime) and catastrophic at the aggregate level (when the earthquake does come, the uninsured majority will overwhelm every recovery mechanism the state possesses).

## The Lifeline Systems

Buildings kill people. But the systems that sustain a city after an earthquake, water, power, gas, transportation, communications, and hospitals, determine whether the damage is a disaster or a civilization-level disruption. California's lifeline infrastructure is a patchwork of ages, conditions, and seismic readiness.

Water: The Hetch Hetchy system delivers water from Yosemite to 2.7 million Bay Area residents through 167 miles of pipeline, portions of which cross the Hayward and San Andreas faults. The San Francisco Public Utilities Commission has spent $4.8 billion on seismic upgrades since 2002 and projects completion by 2028. The Los Angeles Department of Water and Power operates 7,300 miles of water mains, many installed in the early 20th century from cast iron that is brittle and vulnerable to ground deformation. The 2025 Palisades fire revealed that even without an earthquake, the water system could not maintain pressure under extreme demand. In a major earthquake, analysts project that 500 to 1,500 water main breaks could occur simultaneously across Los Angeles, leaving large areas without water for fire suppression or drinking for days to weeks.

Natural gas: Southern California Gas Company operates 6,700 miles of transmission pipeline and 101,000 miles of distribution lines. Gas line breaks in earthquakes ignite fires that cannot be suppressed because the water system is simultaneously damaged. The 1906 San Francisco earthquake's casualties were overwhelmingly caused not by building collapse but by the fires that burned for three days after water mains broke. The 1994 Northridge earthquake caused 100 gas-fed fires. Modern automatic gas shutoff valves, required on new California construction since 2015, mitigate this risk for individual buildings but do not address distribution system ruptures.

Hospitals: California's seismic hospital safety law, SB 1953 (1994), required all hospitals to meet structural performance standards by 2030, a deadline that has been extended twice and now stands at 2035. As of 2024, approximately 400 hospital buildings statewide have not yet met the current structural standard. The cost of compliance for the remaining buildings is estimated at $34 billion to $143 billion, depending on whether hospitals choose to retrofit or rebuild. Some hospitals have announced closures rather than invest in compliance, particularly in rural areas where operating margins are already thin.

Transportation: The Caltrans seismic retrofit program, launched after the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake collapsed a section of the Cypress Street Viaduct in Oakland (killing 42 people) and a span of the Bay Bridge, has retrofitted more than 2,200 state-owned bridges at a cost exceeding $12 billion. The new eastern span of the Bay Bridge, completed in 2013 at a cost of $6.5 billion, was designed to the most advanced seismic standards of any bridge in the world. The BART transbay tube, which carries 150,000 daily riders beneath San Francisco Bay, was retrofitted in the 1990s and is assessed as likely to survive a major earthquake, though with potential flooding from damaged joints.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has known for decades that a catastrophic earthquake is statistically inevitable and has responded with a strategy best described as informed complacency. Only 13 percent of homeowners carry earthquake insurance. An estimated 1.5 million buildings predate modern seismic codes. Retrofit mandates exist in San Francisco and Los Angeles but cover a fraction of vulnerable buildings and allow compliance timelines stretching decades into the future. Approximately 400 hospital buildings have not met seismic safety standards 30 years after the law was enacted. The water and gas systems that will determine whether earthquake damage becomes earthquake catastrophe are partially upgraded and partially decrepit. The state conducts elaborate ShakeOut drills, publishes detailed hazard maps, and funds world-class seismological research, then watches as its earthquake insurance rate declines year after year because the last bad earthquake was 32 years ago and human memory is shorter than a fault's recharge cycle. California is the most seismically informed jurisdiction on Earth. It is not the most seismically prepared.

The generous read is that California has invested more in seismic safety than any other earthquake-prone region in human history. The building codes adopted after each major earthquake have demonstrably saved lives: the 1994 Northridge earthquake (magnitude 6.7) killed 57 people, while the 1988 Armenia earthquake (magnitude 6.8, comparable construction density) killed 25,000. The difference is building codes. The Caltrans retrofit program has strengthened 2,200 bridges. The new Bay Bridge eastern span is an engineering marvel. The SFPUC's $4.8 billion water system upgrade will ensure water delivery across the Hayward Fault. The soft-story retrofit ordinances in San Francisco and Los Angeles are the most aggressive mandatory seismic retrofit programs in the nation. Earthquake early warning, delivered through the ShakeAlert system and pushed to every smartphone in California, provides seconds to tens of seconds of warning before strong shaking arrives, enough time to take cover, slow trains, and shut down industrial processes. California cannot prevent earthquakes. What it has done, imperfectly but at massive scale, is ensure that its modern buildings will not collapse, its bridges will not fall, and its early warning system will give its residents a few precious seconds that no previous generation had.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The USGS publishes earthquake probabilities, fault maps, and shaking hazard assessments. CalOES publishes preparedness guidelines and ShakeOut participation statistics. What no agency publishes is a comprehensive inventory of California's seismic vulnerability: a building-by-building, system-by-system assessment of what will fail and what will survive in a given scenario earthquake. Individual studies exist for specific building types, specific infrastructure systems, and specific geographic areas. A unified assessment does not.

The data does not capture the cascading effects that turn an earthquake into a prolonged crisis. The USGS HayWired scenario, which models a magnitude 7.0 Hayward Fault earthquake, projects not only immediate building damage and casualties but also long-term economic effects: 400,000 displaced residents, $82 billion in economic losses, months-long disruption to water and transportation, and a regional recovery period measured in years. These cascading effects, where infrastructure failures compound each other (no water means no fire suppression means uncontrolled fires means additional building losses), are the gap between earthquake damage and earthquake catastrophe.

The data also does not capture the behavioral dimension. Despite living in the most seismically active state in the contiguous United States, surveys consistently find that fewer than 50 percent of California households have an earthquake emergency kit, fewer than 30 percent have a family communication plan, and fewer than 20 percent have secured heavy furniture and water heaters. The gap between seismic knowledge and seismic action, between knowing that an earthquake is likely and doing something about it, is the most consequential data point that no dataset measures.

---

California's relationship with earthquakes is a study in temporal discounting: the tendency to treat future costs as less real than present ones. The premiums are due now. The earthquake is due later. The retrofit costs money today. The collapse costs money on a day that has not arrived. Every year that passes without a major earthquake reinforces the perception that the risk is abstract, even as the probability accumulates. The USGS's 72 percent figure for the Bay Area is not a warning. It is a countdown. Somewhere beneath the cities, the strain is building at 46 millimeters per year, the same rate it has built for centuries, and the faults do not know or care what the insurance participation rate is, what the retrofit compliance rate is, or how long it has been since the last one. The earthquake will come on its own schedule. The only variable California controls is whether, when it arrives, the buildings stand, the water flows, the hospitals function, and the people are insured. On each of those questions, the answer is: some of them. Not enough of them. And less than the state knows it needs.
