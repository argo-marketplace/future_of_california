# California Counts: The Base
## What $70 billion in annual defense spending, 30 major military installations, 160,000 active-duty personnel, and a defense-industrial complex that builds satellites and submarines reveal about the military economy California depends on and rarely discusses

California receives approximately $70 billion per year in Department of Defense spending, the most of any state. The spending includes approximately $40 billion in defense contracts (awarded to defense manufacturers, technology companies, and service providers), approximately $15 billion in military personnel costs (pay and benefits for active-duty service members stationed in California), approximately $10 billion in operations and maintenance (base operations, training, facilities), and approximately $5 billion in military construction and other categories.

The state hosts approximately 30 major military installations, including Naval Base San Diego (the largest naval base on the West Coast, home port for approximately 60 ships), Marine Corps Base Camp Pendleton (the Marine Corps' largest West Coast expeditionary base), Edwards Air Force Base (flight test center), Naval Air Weapons Station China Lake (weapons research and testing), Beale Air Force Base (reconnaissance), Travis Air Force Base (airlift), and Vandenberg Space Force Base (space launch). Approximately 160,000 active-duty military personnel are stationed in California, along with approximately 50,000 civilian DOD employees and approximately 200,000 defense-industry contractor employees.

## The Defense-Industrial Base

California's defense industry is concentrated in aerospace, electronics, shipbuilding, cybersecurity, and space systems. Major defense contractors with significant California operations include Northrop Grumman (Los Angeles, approximately 20,000 employees), Lockheed Martin (Palmdale, Sunnyvale, approximately 15,000), Raytheon (El Segundo, approximately 10,000), General Atomics (San Diego, approximately 8,000), and dozens of smaller firms. The state's defense-technology sector overlaps with its commercial technology sector: many Silicon Valley firms (Google, Apple, Palantir, Anduril) hold defense contracts, and the boundary between defense and commercial technology is increasingly blurred in areas like artificial intelligence, autonomous systems, satellite communications, and cybersecurity.

The geographic concentration of defense spending creates regional economic dependencies. San Diego County receives approximately $25 billion in annual defense spending (military bases plus defense contracts), representing approximately 10 percent of the county's GDP. The Antelope Valley (Palmdale, Lancaster) depends heavily on aerospace manufacturing. The China Lake/Ridgecrest area is economically dominated by the naval weapons station. In these regions, defense spending is not one sector among many; it is the economic base. A BRAC (Base Realignment and Closure) round or a major contract cancellation can devastate a community.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's $70 billion defense dependency is a structural vulnerability that the state acknowledges implicitly (by lobbying aggressively against base closures and for contract awards) but never addresses explicitly (by diversifying regional economies that depend on defense). The defense industry employs engineers, technicians, and skilled workers at high wages ($80,000 to $200,000) while producing weapons systems, surveillance technology, and military infrastructure that many Californians, in a state that consistently votes for progressive candidates, would find politically uncomfortable to celebrate. The defense economy operates in a peculiar political silence: it is the largest source of federal funds flowing to California, the employer of hundreds of thousands, and the economic foundation of entire regions, yet it is almost never discussed in state policy debates about California's economic future.

The generous read is that California's defense industry is a national asset that reflects the state's unique capabilities: the engineering talent, the research universities (Caltech, Stanford, UC San Diego's defense research), the advanced manufacturing base, and the innovation culture that produce the technologies the military depends on. The defense sector pays well, employs diversely (including significant veteran employment), and produces technological spillovers (the internet, GPS, jet engines, satellite communications) that benefit the civilian economy. California's defense spending is not a subsidy; it is a payment for products and services that the state's workforce and industrial base uniquely provide.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The DOD publishes contract awards by state and congressional district, and the Office of Economic Adjustment tracks base economic impact. California does not publish a state-level defense economic impact analysis that aggregates contracts, personnel spending, base operations, and secondary economic effects (multiplier impacts from defense-worker spending) into a total defense economic footprint. The state's actual economic dependence on defense spending is larger than the $70 billion in direct DOD outlays, but the total is not officially estimated.
