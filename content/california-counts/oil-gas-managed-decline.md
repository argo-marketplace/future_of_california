# California Counts: The Managed Decline
## What 370 million barrels of annual oil production, 35,000 idle wells, a 3,200-foot drilling setback, and $2 billion in estimated orphan well cleanup costs reveal about the petroleum industry California is phasing out while still consuming

California produces approximately 370 million barrels of crude oil per year (approximately 400,000 barrels per day), making it the seventh-largest oil-producing state. Production has declined approximately 50 percent from its peak of 395 million barrels in 1985. The state simultaneously consumes approximately 600 million barrels per year, importing the difference from foreign sources (primarily Saudi Arabia, Ecuador, and Iraq) and other states (primarily Alaska and New Mexico via pipeline). California refines approximately 1.8 million barrels per day across 14 refineries, producing gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel for the state's 30 million registered vehicles and extensive freight and aviation sectors.

SB 1137 (2022) established a 3,200-foot health and safety setback zone around oil and gas wells, prohibiting new drilling within that distance of homes, schools, healthcare facilities, and other sensitive receptors. The law affects approximately 2.7 million Californians who live within 3,200 feet of an active oil or gas well, disproportionately in low-income communities and communities of color in the San Joaquin Valley, Los Angeles Basin, and Kern County. The oil industry challenged SB 1137 via a referendum (Proposition 6 on the 2024 ballot), which failed, and the law took effect.

## The Orphan Well Problem

California has approximately 35,000 idle oil and gas wells (not currently producing but not yet properly plugged and abandoned) and an estimated 5,000 to 10,000 orphan wells (wells whose operators have gone bankrupt or disappeared, leaving no responsible party for cleanup). Properly plugging and abandoning a well costs approximately $50,000 to $500,000, depending on depth, location, and condition. The total estimated cost of plugging California's idle and orphan wells is approximately $2 billion to $6 billion.

The California Geologic Energy Management Division (CalGEM) oversees well plugging and has received approximately $200 million in federal Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act funding for orphan well cleanup. At current plugging rates (approximately 500 to 1,000 wells per year), clearing the backlog will take decades. Idle wells can leak methane (a potent greenhouse gas), contaminate groundwater with produced water and hydrocarbons, and create explosion hazards. The wells are concentrated in Kern County, Los Angeles County, and Ventura County, often in residential neighborhoods developed around or after the oilfields.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California maintains a contradictory posture toward petroleum: regulating in-state production into decline (through setbacks, permitting restrictions, and litigation) while continuing to import and consume roughly the same volume of oil. The decline in California production does not reduce California's oil consumption; it shifts production to jurisdictions with less environmental regulation (Saudi Arabia, Ecuador, the Permian Basin) and increases transportation emissions from importing. Meanwhile, the state has created an unfunded environmental liability of $2 billion to $6 billion in orphan wells that will be cleaned up with public money because the operators who profited from extraction are bankrupt or dissolved. The managed decline is managed only on the production side; the consumption side, the demand that drives global oil production, is addressed through vehicle electrification on a timeline of decades.

The generous read is that California is executing a deliberate phase-out of fossil fuel extraction that no other major oil-producing jurisdiction has attempted. The 3,200-foot setback protects 2.7 million residents from the documented health effects of proximity to oil and gas production (respiratory disease, cancer, adverse birth outcomes). CalGEM's permitting restrictions have reduced new well approvals by approximately 75 percent since 2019. Governor Newsom's 2045 carbon neutrality target provides the long-term framework for eliminating both production and consumption. The orphan well problem is real but solvable: federal funding, operator bonds (which CalGEM has increased), and the industry's remaining profits can cover cleanup if the regulatory timeline is maintained. The transition will take 20 years. That timeline is shorter than any comparable petro-state's plan.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CalGEM publishes well data (location, production, status, operator) and permitting data. It does not publish a comprehensive financial analysis of the state's total future cleanup liability (all idle, orphan, and eventually abandoned wells), the sufficiency of operator bonds to cover that liability, or the health impacts on communities adjacent to idle and orphan wells. The question of whether California's bonding requirements will produce industry-funded cleanup or taxpayer-funded cleanup is an actuarial question that CalGEM does not publicly model.
