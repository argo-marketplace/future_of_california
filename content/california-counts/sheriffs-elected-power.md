# California Counts: The Elected Power
## What 58 elected sheriffs, $14 billion in combined budgets, 40 percent of county general fund spending, and the least accountable law enforcement structure in the state reveal about the office California cannot reform

California's 58 county sheriffs are, by some measures, the most powerful elected officials in the state that most residents cannot name. They control county jail systems that house approximately 65,000 inmates. They provide primary law enforcement to approximately 7 million Californians living in unincorporated areas. They operate on combined annual budgets totaling approximately $14 billion, making sheriff's departments the single largest expenditure category in most county budgets, consuming 30 to 40 percent of county general fund revenue. They are elected independently of county supervisors, not subject to county executive authority, and removable only through recall election or criminal indictment. A sheriff is accountable to voters and, in extremis, to a grand jury. In practice, most sheriffs run unopposed for reelection and hold office for decades.

The Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department, the largest in the nation, operates on an annual budget of approximately $3.8 billion and employs approximately 18,000 personnel (10,000 sworn deputies and 8,000 civilian staff). It patrols unincorporated LA County and contracts to provide policing for 42 cities that have chosen to contract with the sheriff rather than operate their own police departments. The contract model makes the LASD the de facto police department for a combined population of approximately 4 million people, larger than the population of 24 individual states. The department's budget exceeds the entire general fund of the city of San Francisco.

## The Jail Budget

County jails consume a disproportionate share of sheriff's department budgets and, by extension, county budgets. The cost of housing a single inmate in a California county jail averages approximately $200 to $350 per day, depending on the county, with the highest costs in the Bay Area and Southern California coastal counties. Los Angeles County's jail system costs approximately $1.2 billion per year to operate, or roughly $340 per inmate per day. San Francisco's jail system costs approximately $420 per inmate per day, one of the highest per-inmate costs in the nation.

The jail population has declined significantly from its peak. California's county jail population was approximately 83,000 in 2013, before the implementation of Proposition 47 (2014), which reclassified certain nonviolent felonies to misdemeanors, and Proposition 57 (2016), which expanded parole eligibility. By 2023, the county jail population had declined to approximately 65,000. The decline has not produced proportional budget savings because jail operating costs are heavily fixed: the facilities, staffing, healthcare contracts, and food service contracts do not scale down linearly with population. A jail that reduces its population by 25 percent may reduce its variable costs by 10 to 15 percent while its fixed costs remain unchanged.

The healthcare costs within jails are particularly acute. County jails are constitutionally required to provide healthcare, including mental health and substance use treatment, to inmates. Mental illness prevalence among jail inmates is estimated at 30 to 40 percent, and substance use disorder prevalence exceeds 50 percent. Several county jail systems, including Los Angeles, Santa Clara, and Alameda, are under federal consent decrees or court supervision related to inadequate healthcare, mental health treatment, or conditions of confinement. The consent decrees require specific staffing ratios, treatment protocols, and facility improvements that increase costs regardless of population trends.

## The Accountability Gap

Sheriffs occupy a unique position in California's governance structure. They are constitutional officers, established by the state constitution rather than by statute, which gives them a legal independence that police chiefs (who serve at the pleasure of city managers or mayors) do not possess. A county board of supervisors can reduce a sheriff's budget but cannot fire the sheriff, direct specific operational decisions, or override department policies. This independence was designed as a check on political interference in law enforcement. It also functions as a shield against oversight.

The Civilian Oversight Commission model, adopted in Los Angeles County and several other jurisdictions, provides a forum for public accountability but limited enforcement power. The LA County Civilian Oversight Commission can hold hearings, subpoena records, and issue reports. It cannot discipline deputies, terminate employees, or override operational decisions. The Inspector General model, also used in LA County and elsewhere, provides independent investigation of use-of-force incidents and misconduct complaints. The IG can investigate and report. The IG cannot compel discipline.

SB 2 (2021), the "Kenneth Ross Jr. Police Decertification Act," created the first statewide system for decertifying law enforcement officers for serious misconduct. The law gives the Commission on Peace Officer Standards and Training (POST) the authority to revoke the certification of officers found to have committed specified acts (dishonesty, abuse of force, sexual assault, racial bias). The law applies to all peace officers, including sheriff's deputies, but the decertification process is the only statewide accountability mechanism that reaches inside sheriff's departments. Within a department, the sheriff controls discipline, and the elected sheriff's decisions are final absent a successful labor arbitration challenge.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that the sheriff's office is an anachronism: a 19th-century elected law enforcement model operating 21st-century jail systems, contract policing operations, and multibillion-dollar budgets with less external oversight than any city police department. The independence that the constitution provides to sheriffs was designed for an era when county law enforcement meant a sheriff and a few deputies patrolling a rural county. It is not designed for the governance of a $3.8 billion department with 18,000 employees, a jail system housing 14,000 inmates, and contract policing for 42 cities. The accountability mechanisms, elections, oversight commissions, inspectors general, are weak substitutes for the executive authority that cities exercise over their police departments. The result is that the most powerful law enforcement entity in most California counties operates with the least structural accountability.

The generous read is that the elected sheriff model provides direct democratic accountability that appointed police chiefs lack. A sheriff who fails the community faces the voters. The 2022 Los Angeles County sheriff's race, in which incumbent Alex Villanueva was defeated by Robert Luna after a term marked by controversy over department culture, deputy gangs, and use of force, demonstrated that electoral accountability is not theoretical. Voters removed a sheriff they found unacceptable, something that requires a mayor's decision for an appointed police chief. The constitutional independence of the sheriff protects law enforcement from political manipulation by county supervisors, a genuine concern in counties where the board of supervisors may have political interests that conflict with impartial policing. The office is imperfect. The alternative, making the sheriff an appointed position, would require a constitutional amendment and would replace democratic accountability with bureaucratic accountability, a trade that is not obviously superior.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

County budget documents report sheriff's department expenditures at the department level. They typically do not break down spending into the granularity needed to assess cost-effectiveness: the cost per arrest, the cost per call for service, the cost per contract city, or the ratio of patrol spending to administrative spending. The contract policing model is particularly opaque: the rates charged to contract cities are set by the sheriff's department and may or may not reflect the actual cost of providing service to that city. Contract cities that believe they are overpaying have limited ability to verify the cost basis.

Use-of-force data reporting has improved under AB 71 (2015) and subsequent legislation, but the quality and completeness of reporting varies by department. The California Department of Justice publishes the URSUS (Use of Force Incident Reporting) database, which includes serious use-of-force incidents. Routine uses of force (takedowns, control holds, deployment of tasers at less-than-lethal settings) are not uniformly reported.
