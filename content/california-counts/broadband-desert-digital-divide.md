# California Counts: The Broadband Desert
## What 1.3 million unserved households, a $6 billion investment, 55,000 miles of planned fiber, and a 37 percent adoption gap reveal about the state that built the internet but can't connect its own residents

California is the birthplace of the commercial internet. Cisco, Google, Meta, Netflix, and hundreds of the companies that built the digital economy are headquartered in the state. The San Francisco Bay Area generates more internet traffic than most countries. And approximately 1.3 million California households, roughly 9 percent of the total, lack access to broadband internet at speeds the Federal Communications Commission defines as minimally adequate (currently 100 Mbps download, 20 Mbps upload). An additional 2 million households have access but do not subscribe, primarily because they cannot afford it. The total number of Californians on the wrong side of the digital divide, lacking either access or adoption, is approximately 8 to 9 million people, roughly one in five residents of the state that invented the technology they cannot use. The broadband desert is not in the places you would guess first. It is in the Central Valley. It is in the tribal lands of Northern California and the southern deserts. It is in South Los Angeles. It is in the rural Sierra foothills. And it is, overwhelmingly, in communities that are low-income, Latino, Black, Indigenous, elderly, or some combination of all five.

---

## The Map: Where the Gaps Are

The California Public Utilities Commission's broadband map, updated through California Interactive Broadband Map data and FCC Broadband Data Collection filings, reveals the geography of disconnection. The areas without adequate broadband fall into three categories.

Rural and remote areas: approximately 700,000 households in the Sierra foothills, Northern California mountains, Central Coast rangelands, and desert communities lack wireline broadband infrastructure entirely. The economics are straightforward: laying fiber optic cable costs approximately $25,000 to $40,000 per mile in rural terrain and can exceed $100,000 per mile in mountainous or rocky conditions. In areas with one to five households per mile, the cost per connection is $5,000 to $20,000, a figure that no private internet service provider will invest without public subsidy because the monthly revenue from a $60 broadband subscription will not recover the capital cost within any reasonable payback period. These communities rely on DSL (which delivers speeds of 5 to 25 Mbps, below the current broadband definition), satellite (high latency, weather-dependent, data-capped until Starlink's recent expansion), fixed wireless (limited range and capacity), or nothing.

Tribal lands: California's 109 federally recognized tribes and numerous unrecognized tribal communities include some of the most digitally isolated populations in the state. The Yurok Reservation in Humboldt and Del Norte counties, the Round Valley Indian Reservation in Mendocino County, and the Torres Martinez Desert Cahuilla Indians Reservation near the Salton Sea all have broadband access rates below 50 percent. The CPUC's tribal consultation process has identified approximately 50,000 to 75,000 tribal households without adequate broadband. Tribal sovereignty, federal trust land status, and the geographic remoteness of many reservations create jurisdictional and logistical barriers that standard broadband deployment programs do not address.

Urban underserved areas: approximately 600,000 households in urban California have broadband infrastructure available but do not subscribe, primarily because of cost. The adoption gap is concentrated in low-income neighborhoods: South Los Angeles, East Oakland, the Inland Empire, and the agricultural cities of the Central Valley. In these areas, broadband infrastructure exists (cable or fiber passes the home), but the monthly cost of $50 to $80 per month, plus equipment fees and installation, exceeds what households earning $25,000 to $40,000 per year can prioritize over rent, food, and transportation. The FCC's Affordable Connectivity Program, which provided $30 per month broadband subsidies to low-income households, enrolled approximately 2.3 million California households before its federal funding expired in June 2024. The program's lapse left those households facing the full retail cost of broadband, and early data suggests approximately 30 to 40 percent have reduced or cancelled service.

## The Investment: $6 Billion and Counting

California has committed approximately $6 billion to broadband deployment since 2020, the largest state broadband investment in the nation. The funding comes from three primary sources.

The federal Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (2021) allocated approximately $1.86 billion to California through the Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment (BEAD) program, administered by the CPUC. The funds are designated for connecting unserved and underserved locations, with a priority on fiber deployment. California's BEAD initial proposal was approved by NTIA in 2024, and grant awards to internet service providers are projected to begin in 2025-26.

State bond funds: Proposition 1D (2006) and subsequent appropriations provided early broadband funding. The 2021-22 state budget included $3.25 billion in broadband funding, split between a $2 billion middle-mile open-access network (building the backbone fiber that connects local networks to the internet backbone) and $1.25 billion in last-mile grants (connecting individual homes and businesses). The middle-mile network, managed by the California Department of Technology, involves approximately 10,000 miles of new fiber along state highway rights-of-way, connecting rural communities, tribal lands, and agricultural areas to the broader internet.

Federal programs: In addition to BEAD, California receives broadband funding through the USDA ReConnect Program (approximately $200 million awarded to California projects), the FCC's E-Rate program for schools and libraries, Rural Health Care Program, and various tribal broadband programs. The Emergency Connectivity Fund, created during the pandemic, provided approximately $500 million to California schools and libraries for devices and connectivity.

The total planned fiber deployment across all programs is approximately 55,000 miles, enough to connect the vast majority of currently unserved locations. The challenge is execution: securing rights-of-way across private land, permitting construction in environmentally sensitive areas (CEQA review applies to broadband infrastructure), recruiting a workforce skilled in fiber splicing and installation, and managing a procurement process that must evaluate proposals from dozens of ISPs of varying capacity and reliability.

## The Adoption Gap: Access Is Not Enough

The distinction between access (infrastructure passes your home) and adoption (you subscribe and use the service) is the broadband divide's less visible dimension. The CPUC estimates that approximately 87 percent of California households have access to broadband at 100/20 Mbps speeds. But only approximately 78 percent subscribe to any broadband service, and only approximately 63 percent subscribe at speeds the FCC considers broadband. The 37 percent who do not have broadband-speed service include the 9 percent without access and the 28 percent who have access but do not subscribe at adequate speeds.

The reasons for non-adoption are consistently reported in surveys: cost (cited by approximately 55 percent of non-subscribers), lack of perceived need (25 percent, concentrated among elderly residents), digital literacy barriers (15 percent), and language barriers (10 percent, concentrated among non-English-speaking immigrant households). Cost is the dominant barrier, and it is not a perception problem: broadband service in California averages $60 to $80 per month for speeds meeting the FCC definition, plus $10 to $15 per month for equipment rental, plus one-time installation fees of $50 to $100. For a household earning $30,000 per year, broadband at $75 per month represents 3 percent of gross income, a meaningful expenditure competing against necessities.

The Affordable Connectivity Program's expiration in June 2024 eliminated the primary federal tool for closing the adoption gap. California has allocated approximately $50 million in state funds for broadband affordability programs, a fraction of the $690 million per year the ACP was providing to California households. The California Teleconnect Fund, a surcharge-funded program that provides discounts to schools, libraries, hospitals, and community organizations, does not provide direct household subsidies. No existing state program replaces the ACP at comparable scale.

The digital divide has become a social services divide. During the pandemic, education, healthcare (telehealth), government benefits (unemployment insurance, CalFresh, Medi-Cal renewal), court proceedings, and job applications moved online. Households without broadband were functionally excluded from each of these systems. EDD's unemployment insurance system, which shifted to online applications during the pandemic, was inaccessible to an estimated 500,000 to 800,000 Californians who lacked adequate internet access. Telehealth appointments, which expanded from approximately 4 percent of Medi-Cal visits pre-pandemic to 40 percent during the pandemic, require stable broadband connections that many rural and low-income patients do not have.

## The Speed Race: 100/20 Is Already Obsolete

The FCC raised the broadband speed benchmark from 25/3 Mbps (set in 2015) to 100/20 Mbps in 2024, a change that immediately reclassified millions of Americans as underserved. The new benchmark reflects the reality that modern internet usage, including video conferencing, streaming, cloud computing, and multi-device households, requires speeds far above the 2015 standard. But 100/20 Mbps is itself a floor, not a ceiling. Fiber-to-the-home connections typically deliver 1 Gbps (1,000 Mbps) symmetrical, ten times the current benchmark. Cable broadband commonly delivers 300 to 500 Mbps download but only 10 to 35 Mbps upload, an asymmetry that is inadequate for video conferencing, remote work, and cloud-based applications that require robust upload capacity.

California's middle-mile investment is future-proofed for fiber, which has essentially unlimited capacity upgradable through electronics rather than physical cable replacement. The last-mile connections, however, will vary: fiber-to-the-home in areas where density justifies the cost, fixed wireless in areas where terrain and low density make fiber uneconomical, and satellite (primarily Starlink) in the most remote locations. Fixed wireless and satellite connections typically deliver 50 to 200 Mbps with higher latency and lower reliability than fiber, creating a tier of connectivity that meets the current benchmark but may not meet tomorrow's.

The risk is building a broadband network to 2024 standards that is obsolete by 2034. The analogy to highway building is direct: California built freeways to 1960s traffic projections and has been widening them ever since. If the $6 billion broadband investment connects unserved communities at 100 Mbps while served communities access 1 Gbps or faster, the digital divide will not close: it will shift from a gap between connected and unconnected to a gap between adequately connected and inadequately connected, replicating the two-tier service pattern that characterizes every other California infrastructure system.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's $6 billion broadband investment is a remediation project, not an innovation project. The state is spending billions to build infrastructure that should have been built 15 years ago, when the stimulus-funded broadband programs of 2009 failed to reach California's underserved communities at scale. The ISPs that received billions in federal subsidies through the Connect America Fund, the Universal Service Fund, and various grant programs did not deploy broadband to the communities that needed it most, because those communities were not profitable. The market failed. The state did not intervene. And 1.3 million households in the technology capital of the world ended up without internet access in 2026. The $6 billion is welcome. It is also 15 years late, targeted at a speed standard that is already being surpassed, and dependent on an ISP industry that has repeatedly taken public subsidies and failed to deliver on deployment commitments. The broadband desert exists because the state trusted the market to connect unprofitable communities, and the market did exactly what markets do: it served the profitable ones.

The generous read is that California is mounting the largest and most comprehensive state broadband investment in American history, with a design that addresses the mistakes of previous programs. The middle-mile open-access network, by building publicly owned backbone fiber, ensures that last-mile ISPs can reach underserved communities without bearing the full cost of backbone connectivity. The BEAD program's preference for fiber ensures that connections will be future-proof for decades. The $6 billion investment, combined with federal BEAD funds, will connect the vast majority of currently unserved locations within five years. The adoption gap is harder, but the state's broadband equity plan includes digital literacy programs, multilingual outreach, and affordability initiatives that address the non-infrastructure barriers. And the CPUC's broadband mapping, informed by the FCC's Broadband Data Collection, is more accurate than any previous effort, reducing the risk that investment is directed to areas that do not need it while bypassing areas that do. The digital divide is real. The investment to close it is funded, planned, and underway. Whether it succeeds depends on execution, not commitment.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The FCC's Broadband Data Collection and the CPUC's broadband maps report coverage at the address level, a significant improvement over the previous census-block methodology that overstated coverage. But address-level data still does not capture actual service quality: an address shown as "served" at 100 Mbps may experience real-world speeds of 30 to 50 Mbps during peak hours due to network congestion, equipment limitations, or ISP throttling. The gap between advertised speed and experienced speed is consistently 20 to 40 percent for cable broadband and essentially zero for fiber, a distinction that coverage maps do not reflect.

The data does not capture the economic cost of the digital divide. Households without broadband access earn less (broadband access is correlated with approximately $2,000 to $4,000 per year in additional household income, controlling for other factors), have less access to employment (approximately 80 percent of job applications are submitted online), have lower educational attainment (students without home broadband score lower on standardized tests and complete assignments at lower rates), and have worse health outcomes (telehealth access reduces preventable hospitalizations by approximately 15 to 20 percent for chronic disease management). The aggregate economic cost of California's digital divide, the sum of lost income, reduced educational outcomes, and excess healthcare costs across 8 to 9 million people, has not been comprehensively estimated but likely exceeds $5 to $10 billion per year, a figure that would dwarf the one-time infrastructure investment needed to close the gap.

The data also does not capture the competitive landscape that shapes broadband access. Approximately 40 percent of California households have only one ISP offering broadband-speed service, making broadband a de facto monopoly for those consumers. In areas with only one provider, prices average 20 to 30 percent higher than in areas with two or more competitors. The middle-mile open-access network, by reducing backbone costs for last-mile ISPs, may enable new competitors to enter underserved markets. Whether it does depends on whether the CPUC's grant conditions and open-access requirements are enforced, or whether incumbent ISPs use their existing market position to exclude new entrants, a pattern that has characterized telecommunications markets since the breakup of AT&T.

---

The state that built the internet cannot connect 1.3 million of its own households to it. The state where Google, Netflix, and Meta generate hundreds of billions in revenue from internet users has residents in Tulare County, Humboldt County, and South Los Angeles who cannot load a web page. The $6 billion investment will build the pipes. Whether it closes the divide depends on whether pipes are enough, or whether the affordability, literacy, and monopoly barriers that keep 8 to 9 million Californians offline require something the state has not yet built: a broadband system designed for the public interest rather than the ISP's balance sheet. California connected the world. It has not yet connected itself.
