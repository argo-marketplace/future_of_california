# California Counts: The Pollution Map
## What CalEnviroScreen, 2 million people in the most burdened census tracts, 72 percent overlap with communities of color, and $1.6 billion in cap-and-trade investment reveal about the geography of environmental harm in California

In 2013, the California Environmental Protection Agency released CalEnviroScreen, a tool that maps every census tract in California by its cumulative burden of pollution exposure and population vulnerability. The tool combines 21 indicators: pollution burden indicators (ozone, PM2.5, diesel particulate matter, drinking water contaminants, pesticide use, toxic releases, traffic density, cleanup sites, groundwater threats, hazardous waste facilities, impaired water bodies, and solid waste sites) and population characteristic indicators (asthma rates, cardiovascular disease, low birth weight, educational attainment, housing burden, linguistic isolation, poverty, and unemployment). Each census tract receives a composite score from 1 to 100. The top 25 percent, designated "disadvantaged communities" under SB 535 (2012), are the tracts where the highest pollution concentrations overlap with the highest social vulnerabilities.

The map is a portrait of environmental inequality. The most burdened census tracts, those scoring in the top 10 percent, are concentrated in the San Joaquin Valley (Fresno, Bakersfield, Stockton, Modesto), the Inland Empire (San Bernardino, Riverside), southeastern Los Angeles County (Commerce, Vernon, Huntington Park, Paramount), and the port-adjacent communities of Long Beach, Wilmington, and West Oakland. Approximately 2 million Californians live in the top-10-percent tracts. They breathe the worst air, drink the most contaminated water, live closest to the most toxic facilities, and have the fewest resources, income, education, health insurance, to mitigate the harm.

## The Correlation That Is Not a Coincidence

The overlap between environmental burden and race is stark. Approximately 72 percent of residents in CalEnviroScreen's top-quartile (most burdened) census tracts are people of color, predominantly Latino and Black. In the San Joaquin Valley, the most burdened tracts are 80 to 90 percent Latino. In South Los Angeles, they are 70 to 85 percent Black and Latino. In West Oakland, 75 percent. The correlation is not a coincidence. It is the residue of a century of land use decisions: the placement of freeways through Black neighborhoods during the 1950s and 1960s, the zoning of industrial facilities in low-income communities that lacked political power to oppose them, the exclusion of people of color from the suburban neighborhoods that were built upwind and upstream from the pollution sources, and the continuing pattern of locating warehouses, distribution centers, and truck corridors in the Inland Empire communities that house the workforce that staffs them.

AB 617 (2017) required the California Air Resources Board to develop community-level air monitoring and emissions reduction plans for the most pollution-burdened communities. CARB has designated approximately 20 communities for enhanced monitoring and the development of Community Emissions Reduction Programs (CERPs). The CERPs identify local pollution sources (a chrome plating facility, a railyard, a freeway corridor) and require specific emissions reductions on defined timelines. The program has produced real improvements in some communities: the closure or retrofit of specific polluting facilities, the installation of air filtration in schools near freeways, and the imposition of anti-idling requirements on trucks at warehouses.

The scale of the program, however, is small relative to the problem. Twenty designated communities out of approximately 2,000 census tracts in the top quartile means that roughly 1 percent of the most burdened tracts have community-specific emissions reduction plans. The remaining 99 percent are addressed through statewide regulation, which improves air quality broadly but does not target the localized hotspots (a single refinery, a particular freeway interchange, a cluster of warehouses) that produce the most concentrated exposures.

## The Cap-and-Trade Investment

SB 535 (2012) and AB 1550 (2016) require that at least 25 percent of cap-and-trade auction revenue be invested in projects that benefit disadvantaged communities as identified by CalEnviroScreen, with at least 10 percent funding projects located within those communities. The cap-and-trade program has generated approximately $26 billion in cumulative auction revenue since its inception. Investments in disadvantaged communities have exceeded $1.6 billion through programs including affordable housing near transit, clean vehicle incentives, urban greening, and the Transformative Climate Communities program.

The Transformative Climate Communities (TCC) program is the most ambitious. It provides large grants ($35 million to $70 million) to coalitions of community organizations, local governments, and tribes to implement integrated climate and equity projects in the most burdened neighborhoods. TCC grants have funded projects in Fresno, Ontario, Stockton, Watts, East Oakland, and other communities, including affordable housing, urban tree canopy, community solar, active transportation infrastructure, and workforce training. The grants are transformative in scale for the neighborhoods they serve, but the program funds approximately three to five communities per funding cycle, a pace that would require decades to reach all qualifying neighborhoods.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that CalEnviroScreen documented a moral catastrophe and the state responded with a mapping tool and a modest investment program. The map shows that 2 million Californians live in census tracts where pollution burden and social vulnerability are concentrated to a degree that produces measurable health harm: higher asthma hospitalization rates, higher cardiovascular mortality, higher rates of low-birth-weight babies. The people living in these tracts are disproportionately people of color. The pollution sources, freeways, refineries, warehouses, railyards, chemical facilities, were placed in their neighborhoods by decisions made over decades, and removing them requires decades more. The $1.6 billion in cap-and-trade investment is approximately $800 per person living in the top-quartile tracts, spread over more than a decade. The scale of investment does not match the scale of harm.

The generous read is that California is the only state in the nation that has built a comprehensive, publicly available, census-tract-level cumulative impact assessment tool and used it to direct regulatory and fiscal resources toward the most burdened communities. CalEnviroScreen is a model that other states and countries have studied and replicated. AB 617's community-level monitoring and emissions reduction programs are producing measurable improvements in the designated communities. The cap-and-trade investment requirements ensure that climate policy dollars flow to the communities most affected by both pollution and climate change. And the long-term trend in California's air quality is positive: statewide PM2.5 concentrations have declined approximately 40 percent since 2000, and ozone concentrations in the San Joaquin Valley, while still the worst in the nation, have improved significantly. The gap between the most and least burdened communities is narrowing, slowly. The tools for accelerating that narrowing, CalEnviroScreen, AB 617, SB 535, now exist. The question is whether the state will fund them at the scale the data demands.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CalEnviroScreen uses modeled estimates for several pollution indicators (ozone, PM2.5) rather than direct measurements at the census tract level. The actual exposure experienced by individuals depends on factors the model does not capture: time spent indoors, proximity to specific sources within the tract, occupational exposure, and the quality of building envelopes (which determines how much outdoor air pollution penetrates indoor environments). A census tract with a high CalEnviroScreen score contains residents with varying actual exposures, and the tract-level average may not represent any individual's actual experience.

The health outcome data in CalEnviroScreen (asthma, cardiovascular disease, low birth weight) is correlational. The tool does not establish causal relationships between specific pollution sources and specific health outcomes in specific tracts. The contribution of poverty, stress, diet, healthcare access, and other non-environmental factors to the health disparities observed in high-scoring tracts is not separated from the contribution of pollution exposure. CalEnviroScreen shows where harm concentrates. It does not isolate why.
