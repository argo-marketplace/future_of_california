# California Counts: The Empty Classroom
## What 24,000 unfilled positions, a 50 percent five-year attrition rate, $104,000 median salary, and a $135 billion K-12 budget reveal about the state that cannot staff its schools

California employs approximately 320,000 teachers to serve 5.9 million K-12 students. It needs more. The Learning Policy Institute estimated approximately 24,000 teacher vacancies and positions filled by underqualified or substitute teachers statewide in 2024-25. The shortage is concentrated in the subjects and regions where the need is greatest: mathematics, science, special education, and bilingual education, disproportionately in high-poverty schools in the Central Valley, Inland Empire, and rural Northern California. A student attending a low-income school in Kern County is roughly three times more likely to be taught by a teacher without a full credential than a student in Palo Alto. The teacher shortage is not distributed randomly. It maps the same geography of disadvantage that appears in every California Counts analysis: the communities with the greatest need have the fewest qualified teachers.

The median teacher salary in California is approximately $104,000, the third-highest in the nation behind New York and Massachusetts and roughly 30 percent above the national median of $80,000. The salary sounds generous. It is not, when measured against the cost of living where most California teachers work. A teacher earning $104,000 in the Bay Area, where median rent is $3,200 per month and the median home price exceeds $1.2 million, takes home approximately $6,500 per month after taxes and cannot qualify for a mortgage on a median home without a second income. The salary is competitive nationally and insufficient locally. The housing crisis that drives out nurses, firefighters, and middle-class families drives out teachers through the same mechanism: wages that are high by national standards but low relative to the local cost of living.

## The Pipeline Problem

California's teacher preparation pipeline has been contracting for two decades. The number of new teaching credentials issued annually peaked at approximately 44,000 in 2003-04 and has declined to approximately 23,000 in recent years, a drop of nearly 50 percent. Enrollment in teacher preparation programs declined from approximately 77,000 to 37,000 over the same period. The CSU system, which produces roughly 50 percent of the state's new teachers, has seen education program enrollment decline by 40 percent.

The decline reflects economic calculation. A student who completes a bachelor's degree and a post-baccalaureate credential program has invested five to six years and $50,000 to $100,000 in education costs. Their starting salary as a California teacher is approximately $55,000 to $65,000 depending on the district. Their classmate who majored in computer science starts at $120,000 to $180,000 in total compensation. The classmate who became a nurse starts at $90,000 to $110,000. The opportunity cost of choosing teaching over comparable professions requiring similar education levels is $30,000 to $100,000 per year, compounded over a 30-year career. The pipeline is emptying because talented college graduates are doing arithmetic.

The attrition compounds the pipeline problem. Approximately 50 percent of new California teachers leave the profession within their first five years. The reasons are consistent across surveys: workload (administrative burden, testing requirements, IEP documentation), student behavior challenges, inadequate support and mentoring, and compensation that does not keep pace with cost of living. Experienced teachers also leave: the pandemic accelerated retirements and mid-career exits, with approximately 15,000 teachers leaving the profession during the 2020-21 and 2021-22 school years above normal attrition rates.

## LCFF and the Funding Formula

California's K-12 education budget of approximately $135 billion (fiscal year 2025-26, combining state, federal, and local sources) is distributed primarily through the Local Control Funding Formula, enacted in 2013 to replace a labyrinthine system of categorical programs with a streamlined, equity-weighted formula. LCFF provides a base grant per student (approximately $12,000 to $13,500 depending on grade level) plus supplemental grants (20 percent above base) and concentration grants (an additional 65 percent of the supplemental grant for districts where more than 55 percent of students are low-income, English learners, or foster youth).

LCFF increased total K-12 spending significantly: per-pupil expenditure rose from approximately $9,200 in 2012-13 to approximately $23,000 in 2024-25 (including all sources). The equity weighting directs more funding to high-need districts, partially addressing decades of funding disparities. The formula gives districts significant spending discretion through Local Control and Accountability Plans, which require community input but do not prescribe how supplemental and concentration funds are spent.

The LCFF's limitation is that it funds districts, not outcomes. A district that receives $5 million in concentration grant funding can spend it on teacher recruitment, counselors, technology, facilities, or any other purpose described in its LCAP. Whether the spending improves outcomes for the target population is measured by California School Dashboard indicators (graduation rates, test scores, suspension rates, English learner reclassification), but the accountability mechanism is advisory rather than enforceable. Districts in the lowest performance tiers receive technical assistance. They do not lose funding. The formula ensures equity of inputs. It does not ensure equity of results.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's teacher shortage is the predictable result of paying teachers $104,000 in a state where $104,000 is not enough to buy a house, asking them to manage classes of 30 to 35 students with increasing behavioral complexity, burdening them with administrative requirements that consume planning and instruction time, and then expressing surprise when they leave or never enter the profession. The shortage falls hardest on the students who need the most: low-income, English learner, special education, and students of color in the schools that cannot attract and retain qualified teachers. The $135 billion K-12 budget is the largest in the nation. It has not produced teacher staffing levels adequate to serve 5.9 million students. The pipeline is contracting. The attrition is accelerating. And the gap between California's per-pupil spending ($23,000, above the national average) and its educational outcomes (30th in the nation in NAEP reading, 35th in math) suggests that the spending is not reaching the classroom at the level the numbers imply.

The generous read is that California has dramatically increased K-12 funding through LCFF, created the most equity-weighted funding formula in the nation, expanded the teacher residency programs that provide paid clinical training, and invested in recruitment incentives including the Golden State Teacher Grant ($20,000 for credential candidates who commit to teaching in high-need subjects and schools). Per-pupil spending has more than doubled in a decade. The universal Transitional Kindergarten expansion is adding an entire year of public education. Community schools, which integrate health, social, and family services into the school site, are expanding with $4.1 billion in state investment. The teacher shortage is real and serious. It is also a national problem, not a California problem: 44 states report teacher shortages. California's response, combining higher pay, expanded residency programs, and student loan forgiveness for teachers in high-need areas, is more aggressive than most states'. The challenge is the housing market, which no education policy can fix.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CDE publishes teacher workforce data including credential status, demographics, and assignment. It does not publish a real-time vacancy count. The 24,000-position estimate is derived from credential data, substitute teacher utilization, and survey research, not from a unified reporting system. The true shortage may be larger because districts sometimes consolidate classes rather than report vacancies, masking the gap.

The data does not capture teacher quality, only teacher credentials. A fully credentialed teacher in their 20th year is counted the same as a first-year teacher with an emergency permit. The distribution of experienced, effective teachers (a quality that research consistently links to student outcomes) is not tracked in a way that allows statewide analysis of whether high-need students are systematically taught by less experienced or less effective teachers, though smaller studies consistently find that they are.
