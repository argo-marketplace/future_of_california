# California Counts: The Insurer of Last Resort
## What 400,000 FAIR Plan policies, a 600 percent increase since 2019, a $6 billion exposure in high-fire-risk zones, and an insurance market that private companies are abandoning reveal about the property insurance crisis California acknowledged too late and now cannot stop

The California FAIR Plan (Fair Access to Insurance Requirements) is the state's insurer of last resort, a shared risk pool created in 1968 after the Watts riots to provide basic property insurance to homeowners who cannot obtain coverage in the private market. The FAIR Plan is not a government agency; it is an association of all private insurers doing business in California, each of which shares in the plan's losses in proportion to its market share. As of 2025, the FAIR Plan has approximately 400,000 active policies, up from approximately 200,000 in 2021 and approximately 60,000 in 2019. The 600 percent increase reflects the rapid withdrawal of private insurers from wildfire-prone areas.

The FAIR Plan provides a "bare walls" policy: dwelling coverage only, with no personal property, liability, or additional living expense coverage (though a supplemental "Difference in Conditions" policy is available from some admitted carriers at additional cost). FAIR Plan premiums are significantly higher than private market premiums: homeowners in high-fire-risk areas pay $3,000 to $15,000 per year for FAIR Plan coverage, compared to $1,500 to $4,000 for equivalent private coverage (when available). The plan's total exposure (maximum potential payout from a catastrophic event) exceeds $6 billion, concentrated in the wildland-urban interface of Los Angeles, San Diego, the Sierra foothills, and Sonoma and Napa counties.

---

## The Spiral

The insurance crisis is a spiral: private insurers leave high-risk areas (citing wildfire losses, reinsurance costs, and regulatory constraints on rate increases), homeowners are pushed to the FAIR Plan, the FAIR Plan's exposure grows, and the risk of a catastrophic loss event that exceeds the FAIR Plan's reserves increases. The FAIR Plan's surplus (its reserve against losses) is approximately $200 million, a fraction of its $6 billion exposure. If a major wildfire destroys thousands of FAIR Plan-insured homes simultaneously, the plan would need to assess all private insurers in California for the shortfall, potentially destabilizing the broader insurance market.

Commissioner Lara's Sustainable Insurance Strategy (2023-2024) attempted to address the crisis by allowing insurers to use forward-looking catastrophe models (rather than historical loss data) in rate setting and to include reinsurance costs in their rates, in exchange for commitments to write policies in wildfire-prone areas. The reform essentially allows insurers to raise rates significantly (potentially 30 to 50 percent in high-risk zones) in exchange for remaining in the market. Whether the rate increases are sufficient to attract insurers back, or whether they merely make insurance unaffordable for middle-income homeowners in fire zones, is the central uncertainty.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's insurance crisis is a regulatory failure compounding a climate reality. For decades, the Department of Insurance held rates below actuarial levels in wildfire-prone areas (through Proposition 103's prior-approval rate regulation), creating an implicit subsidy that encouraged development in the wildland-urban interface. When wildfire losses spiked (the 2017 and 2018 fire seasons produced $30 billion in insured losses), insurers discovered that regulated rates could not cover actual risk, and they withdrew. The FAIR Plan, designed as a temporary backstop for a small number of hard-to-insure properties, is now the primary insurer for 400,000 homes with insufficient reserves to survive a catastrophic event. The state maintained the illusion of affordable insurance by suppressing rates until the illusion became untenable.

The generous read is that the insurance crisis is being addressed through structural reform. The Sustainable Insurance Strategy resets the regulatory framework to allow actuarially sound pricing, which is the prerequisite for private market re-entry. The FAIR Plan's growth, while alarming, reflects a functioning backstop: no California homeowner is uninsurable. The plan exists specifically for this purpose. The reforms also include wildfire mitigation requirements (the Safer from Wildfires framework), which condition insurance availability on community-level fire hardening, creating incentives for the defensible space, building code compliance, and community fire planning that actually reduce risk rather than simply pricing it.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Department of Insurance publishes FAIR Plan policy counts and the California FAIR Plan Association publishes financial reports. Neither publishes granular data on FAIR Plan policyholders (income, property value, geographic concentration, length of time on FAIR Plan, prior insurer) that would allow assessment of who bears the cost of the insurance market's retreat and whether the FAIR Plan disproportionately affects lower-income homeowners who cannot afford either FAIR Plan premiums or the private alternatives.
