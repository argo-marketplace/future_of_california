# California Counts: The Valley and the State
## What $2 trillion in market capitalization, 1.9 million tech workers, 30 percent of U.S. venture capital, and a $4.1 trillion GDP built on five companies reveal about the industry that makes California rich and makes California's budget a derivative of its stock price

California's technology sector, broadly defined to include software, hardware, internet services, semiconductor design, and related industries, employs approximately 1.9 million workers and generates approximately $900 billion in annual output, roughly 22 percent of the state's $4.1 trillion GDP. The sector is concentrated to a degree unmatched by any other industry in any other state: the five largest California-headquartered technology companies (Apple, Alphabet/Google, Meta, Nvidia, and Broadcom) had a combined market capitalization exceeding $12 trillion in early 2026. The personal income tax paid by their employees, executives, and shareholders constitutes a significant portion of the state's tax revenue, a dependence documented in the "One Percent Budget" installment of this series.

The geographic concentration is equally extreme. Santa Clara County (the heart of Silicon Valley) alone accounts for approximately $330 billion in annual GDP, more than 20 individual U.S. states. San Francisco County adds approximately $200 billion. Together, two counties with a combined population of approximately 2.8 million people (7 percent of the state's population) generate approximately 13 percent of California's GDP. The concentration has produced the highest median household incomes in the nation ($140,000 in Santa Clara County, $135,000 in San Francisco), the highest housing costs, and a workforce that is simultaneously one of the most highly compensated and one of the most geographically immobile (tethered to a region where the median home costs $1.5 million to $2 million).

## The AI Boom and the Revenue Question

The artificial intelligence boom that began with the release of ChatGPT in late 2022 has concentrated even more economic value in California. The leading AI companies (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta AI) are headquartered or have major operations in the Bay Area. Nvidia, whose GPU chips power the AI computing infrastructure, has seen its market capitalization increase from approximately $350 billion in early 2023 to over $3 trillion, a roughly 10x increase that has generated enormous capital gains tax revenue for California.

The revenue bonanza from the AI boom is visible in the state's fiscal projections. Capital gains realizations in California are estimated to have exceeded $300 billion in the most recent tax year, driven heavily by the appreciation of technology stocks. The resulting income tax revenue has produced a budget surplus after two years of deficits. But the boom is also a demonstration of the fiscal vulnerability documented throughout this series: California's budget is dependent on the market performance of a handful of companies in a single industry in a single region. A correction in AI-related stocks, a regulatory crackdown on AI development, or a shift of AI research to other states or countries would produce a revenue decline that no amount of fiscal management could absorb.

## The Two Californias

The technology sector has produced a bifurcation of the California economy into what economists have called "two Californias." The coastal technology corridor (San Francisco, Silicon Valley, parts of Los Angeles, and San Diego's biotech cluster) produces high wages, low unemployment, and extreme housing costs. The interior California (Central Valley, Inland Empire, far north) produces lower wages, higher unemployment, and a cost of living that is more affordable but still elevated by California standards. A software engineer in Palo Alto earns a median of $200,000 per year and pays $4,000 per month for a one-bedroom apartment. A warehouse worker in Stockton earns $40,000 per year and pays $1,800 per month. Both are California workers. Their economic realities share a state name and little else.

The technology sector's growth has not produced broad-based prosperity. The income gains of the past two decades in California have accrued overwhelmingly to workers in the top income quintile, driven by technology sector compensation. The bottom three quintiles have seen real income growth at or below the national average, while bearing housing and living costs above the national average. The state's Gini coefficient (a measure of income inequality) is approximately 0.49, the third highest in the nation, behind only New York and Connecticut.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has allowed its economy, its budget, and its governance capacity to become dependent on an industry that employs 5 percent of its workforce, occupies 2 percent of its geography, and generates a disproportionate share of its tax revenue, its cultural identity, and its political power. The technology sector's interests, immigration policy that provides H-1B workers, housing policy that does not threaten stock-option-enriched homeowners too aggressively, tax policy that does not drive companies to Texas or Florida, constrain the state's policy options in ways that serve 1.9 million tech workers at the expense of 18 million non-tech workers. California did not choose to become a technology monoculture. It became one through the compound effects of venture capital concentration, university research investment, agglomeration economies, and the self-reinforcing dynamics of talent clustering. The result is a state whose fiscal health is a bet on the stock prices of five companies.

The generous read is that the technology sector is the reason California is the fifth-largest economy in the world rather than a large agricultural state with nice weather. The $900 billion in annual tech sector output funds the public services that the rest of the state depends on: the Medi-Cal program, the K-12 system, the university system, the infrastructure investments. The concentration of technology talent in California produces innovation that benefits the entire world: the AI systems, the electric vehicle technology, the semiconductor designs, the software platforms that define the modern economy are disproportionately developed in California. The fiscal vulnerability is real, but the alternative, an economy without the technology sector, would be a California with dramatically less revenue and dramatically fewer resources for the public investments it needs.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The EDD publishes employment and wage data by industry and county. The Franchise Tax Board publishes income tax data by income bracket. Neither publishes a crosswalk that links specific industry employment to specific tax revenue, which would quantify exactly how much of the state's income tax revenue is generated by the technology sector versus other industries. The dependence is estimated, not measured, because the tax data does not include industry codes.
