# California Counts: The Shelter That Isn't
## What 170,000 domestic violence calls per year, 104 licensed shelters, 8,000 beds, and a 60 percent turn-away rate reveal about the crisis response system California funds at a fraction of need while domestic violence remains the leading cause of injury to women

California law enforcement receives approximately 170,000 domestic violence-related calls for service per year. The calls produce approximately 100,000 incident reports and approximately 40,000 arrests. The California Department of Justice reports approximately 170 domestic violence-related homicides per year, a figure that has remained roughly stable for the past decade despite fluctuations in other violent crime categories. Domestic violence is the leading single cause of injury to women ages 15 to 44 in California, exceeding car accidents, muggings, and stranger assaults combined.

The state's domestic violence shelter system consists of approximately 104 licensed shelters operated by nonprofit organizations, providing approximately 8,000 beds. The shelters are funded through a combination of state General Fund appropriations (approximately $50 million per year through the Governor's Office of Emergency Services, CalOES), federal VAWA (Violence Against Women Act) grants (approximately $30 million), county contributions, and private fundraising. Total annual funding for California's domestic violence services is approximately $150 million, covering shelters, hotlines, legal services, counseling, children's programs, and transitional housing.

---

## The Capacity Crisis

The 8,000 shelter beds serve a population that generates 170,000 calls per year. The California Partnership to End Domestic Violence reports that shelters turn away approximately 60 percent of requests for admission due to capacity constraints. On any given night, California's domestic violence shelters are full, and survivors seeking safety are referred to waiting lists, motels (when vouchers are available), or general homeless shelters (which are not designed for the safety and confidentiality that domestic violence survivors require).

The turn-away rate is highest in rural counties (where shelters may have 10 to 20 beds for an entire county) and in regions with high housing costs (where transitional housing after shelter is unaffordable). The average shelter stay is 30 to 60 days, after which survivors must find permanent housing, an effectively impossible task for someone who has fled with no financial resources in a state where the median rent is $2,000 per month. The result is a system that provides temporary crisis response (30 to 60 days of safety) but cannot provide the stable housing, economic independence, and sustained safety planning that prevent returns to abusive situations.

---

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's domestic violence service system is crisis-only: it provides emergency shelter for a fraction of those who need it, for a duration too short to establish safety, in a housing market that prevents permanent escape. The $150 million annual investment is approximately $880 per domestic violence call, approximately $1,500 per incident report, and approximately $19,000 per shelter bed per year. The per-bed funding is insufficient to pay staff a living wage (domestic violence shelter workers earn approximately $18 to $25 per hour, among the lowest-paid social service workers in the state), maintain facilities, and provide the wraparound services (legal advocacy, counseling, economic empowerment, children's therapy) that enable survivors to build independent lives. The 60 percent turn-away rate means the system fails the majority of people who ask for help at their most dangerous moment.

The generous read is that California's domestic violence shelters save lives: research consistently finds that shelter access reduces the likelihood of further violence by 50 to 70 percent in the year following shelter stay. The 104 shelters and the hotline network (the National Domestic Violence Hotline receives approximately 50,000 calls per year from California) provide safety planning, legal advocacy, and connections to services even for survivors who cannot access shelter beds. Legislative reforms (restraining order improvements, firearms surrender requirements, confidential address programs, workplace protections for survivors) have expanded the legal tools available to domestic violence survivors. The system is underfunded but not ineffective: it serves approximately 50,000 survivors per year through shelter, counseling, legal services, and other programs.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CalOES collects shelter utilization data and the DOJ publishes domestic violence crime data. Neither publishes a comprehensive assessment of unmet need: how many survivors seek services and do not receive them, what happens to the 60 percent turned away from shelter, and whether funded services produce long-term safety (freedom from further violence, stable housing, economic independence). The pipeline from crisis call to long-term safety is not tracked across the multiple systems (law enforcement, courts, shelters, housing, social services) that a survivor navigates.
