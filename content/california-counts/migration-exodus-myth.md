# California Counts: The Exodus That Wasn't
## What 817,000 departures, 475,000 arrivals, a net loss of 342,000, and a $3.6 trillion GDP reveal about the migration narrative that launched a thousand op-eds

Between 2020 and 2023, approximately 817,000 more people left California for other states than moved in from other states. This is the number that launched the exodus narrative: California is emptying, fleeing its taxes, its regulations, its cost of living, its politics. The narrative is not false. It is incomplete. During the same period, approximately 700,000 international immigrants arrived, partially offsetting domestic losses. California's total population declined from 39.5 million to approximately 38.9 million, a drop of roughly 1.5 percent, before stabilizing and ticking upward in 2024-25 to approximately 39.1 million. The state that was supposedly emptying lost fewer people, as a percentage of population, than West Virginia, Louisiana, or Illinois. It retained the largest economy, the largest tax base, and the largest population of any state. The exodus is real in the data and exaggerated in the discourse. What the migration numbers actually reveal is not a state in collapse but a state in economic sorting: expensive for the middle class, hostile to the working poor, and still magnetic for the highly skilled, the immigrant, and the ambitious.

---

## Who Leaves

The IRS Statistics of Income migration data, which tracks tax returns filed in different states year over year, provides the most granular picture of who leaves California. The data shows that net domestic out-migration is concentrated in specific income bands and demographics.

Middle-income households, those earning $50,000 to $200,000 per year, account for the largest share of net domestic out-migration by volume. These are the households most squeezed by California's cost structure: too wealthy for public assistance, too poor for the housing market. A two-income household earning $150,000, comfortable by national standards, faces median rents of $2,800 per month and median home prices of $750,000 in metropolitan California. The same household in Austin, Phoenix, Nashville, or Boise can rent for $1,800 or buy for $400,000. The arithmetic is not subtle. It is the reason the moving trucks on Interstate 10 are fuller heading east than west.

High-income households (above $200,000) show a smaller but notable net outflow, driven in part by California's top marginal income tax rate of 13.3 percent, the highest in the nation. The outflow accelerated during the pandemic as remote work decoupled high-paying jobs from California locations. Tech workers, financial professionals, and entrepreneurs moved to Nevada (no income tax), Texas (no income tax), Florida (no income tax), and Tennessee (no income tax) while retaining California-level salaries. The Franchise Tax Board estimated that the state lost approximately $29 billion in adjusted gross income to out-migration between 2020 and 2022, representing roughly $3.5 billion in foregone income tax revenue.

Low-income households (below $50,000) have the highest out-migration rate relative to their population share. They are being priced out of the state entirely: unable to afford rent, ineligible for the limited affordable housing stock, and facing a cost of living that consumes their income. Many move to the Inland Empire or Central Valley first, California's internal pressure valve, before eventually leaving the state when those regions too become unaffordable.

## Who Arrives

The exodus narrative focuses on departures and ignores arrivals. California continues to attract two populations at high rates: international immigrants and high-income domestic migrants in specific industries.

International immigration has historically been California's population engine. The state receives approximately 175,000 to 225,000 international immigrants per year, including H-1B visa holders in technology and healthcare, family-based immigrants, refugees, and undocumented arrivals. The immigrant population is approximately 10.5 million, or 27 percent of the state's total, the highest share of any state. International immigrants account for roughly 34 percent of California's STEM workforce, 40 percent of its agricultural workforce, and 38 percent of its healthcare workers. They are also responsible for approximately 40 percent of the state's patent filings and a disproportionate share of business formation.

Domestic in-migration, while smaller than out-migration, is not negligible. Approximately 475,000 people moved to California from other states between 2020 and 2023. The in-migrants skew younger (25 to 39), more educated (disproportionately holding bachelor's or advanced degrees), and are concentrated in high-wage industries: technology, entertainment, biotech, finance, and professional services. The San Francisco Bay Area, despite its domestic out-migration in aggregate, continues to attract domestic in-migrants in AI, venture capital, and biotech at rates that partially offset the loss of mid-career workers and families.

The net effect is demographic sorting. California is losing its middle: the teachers, firefighters, nurses, construction workers, and small business owners who earn too much for subsidies and too little for the housing market. It is retaining and attracting the top (high-income professionals, entrepreneurs, international talent) and struggling to retain the bottom (low-wage workers who have no alternative). The state's income distribution is hollowing, a pattern that mirrors its housing market: a shrinking middle tier between luxury and subsidized.

## The Tax Revenue Paradox

The $29 billion in adjusted gross income lost to out-migration between 2020 and 2022 is real and significant. It is also dwarfed by the income generated by those who remain. California's total personal income exceeded $3.3 trillion in 2024. Its personal income tax revenue, approximately $115 billion, is the single largest revenue source in the state budget and remains at or near historic highs despite net out-migration.

The paradox resolves when you examine the income distribution of migrants versus residents. The average income of those who leave is approximately $80,000 to $110,000 (middle to upper-middle class). The average income of those who remain and those who arrive internationally skews both lower (immigrants in service industries) and higher (tech workers, executives, professionals in the state's dominant industries). California's income tax is steeply progressive: the top 1 percent of earners pay approximately 50 percent of all personal income tax revenue. As long as that top 1 percent remains, the state budget is disproportionately insulated from the loss of middle-income households.

This is the structural fragility beneath the revenue resilience. California's budget depends on a small number of very high earners who are mobile, often unmarried to any particular geography, and increasingly able to work remotely. A stock market downturn, a tech industry contraction, or a threshold shift in remote work norms that causes even 5 to 10 percent of top earners to relocate would produce a budget impact far larger than the current middle-class out-migration. Capital gains income, which constitutes roughly 10 to 15 percent of general fund revenue in good years, is the most volatile component and the most geographically mobile. The tax base is concentrated, and concentrated tax bases are fragile. California's budget has not yet felt the exodus because the people who pay most of the taxes have not yet left. Whether they will is the $115 billion question.

## Where They Go

The top destinations for Californians leaving the state, by volume, are Texas, Arizona, Nevada, Washington, Oregon, and Florida. The destinations reflect a gradient of motivations.

Texas receives the largest absolute number of California migrants, approximately 100,000 to 120,000 per year. The appeal is no state income tax, lower housing costs (median home price roughly $300,000 versus $750,000), and a business environment perceived as less regulated. Austin has absorbed a disproportionate share of California's tech out-migration, growing its technology sector by approximately 40 percent between 2019 and 2024. Dallas and Houston have attracted corporate relocations: Tesla, Oracle, Hewlett Packard Enterprise, and Charles Schwab all moved headquarters to Texas.

Arizona, particularly the Phoenix metropolitan area, receives approximately 70,000 to 80,000 California migrants per year. Phoenix's housing stock, built rapidly on cheap desert land, absorbs demand that California's constrained housing market cannot. The median home price in Phoenix is roughly $420,000, half of California's comparable markets. Nevada (Las Vegas and Reno) attracts approximately 50,000 to 60,000 per year, again driven by no income tax and lower housing costs. Oregon and Washington receive migrants from Northern California attracted to the Pacific Northwest lifestyle at lower cost, though Portland and Seattle's own housing costs have risen substantially.

The irony of the exodus destinations is that California's migrants bring with them the economic behaviors, housing preferences, and political attitudes that California's critics attribute to the state's problems. Austin's housing prices have risen 50 percent since the tech in-migration began. Phoenix's water supply is strained by population growth in a desert. Boise's locals complain about California transplants driving up home prices. The migration does not solve the cost-of-living problem. It redistributes it.

## The Corporate Relocation Narrative

The corporate relocation story is real but narrower than the narrative suggests. Between 2018 and 2024, approximately 350 companies relocated their headquarters from California to other states, according to tracking by the Hoover Institution. The most prominent: Tesla (to Austin), Oracle (to Austin, later Nashville), Hewlett Packard Enterprise (to Houston), Charles Schwab (to Dallas), and various venture capital and crypto firms to Miami. Each relocation generated headlines that reinforced the exodus narrative.

What the headline count does not capture is that many "relocations" involve moving a headquarters office of 50 to 200 employees while retaining engineering, design, and operational centers of thousands of employees in California. Tesla's headquarters is in Austin. Its largest factory, its design center, and approximately 20,000 California employees remain. Oracle relocated its headquarters but retained its Redwood City campus. The corporate relocation is often an executive mailing address change, not an operational departure.

Meanwhile, California continues to attract new companies and investment at rates that dwarf the departures. Venture capital investment in California companies totaled approximately $125 billion in 2024, roughly half of all U.S. venture investment. The San Francisco Bay Area remains the global center of AI development, with OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and Meta AI all headquartered or primarily based in the region. The biotech industry in San Diego and the Bay Area attracted approximately $25 billion in venture funding. Entertainment production in Los Angeles, despite runaway production to other states and countries, generated approximately $36 billion in economic output.

The corporate relocation narrative describes a real phenomenon at the margin. The venture capital and innovation narrative describes the core. The margin is moving. The core is not.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California is losing the people it most needs and retaining the people who least need the state. The middle class, the workforce that staffs schools, fills hospitals, responds to emergencies, and sustains the small businesses that constitute community life, is being pushed out by a housing market that the state's own regulatory apparatus created. The loss of $29 billion in adjusted gross income in three years is not a rounding error. The 350 corporate relocations are not noise. The structural dependence on the top 1 percent for 50 percent of income tax revenue is not stability: it is the fiscal equivalent of building a city on a fault line. The state is becoming a luxury product: available to those who can afford it, irrelevant to those who cannot. The international immigration that backfills the population loss is not equivalent: immigrants arrive with lower average incomes, higher service needs, and longer timelines to full economic contribution. California is substituting a middle-class tax base with a bifurcated one, and the political consequences of a state where the majority of residents cannot afford to buy a home are already visible in the ballot measures, housing protests, and anti-development backlash that constrain the supply response that could solve the underlying problem.

The generous read is that California's population dynamics reflect exactly what you would expect from the world's fifth-largest economy adjusting to a post-pandemic reality. Domestic out-migration from high-cost states is a national pattern: New York, New Jersey, Connecticut, and Massachusetts all experienced similar trends. California's GDP grew through the migration period, reaching $3.6 trillion in 2024. Its unemployment rate stabilized below 5 percent. Its tax revenue, after a pandemic dip and a 2023-24 correction, remains among the highest in state history. The people arriving, international immigrants and high-skilled domestic migrants, bring the talent that sustains the innovation economy. The people leaving, primarily middle-income households seeking lower costs, are making rational choices that are painful for California but beneficial for their families. The state's challenge is housing supply, not population flight: if California built housing at the rate Texas does, the cost pressure that drives out-migration would ease, and the narrative would reverse. The exodus is a symptom of a housing shortage, not a verdict on the state's economy, governance, or future.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The IRS migration data captures tax returns that move between states. It does not capture why people move. Post-hoc surveys, including those by the California Policy Lab, find that the top reason for leaving is housing cost (approximately 50 to 55 percent of respondents), followed by employment (15 to 20 percent), family proximity (10 to 15 percent), and quality of life (10 percent). Taxes and politics, the reasons most cited in op-eds and social media, are mentioned by approximately 5 to 10 percent of departing households. The narrative that California is emptying because of progressive politics is not supported by the migration data. The narrative that it is emptying because of housing costs is.

The data does not capture the experience of those who want to leave but cannot afford to. Moving is expensive: first and last month's rent, moving costs, job transition, and the opportunity cost of leaving a network. For low-income Californians, the cost of leaving is itself a barrier, creating a population of "stuck" residents who would relocate if they could but remain in California in housing situations, doubling up with family, couch surfing, or paying 50 to 60 percent of income in rent, that the migration statistics do not describe as housing distress because the individual has not moved.

The data also does not capture return migration. An unknown but nontrivial number of Californians who relocate to Texas, Arizona, or Nevada return within two to five years, finding that lower housing costs come with tradeoffs they did not anticipate: extreme heat, limited public services, lower-quality schools, or the absence of the cultural and natural amenities that California provides. No systematic tracking of return migration exists. The exodus, to the extent it is real, may be partially reversible. The data does not tell us, because no one is collecting it.

---

California has always been a state people move to and move from. The Gold Rush was a migration. The Dust Bowl was a migration. The postwar suburban boom was a migration. The tech boom of the 1990s was a migration. In each era, the arrivals and departures reshaped the state's economy, demographics, and politics. The current migration is no different in kind. It is different in direction: for the first time since the Great Depression, more Americans are leaving California than arriving. But the state's total economic output continues to grow. Its population, after a brief dip, is recovering. Its innovation economy remains without peer. The exodus is real for the families who pack their trucks and drive east on I-10, their reasons valid and their loss felt in the schools and neighborhoods they leave behind. It is not real in the sense that the op-ed pages need it to be: California is not dying, collapsing, or emptying. It is sorting. And the question the sorting raises is not whether the state will survive the departure of its middle class, but what kind of state it becomes without one.
