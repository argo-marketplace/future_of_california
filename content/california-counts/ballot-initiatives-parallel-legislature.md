# California Counts: Government by Petition
## What 2,074 initiatives filed, $1.2 billion spent in a single cycle, and an 874,641-signature threshold reveal about the parallel legislature that voters built

California's initiative process allows any citizen to propose a law or constitutional amendment, collect enough signatures to place it on the ballot, and enact it by majority vote, bypassing the legislature entirely. Since 1912, when Progressive-era reformers added the initiative, referendum, and recall to the state constitution, Californians have filed 2,074 initiative proposals with the Attorney General's office. Of those, 407 qualified for the ballot. Of those, 139 were approved by voters. Those 139 measures have shaped nearly every major policy domain in the state: taxation (Proposition 13), criminal sentencing (Three Strikes), education funding (Proposition 98), insurance regulation (Proposition 103), affirmative action (Proposition 209), marijuana legalization (Proposition 64), and the structure of state government itself (the citizens redistricting commission, the top-two primary, and the rainy day fund). The initiative process is California's parallel legislature, one that meets every two to four years, takes no testimony, holds no committee hearings, permits no amendments, and produces laws that the actual legislature cannot change without another vote of the people.

---

## The Signature Industry

Qualifying an initiative for the ballot requires collecting valid signatures from registered voters equal to 5 percent of the votes cast in the most recent gubernatorial election for a statute, or 8 percent for a constitutional amendment. For the 2026 cycle, the thresholds are approximately 546,651 signatures for a statute and 874,641 for a constitutional amendment. Campaigns typically collect 30 to 50 percent more signatures than required to account for invalid signatures identified during the Secretary of State's verification process.

The signature-gathering industry in California is a specialized business dominated by a handful of firms. Petition circulators, the people who stand outside grocery stores and shopping malls with clipboards, are overwhelmingly paid per signature rather than salaried. The going rate per valid signature has risen from approximately $1 to $2 in the 1990s to $5 to $15 in the 2020s, depending on the complexity of the measure, the time pressure, and the competitive landscape (when multiple initiatives are circulating simultaneously, firms bid up circulators' pay). A constitutional amendment requiring 874,641 valid signatures, with a 40 percent cushion for invalids, requires collecting approximately 1.2 million raw signatures. At $8 to $12 per signature, the cost of qualification alone is $10 million to $15 million before a single campaign ad airs.

This cost is the first filter. The initiative process is nominally open to any citizen. In practice, it is available to those who can raise $10 million to $15 million for qualification and $30 million to $100 million or more for a campaign. The typical initiative proponent in modern California is not a citizen reformer with a clipboard. It is a corporation, an industry association, a labor union, a billionaire, or a political party. The 2024 ballot included measures backed by Uber, Lyft, and DoorDash (spending $200 million on a single measure in 2020 for Proposition 22), the dialysis industry, the gambling industry, and various healthcare and environmental interests. The populist reform tool of 1912 has become the most expensive form of legislation in American democracy.

## The Spending: Billions at the Ballot

Campaign spending on California ballot measures has escalated exponentially. In the 2020 general election cycle, total spending on the 12 statewide ballot measures exceeded $1.2 billion, the most expensive initiative election in the state's history. Proposition 22, the app-based driver classification measure backed by Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, and Instacart, alone attracted $224 million in total spending: $204 million by the Yes campaign (the companies) and $20 million by the No campaign (labor unions). It was the most expensive ballot measure campaign in American history.

The spending is lopsided. A 2023 analysis by the California Fair Political Practices Commission found that in contested ballot measure campaigns, the side with more money wins approximately 80 percent of the time when spending exceeds $10 million. Below that threshold, outcomes are more competitive. The correlation between spending and outcome does not prove causation (popular measures attract more funding), but it establishes a practical reality: defeating a well-funded initiative requires tens of millions of dollars. Mounting a successful initiative against organized opposition requires hundreds of millions.

The spending goes primarily to television and digital advertising, followed by direct mail, field operations, and consultants. California's media markets are among the most expensive in the country. The Los Angeles media market alone reaches 5.5 million households and charges premium rates for political advertising. A statewide television campaign sufficient to move opinion on a ballot measure costs $30 million to $60 million. Digital advertising has grown to roughly 30 to 40 percent of ballot measure spending, up from negligible amounts a decade ago.

The economic ecosystem surrounding ballot measures employs thousands of Californians: signature gatherers, campaign consultants, pollsters, media buyers, direct mail firms, field organizers, and attorneys. The initiative industry has its own trade associations, annual conferences, and lobbying operations. It is a permanent infrastructure that exists to produce ballot measures regardless of whether any particular policy need calls for one.

## The Amendment Problem

The most consequential feature of California's initiative process is that initiative statutes can only be amended by another vote of the people, unless the initiative itself authorizes legislative amendment. Most initiatives do not include such authorization. The result is that voter-enacted laws exist in a kind of constitutional amber: once passed, they can be modified only by qualifying and passing another initiative, a process that costs tens of millions of dollars and requires a statewide election.

This rigidity creates policy fossils. Proposition 13, passed in 1978, capped property tax rates at 1 percent of assessed value and limited annual assessment increases to 2 percent until sale. The measure was a rational response to property tax spikes that were forcing elderly homeowners out of their homes. Forty-eight years later, its provisions create a system where identical houses on the same street can have assessed values (and therefore tax bills) differing by a factor of 10, depending on when each was last sold. A home purchased in 1978 for $50,000 might have an assessed value of $130,000. An identical home next door purchased in 2023 for $1.2 million is assessed at $1.2 million. The first owner pays approximately $1,300 per year in property tax. The second pays approximately $12,000. Commercial property receives the same protection, leading to the Proposition 15 campaign in 2020 (the "split roll" initiative) that would have reassessed commercial property at market value. It failed, 52 to 48 percent.

Three Strikes, passed as Proposition 184 in 1994, required a sentence of 25 years to life for any felony committed by a defendant with two prior serious or violent felony convictions. The measure produced sentences of 25 years to life for third-strike offenses as minor as shoplifting a $2.50 pair of socks and stealing a slice of pizza. It could not be reformed by the legislature. It took 18 years and another initiative, Proposition 36 in 2012, to modify the law so that the third strike must be a serious or violent felony. During those 18 years, thousands of inmates served decades-long sentences for minor offenses because the only way to change a voter-enacted law was to pass another one.

The constitutional amendment process is even more rigid. Initiatives that amend the California Constitution can be changed only by another constitutional amendment, which requires 874,641 signatures and a majority vote. The California Constitution, at approximately 75,000 words, is the third-longest state constitution in the nation (behind Alabama and Texas) and one of the most frequently amended, in significant part because the initiative process makes it relatively easy to add provisions and extremely difficult to remove them.

## The Counter-Initiative Arms Race

The initiative process has spawned a tactical innovation: the counter-initiative. When one interest group qualifies an initiative that threatens another, the threatened group qualifies its own initiative designed to nullify, supersede, or preempt the original. If both pass, the one receiving more votes takes effect. If the counter-initiative passes and the original fails, the threat is eliminated. The counter-initiative is less a tool of democratic deliberation than a form of legislative warfare conducted through the ballot.

The 2024 election cycle featured several examples. The gambling industry saw competing measures on sports betting, with tribal casinos and commercial operators each backing rival initiatives. Healthcare and labor interests have filed competing measures on multiple occasions. The insurance industry has used counter-initiatives to neutralize consumer protection measures. In each case, voters face a ballot with multiple measures on the same topic, often with deliberately confusing language, funded by tens of millions from opposing industries.

The effect on voters is measurable. Voter drop-off on ballot measures increases with ballot complexity. In elections with 10 or more statewide measures, the percentage of voters who cast ballots on the last measure on the ballot is 10 to 15 percentage points lower than the percentage who vote on the first. Complex ballot language compounds the problem. A 2022 study by the Public Policy Institute of California found that 72 percent of voters said ballot measures were "too complicated and confusing" and that 56 percent reported sometimes voting on measures they did not fully understand.

## The Unintended Consequences Archive

The initiative process does not include the safeguards that exist in legislative lawmaking: committee hearings, expert testimony, fiscal analysis before passage (the LAO provides analysis, but voters may not read it), amendment in conference, or the governor's veto. The result is a body of voter-enacted law that includes provisions no legislature would have passed with full information.

Proposition 65 (1986) required businesses to warn consumers about exposure to chemicals known to cause cancer or reproductive harm. Forty years later, the warnings are so ubiquitous, appearing on coffee, parking garages, apartment buildings, furniture, and theme parks, that they convey no useful information. The measure created a private enforcement mechanism that generates approximately $35 million per year in settlements, the majority of which goes to attorneys rather than affected consumers. The legislature cannot reform it without voter approval.

Proposition 47 (2014) reclassified certain nonviolent property crimes and drug offenses from felonies to misdemeanors. The measure reduced prison populations and generated savings redirected to mental health and substance abuse treatment. It also created a practical threshold: theft of property valued below $950 was a misdemeanor, leading to organized retail theft operations calibrated to stay below the threshold. Whether Proposition 47 caused the subsequent increase in retail theft is debated by criminologists. That the debate exists and the legislature could not adjust the threshold without another initiative is the structural point.

Proposition 22 (2020) classified app-based transportation and delivery drivers as independent contractors rather than employees, overriding AB 5, a legislature-enacted law. The measure included a provision requiring a seven-eighths supermajority of the legislature (58 of 80 assembly members, 32 of 40 senators) to amend it, and even then only in ways "consistent with" the measure's intent. This provision effectively made a statute passed by 58 percent of voters harder to change than the U.S. Constitution, which requires two-thirds of both houses of Congress. A court later struck down this provision as unconstitutional, but the underlying classification remains voter-enacted law.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's initiative process has been captured by the interests it was designed to circumvent. The Progressive reformers of 1912 created direct democracy to break the Southern Pacific Railroad's grip on the legislature. A century later, the initiative process is the most expensive legislative forum in America, accessible only to those who can raise $10 million for signatures and $100 million for campaigns. Corporations use it to override labor laws (Proposition 22). Industries use counter-initiatives to block regulation. Wealthy individuals use it to enact personal policy preferences. The resulting laws cannot be amended, creating a body of rigid statute and constitutional provision that accumulates like sediment, each layer constraining the legislature's ability to govern. The 75,000-word California Constitution, the ballot with 12 measures that 56 percent of voters do not fully understand, the Proposition 65 warnings that warn about everything and therefore nothing: these are the products of a system that treats lawmaking as a marketing exercise and voters as a focus group to be persuaded rather than a deliberative body to be informed.

The generous read is that the initiative process remains the most powerful tool of democratic self-governance in any American state. The legislature refused to address property taxes; voters passed Proposition 13. The legislature refused to guarantee education funding; voters passed Proposition 98. The legislature would not legalize marijuana; voters passed Proposition 64. The legislature could not reform criminal sentencing; voters passed Proposition 36. The legislature would not create an independent redistricting commission; voters passed Propositions 11 and 20. Every major structural reform in California over the past 50 years has come through the initiative process because the legislature is structurally incapable of enacting reforms that threaten incumbent interests. The cost of the process is high, and the corporate capture is real, but the alternative, relying exclusively on a legislature that is itself captured by donors, lobbyists, and party structures, is not obviously superior. The initiative process is expensive democracy. The legislature is inexpensive oligarchy. The voters, whatever their confusion, have chosen the former when the latter fails them. That is not a system failure. It is the system working.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Secretary of State's database tracks initiative filings, signature counts, qualification, election results, and campaign finance reports. It does not track the downstream effects of passed initiatives: how they interact with existing law, whether they achieve their stated goals, what unintended consequences emerge, or what they cost to implement. The fiscal impact estimates provided to voters before elections are produced under severe time constraints and routinely underestimate both costs and savings.

The data does not capture the legislative shadow of the initiative process. The threat of an initiative often drives legislative action: the legislature passes a bill to preempt a measure that has qualified or is gathering signatures. In some cases, initiative proponents file measures with no intention of going to the ballot, using the qualification process as leverage to force legislative concessions. This shadow legislation is invisible in election data but shapes policy outcomes significantly. How many bills have been passed or modified because of the implicit threat of an initiative that was never filed is unknowable.

The data also does not capture what political scientists call "direct democracy fatigue." California voters are asked to decide complex policy questions, tax rates, bond authorizations, criminal sentencing structures, and regulatory frameworks, that in other states are decided by legislators who devote full-time attention to the issues. The informational burden on voters is enormous. Whether voters make good decisions under these conditions is debated. What is measurable is that voter participation on ballot measures declines with ballot length, that voter knowledge of measure content is low, and that campaign spending is the strongest predictor of outcomes. Whether this constitutes democracy or its simulation depends on one's theory of what democracy requires: informed deliberation, or the right to decide regardless of information.

---

California governs itself through two legislatures. One meets in Sacramento, holds hearings, takes testimony, and amends bills through a deliberative process that, however imperfect, is designed to produce informed law. The other meets at the ballot box, takes no testimony, holds no hearings, permits no amendments, and produces laws that the first legislature cannot change. The second legislature has enacted some of the most consequential policy in the state's history. It has also produced rigid, unintended, and occasionally absurd outcomes that persist for decades because the only cure for a bad initiative is a good one, and a good one costs $50 million. The initiative process was born from the conviction that the people should be able to make their own laws when the legislature will not. That conviction remains defensible. What the Progressives of 1912 could not have anticipated is that the process of making laws by petition would become a billion-dollar industry, and that the power they gave to the people would be exercised primarily by the interests that can afford to ask.
