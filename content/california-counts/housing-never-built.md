# California Counts: The Houses That Were Never Built
## What 2.5 million units short, 120,000 permits per year, a 3.5 million-unit RHNA target, and 62 percent of cities out of compliance reveal about the planning system that made California unaffordable

California needs approximately 2.5 million more housing units than it currently has, according to estimates by the Department of Housing and Community Development and the McKinsey Global Institute. The deficit accumulated over four decades of underproduction: between 1980 and 2020, California permitted approximately 100,000 to 120,000 housing units per year while population growth, household formation, and economic expansion demanded roughly 180,000 to 250,000. The gap, compounding annually, produced the housing shortage that drives every other crisis in the state: the homelessness, the out-migration, the unaffordable rents, the two-hour commutes, the farmworker overcrowding, and the generational divide between homeowners who bought in 1990 and renters who will never buy at all.

The state's Regional Housing Needs Allocation process, the mechanism by which HCD assigns housing production targets to every city and county, allocated approximately 2.5 million units for the current 6th RHNA cycle (2021-2029). The targets are based on population projections, existing housing need, and income categories (very low, low, moderate, and above moderate income). Cities are required to zone enough land at sufficient density to accommodate their RHNA allocation. They are not required to build anything. The distinction is the system's fatal design flaw: California mandates planning for housing but does not mandate producing it. A city can comply with RHNA by rezoning a parcel on paper while maintaining the permitting delays, design review requirements, and community opposition processes that ensure nothing is built on it for a decade.

## The Production Gap by the Numbers

California permitted approximately 120,000 housing units in 2024, roughly the same as it has averaged for the past decade. Of those, approximately 50,000 were single-family homes and 70,000 were multifamily units in buildings of five or more units. The ratio has shifted toward multifamily in recent years, reflecting both the state's density mandates and the economics of development: multifamily construction is the only housing type that pencils at current land and construction costs in most California metropolitan areas.

The 120,000-unit annual production rate is approximately one-third of what the state needs. The shortfall is not distributed evenly. Coastal metropolitan areas, where demand is highest and land is most constrained, produce the least relative to need. The Bay Area's RHNA allocation for the 6th cycle is approximately 441,000 units. Annual production has averaged roughly 15,000 to 20,000 units. At that rate, the region will meet approximately 35 to 40 percent of its eight-year allocation. The Southern California Association of Governments region has a RHNA allocation of approximately 1.3 million units. Annual production of roughly 50,000 to 60,000 units implies completion of approximately 35 percent. The Inland Empire and Central Valley produce closer to their proportional targets because land is cheaper and local opposition is less organized, but the jobs are in the coastal metros, producing the spatial mismatch between where housing is affordable and where employment exists.

The per-unit cost of construction in California averages approximately $450,000 to $550,000 for market-rate multifamily housing in the Bay Area and Los Angeles, roughly 50 percent above the national average. Labor costs, reflecting prevailing wage requirements and a construction workforce that has not grown proportionally to demand, account for approximately 40 percent of total cost. Materials account for 30 percent. Land, entitlement, and soft costs (architecture, engineering, permitting, legal, financing) account for the remaining 30 percent. The cost structure means that new market-rate housing in coastal California is buildable only at rents of $3,000 or more per month for a one-bedroom apartment, pricing out the middle-income households that the housing is nominally intended to serve.

## The Zoning Barrier

California has approximately 482 incorporated cities and 58 counties, each with independent land-use authority. Zoning, the legal framework that determines what can be built where, is set locally. Despite two decades of state legislation aimed at increasing housing production, local governments retain enormous discretion over the development process: design review, parking requirements, height limits, setback requirements, density restrictions, and conditional use permits collectively determine whether a project is financially viable.

The Housing Accountability Act, strengthened by SB 35 (2017) and SB 423 (2023), created streamlined approval pathways for housing projects that meet objective zoning standards. SB 9 (2021) allowed lot splits and duplexes in single-family zones. SB 10 (2021) enabled cities to voluntarily zone for up to 10-unit buildings near transit. AB 2011 (2022) allowed housing by right on commercially zoned land. The cumulative effect of these laws is to narrow the discretion that local governments exercise over housing approvals.

The response from many cities has been creative compliance: adopting zoning that technically meets RHNA requirements while maintaining approval processes that functionally prevent or delay construction. The Attorney General's Housing Strike Force, created in 2023, has sent violation notices to dozens of cities for failure to comply with housing element law. HCD has found approximately 62 percent of cities out of compliance with at least one housing law requirement as of 2025. The enforcement tools include loss of certain state funding and court-imposed remedies, but the practical capacity of state agencies to litigate against hundreds of noncompliant cities simultaneously is limited.

The political economy of local opposition to housing is structural. Existing homeowners benefit from housing scarcity: restricted supply increases property values. Homeowners vote in local elections at higher rates than renters. City councils and planning commissions are disproportionately composed of homeowners. The incentive structure produces decisions that protect existing property values at the expense of new housing production, a dynamic that economists call the "homevoter hypothesis" and that housing advocates call NIMBYism. Both descriptions are accurate.

## The Affordable Housing Math

Affordable housing, defined as housing restricted to households earning 30 to 80 percent of area median income, is the segment of the market where the gap between need and production is widest. California's RHNA allocations for the 6th cycle include approximately 700,000 units at very low income (below 50 percent AMI) and 430,000 at low income (50 to 80 percent AMI). Combined, these categories represent roughly 45 percent of the total RHNA allocation.

Market-rate development does not produce affordable housing. A new apartment in San Francisco renting for $3,500 per month is affordable to a household earning approximately $140,000 per year. A household at 50 percent of Bay Area AMI earns approximately $65,000 and can afford rent of approximately $1,625. The gap between what the market produces and what low-income households can pay is bridged by subsidies: Low Income Housing Tax Credits, state and local bond funds, inclusionary zoning requirements, and density bonuses.

The cost of producing one unit of deed-restricted affordable housing in California averages $600,000 to $700,000 in the Bay Area and Los Angeles, as documented by the Terner Center at UC Berkeley. At $650,000 per unit, producing the 1.1 million affordable units in the RHNA allocation would cost approximately $715 billion in capital alone, roughly twice the state's total annual budget. No combination of existing funding sources comes close to that figure. The state's annual affordable housing budget, combining LIHTC allocations, state bond funds (Proposition 1 of 2018 provided $4 billion), and local programs, produces approximately 10,000 to 15,000 deed-restricted affordable units per year. At that rate, meeting the affordable housing RHNA would take 73 to 110 years.

## The Prop 13 Connection

Proposition 13 (1978) is not usually discussed as a housing policy. It is one. By capping property tax at 1 percent of assessed value and limiting reassessment to 2 percent per year unless the property is sold, Prop 13 created a fiscal incentive structure for local governments that discourages housing and encourages commercial development. Property tax revenue from a new housing development, assessed at current market value, generates less net revenue for a city than the cost of services (schools, police, fire, roads, parks) that new residents demand. Commercial and retail development generates sales tax revenue, which cities keep, and property tax revenue, without the service costs that residential development imposes.

The result is a local fiscal incentive to approve retail centers and office parks over housing, a pattern called the "fiscalization of land use" that planning scholars have documented since the 1980s. Cities that approve housing do so despite the fiscal incentive, not because of it. Cities that resist housing are acting rationally within the fiscal framework Prop 13 created. The state has attempted to counteract this incentive through the RHNA process, streamlining mandates, and builder's remedy provisions, but the underlying fiscal structure remains: a city that approves 1,000 new housing units will spend more on services for those residents than it collects in property tax, a deficit that must be funded from other revenue sources or absorbed as a service reduction for existing residents.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's housing crisis is a policy choice, not a natural disaster. The state chose to give 482 cities independent land-use authority. The cities chose to zone for scarcity. The voters chose to pass Proposition 13, which made housing fiscally unattractive for local governments. The state legislature chose to mandate planning rather than production. The result is a housing deficit of 2.5 million units, median home prices of $750,000, median rents of $2,800, and a generation of Californians for whom homeownership is mathematically impossible without inherited wealth or a dual six-figure income. The state's housing laws, however numerous, have not produced housing. They have produced compliance processes, rezoning exercises, and Housing Element documents that describe the housing that should exist while the vacancy rate sits below 1 percent and the wait list for affordable housing in Los Angeles exceeds 10 years.

The generous read is that California has enacted more pro-housing legislation in the past eight years than in the previous fifty, and the effects are beginning to show. SB 35 streamlined approvals for 150,000 or more units in its first five years. SB 9 has generated thousands of lot split and duplex applications. AB 2011 opened commercial corridors to housing development by right. The Attorney General's enforcement actions and HCD's compliance reviews are creating real consequences for noncompliant cities for the first time. Annual housing production, while still insufficient, has increased from approximately 85,000 units in 2010 to 120,000 in 2024, a 40 percent increase. The trajectory is positive. The destination, 2.5 million units to close the deficit, is distant. But the legislative framework now in place, if enforced and sustained, represents the most serious state-level commitment to housing production in California's history.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

HCD publishes Housing Element compliance status, RHNA allocations, and annual progress reports from jurisdictions. The data shows what cities plan and permit. It does not show what is actually built and occupied. A permitted unit is not a built unit: approximately 15 to 20 percent of permitted multifamily units are never constructed due to financing failures, cost escalation, or developer decisions to abandon projects. The gap between permits and completions means that the already-insufficient permit numbers overstate actual production.

The data does not capture the informal housing market: garage conversions, unpermitted additions, and unauthorized dwelling units that house an estimated 300,000 to 500,000 California households. These units provide affordable housing in practice but do not appear in production statistics, may not meet safety standards, and exist in a legal gray zone that ADU legalization (AB 68, SB 13, and subsequent legislation) is gradually formalizing.

The data also does not capture the human cost of the housing shortage in units that are not commute hours, rent burdens, or homelessness statistics but in the decisions people make: the nurse who commutes two hours each way from Palmdale because she cannot afford to live near her hospital, the teacher who leaves the profession because her salary cannot cover rent, the young family that leaves the state because the down payment on a starter home exceeds their lifetime savings. The housing shortage is California's most consequential policy failure. Its costs are distributed across every other system the state operates: transportation, education, healthcare, child welfare, and the economy itself. Every California problem is, at some level, a housing problem. The data shows the units. It does not show the lives deformed by their absence.
