# California Counts: The Settlement Money
## What $32 billion in national settlements, California's $7 billion share, 58 county spending plans, and zero statewide accountability for outcomes reveal about the largest public health windfall since the tobacco settlement and the system California built to spend it

The national opioid litigation settlements, negotiated between state and local governments and pharmaceutical manufacturers, distributors, and retailers (Johnson and Johnson, McKesson, Cardinal Health, AmerisourceBergen, Walgreens, CVS, Walmart, Teva, Allergan, and others), will distribute approximately $32 billion to state and local governments over 18 years. California's share is approximately $7 billion, the largest allocation of any state, to be distributed to the state government, 58 counties, and hundreds of cities through a series of agreements that specify how the funds may be used.

The funds are restricted to opioid abatement: prevention, treatment, recovery, and harm reduction for opioid use disorder and the broader substance use crisis that the opioid epidemic catalyzed. Eligible uses include medication-assisted treatment (MAT) expansion, naloxone distribution, recovery housing, syringe service programs, workforce training for behavioral health providers, data infrastructure, and criminal justice diversion programs. The California Opioid Abatement Fund, administered by the California Department of Health Care Services, receives the state's direct share (approximately $2 billion over 18 years) and distributes it through competitive grants and formula allocations. The remaining approximately $5 billion flows directly to counties and cities.

## The Tobacco Settlement Precedent

The opioid settlements are frequently compared to the 1998 Master Settlement Agreement (MSA) with tobacco companies, which has distributed approximately $8 billion to California over 25 years. The tobacco precedent is cautionary. Studies by the Campaign for Tobacco-Free Kids and the Government Accountability Office found that states spent the vast majority of tobacco settlement funds on general government purposes rather than on tobacco prevention and cessation. California, which was more disciplined than most states, dedicated approximately 20 percent of its tobacco settlement revenue to tobacco control and public health. The remaining 80 percent was spent on budget stabilization, debt service, and general fund purposes.

The opioid settlement agreements were structured specifically to avoid the tobacco outcome. The allocation agreements restrict spending to enumerated opioid abatement strategies, require recipient jurisdictions to report on spending, and empower oversight entities to review compliance. California's Opioid Settlement Fund Act (AB 2019) requires counties and cities to spend settlement funds only on approved abatement activities and to report annually on expenditures and outcomes. The reporting requirements are more stringent than those imposed on tobacco settlement funds, but the enforcement mechanism is unclear: what happens to a county that spends settlement funds on non-abatement purposes is not well-defined.

## The 58-County Experiment

Each of California's 58 counties is developing its own opioid abatement plan, reflecting local priorities, existing infrastructure, and the scale of the local opioid crisis. The variation is significant. San Francisco, where the fentanyl crisis has produced visible street-level drug use and overdose deaths concentrated in the Tenderloin and South of Market neighborhoods, is investing heavily in supervised consumption sites, naloxone distribution, and medication-assisted treatment expansion. Rural counties in the north, where the opioid crisis manifests as prescription opioid and methamphetamine use among dispersed populations, are investing in telehealth treatment, mobile recovery services, and criminal justice diversion.

The per-capita allocation varies by county based on litigation participation and demographic factors. Large urban counties with active litigation (Los Angeles, San Francisco, San Diego) receive disproportionately large shares. Small rural counties receive smaller absolute amounts that may be insufficient to fund standalone programs. The annual per-capita allocation for some rural counties is less than $5 per resident, an amount that cannot fund a treatment slot, a recovery house bed, or a prevention program.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that the opioid settlement money is a windfall that will be absorbed into 58 county bureaucracies with minimal accountability for outcomes. The $7 billion sounds transformative, but distributed over 18 years across 58 counties and hundreds of cities, the annual per-capita spending is approximately $10 to $15 per Californian, a fraction of the estimated $30 billion to $50 billion in annual economic costs of the opioid and broader substance use crisis in the state. The settlement requires reporting on expenditures but does not require reporting on outcomes: whether the money reduced overdose deaths, increased treatment access, improved recovery rates, or prevented new cases of opioid use disorder. Without outcome measurement, the settlement will produce 58 county spending reports and zero evidence of whether the spending worked. The tobacco settlement demonstrated that large legal windfalls can be absorbed by government without producing measurable public health improvement. The opioid settlement has more restrictions. It does not have more accountability.

The generous read is that the opioid settlement provides the first dedicated, sustained funding for substance use treatment and prevention infrastructure in California's history. The behavioral health system that the settlement funds is building, treatment slots, recovery housing, harm reduction services, workforce training, will outlast the settlement itself and serve populations affected by substance use disorders beyond opioids. The reporting requirements and the opioid-abatement spending restrictions, while imperfect, ensure that the money is directed toward its intended purpose. And the alternative to the settlement, no accountability for the pharmaceutical companies that manufactured, distributed, and marketed the drugs that have killed over 100,000 Americans per year, is worse.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

DHCS and the California Opioid Settlement Fund publish expenditure reports showing how settlement funds are spent by category and jurisdiction. They do not publish outcome data linking settlement expenditures to measurable changes in opioid overdose deaths, treatment admissions, recovery outcomes, or naloxone distribution and reversal rates. The infrastructure for measuring whether the settlement money is producing better outcomes does not yet exist at the state level.
