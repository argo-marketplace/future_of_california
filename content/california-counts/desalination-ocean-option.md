# California Counts: The Ocean Option
## What 12 operating desalination plants, 50 million gallons per day of capacity, a $1 billion Carlsbad facility, and a rejected Huntington Beach proposal reveal about the water source California sits next to, cannot afford to ignore, and cannot build fast enough to matter

California operates approximately 12 seawater desalination facilities with a combined capacity of approximately 50 million gallons per day (approximately 56,000 acre-feet per year). The largest is the Claude "Bud" Lewis Carlsbad Desalination Plant in San Diego County, which produces approximately 50 million gallons per day (56,000 acre-feet per year), supplying approximately 10 percent of San Diego County's water demand. The Carlsbad plant, operated by Poseidon Water, cost approximately $1 billion to build and opened in 2015.

California's total urban water demand is approximately 8 million acre-feet per year. Desalination currently provides less than 1 percent of that total. The state's desalination capacity is dwarfed by its water recycling capacity (approximately 1.8 million acre-feet per year) and is a rounding error compared to its surface water and groundwater supplies. The 50 million gallons per day of desalinated seawater represents a technology that works but has not been deployed at a scale that alters California's water supply equation.

## The Huntington Beach Decision

In May 2022, the California Coastal Commission unanimously rejected Poseidon Water's application to build a 50 million gallon-per-day desalination plant in Huntington Beach, a project that had been in permitting for approximately 20 years. The commission cited concerns about marine life impacts (the intake system would kill billions of microscopic organisms), greenhouse gas emissions (desalination is energy-intensive, requiring approximately 15,000 kilowatt-hours per acre-foot), inconsistency with the Coastal Act's marine resource protections, and environmental justice impacts (the plant would be located adjacent to disadvantaged communities). The rejection was a landmark: the largest proposed desalination plant in the Western Hemisphere, supported by a billion-dollar developer and the local water agency, was denied by a regulatory body prioritizing marine ecology and environmental justice over water supply.

The energy cost of desalination is the persistent economic challenge. Reverse osmosis desalination requires approximately 10,000 to 15,000 kilowatt-hours per acre-foot, compared to approximately 2,000 to 4,000 kWh per acre-foot for water recycling and approximately 3,000 to 5,000 kWh per acre-foot for imported water (via the State Water Project). At current electricity rates, desalinated water costs approximately $2,000 to $2,500 per acre-foot, two to four times the cost of imported water or recycled water.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has spent 20 years debating desalination and has one large plant to show for it. The Carlsbad facility works, delivers reliable water, and has been particularly valuable during droughts when other supplies are curtailed. But the cost ($2,000 to $2,500 per acre-foot), the energy intensity (contributing to grid strain and carbon emissions), and the regulatory environment (the Coastal Commission's Huntington Beach rejection signals that future projects face similar scrutiny) mean that desalination will remain a marginal source. California's water future is being built on recycling (cheaper, less energy-intensive, regulatorily favored) and conservation (cheapest of all), not desalination. The ocean option exists but is too expensive, too slow to permit, and too energy-intensive to deploy at the scale that would materially change California's water supply.

The generous read is that desalination is the only water source that is functionally unlimited and drought-proof. Every other California water source (surface water, groundwater, imported water, recycled water) depends on precipitation. Desalination depends on the Pacific Ocean. In a future of increasing drought severity and duration, the reliability premium of desalination may justify its cost premium. Technology is improving: energy consumption per acre-foot has declined approximately 50 percent since 1990, and coupling desalination with renewable energy (solar-powered desalination) can address both the cost and the carbon concerns. Israel, Saudi Arabia, and Australia have built national-scale desalination systems. California has the technology, the coastline, and the need. It lacks only the political will.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The State Water Resources Control Board regulates desalination facility discharge but does not publish a statewide inventory of desalination capacity, production, cost, or energy consumption. The comparison of desalination costs to alternative water sources depends on assumptions (energy prices, capital costs, discount rates, environmental mitigation requirements) that vary by project and are not standardized across the agencies that evaluate them.
