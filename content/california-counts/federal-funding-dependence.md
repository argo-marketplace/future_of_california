# California Counts: The Federal Check
## What $150 billion in annual federal transfers, $90 billion for Medi-Cal alone, a 50 percent federal matching rate, and a 2025 congressional debate over $20 to $40 billion in potential cuts reveal about the state that governs progressively on money it does not control

California's total annual state budget is approximately $300 billion. Roughly half of that, approximately $150 billion, arrives not as tax revenue collected from Californians but as federal transfers from Washington: Medicaid matching funds, Title I education grants, transportation formula apportionments, nutrition assistance, housing vouchers, and dozens of other categorical and formula programs. California raises approximately $100 billion in state income, sales, and corporate taxes for the General Fund, and the state collects additional revenues through fees, special funds, and bonds. But the federal share is so large that any significant change in federal funding policy can produce a state fiscal crisis larger than any recession California has navigated in the modern era. California's budget is, in ways that its architects and defenders do not always acknowledge, a joint venture with the federal government. California is the managing partner. Washington is the major investor. And the terms of that investment can change without California's consent.

---

## The Scale: $150 Billion in Federal Money

The federal funding flowing into California's budget is not a supplement. It is a structural component. The $150 billion figure is approximate because the definition of "state budget" varies, and some federal funds flow to local governments, school districts, and nonprofits as pass-throughs that appear in the state budget as expenditure but not as state-raised revenue. On a consistent accounting basis, federal funds represent approximately 35 to 40 percent of total state expenditures and a much larger share of spending in specific program categories.

The largest categories, ranked by approximate magnitude, are Medi-Cal/Medicaid (approximately $90 billion in federal matching funds), federal education grants including Title I and IDEA (approximately $10 billion to $12 billion), CalFresh/SNAP (approximately $8 billion to $10 billion), transportation federal aid from the Highway Trust Fund and related programs (approximately $6 billion to $8 billion per year), housing assistance including Section 8 vouchers and the Low Income Housing Tax Credit (approximately $4 billion to $6 billion), and various smaller categorical grants across social services, environmental programs, and workforce development.

The $90 billion Medi-Cal figure deserves particular attention because of its size and because of the specific federal matching mechanism that determines it.

## Medi-Cal and the FMAP: The Biggest Check

Medi-Cal, California's Medicaid program, covered approximately 14.5 million Californians as of 2025, representing roughly 35 percent of the state's total population and about 90 percent of the Affordable Care Act's Medicaid expansion population in California. Total Medi-Cal spending, state and federal combined, is approximately $170 billion per year, making it far and away the largest single expenditure in the California budget.

The federal share is determined by the Federal Medical Assistance Percentage, or FMAP. California's base FMAP is approximately 50 percent, meaning the federal government reimburses the state for approximately 50 cents of every dollar of eligible Medi-Cal expenditure. On a $170 billion total program, a 50 percent FMAP implies approximately $85 billion in federal reimbursement. Additional enhanced FMAP rates for specific populations, including ACA expansion enrollees (who historically received a 90 percent federal match), and various temporary enhanced rates enacted during the COVID period, have pushed California's effective blended FMAP above the base 50 percent rate in recent years.

The temporary COVID-era enhanced FMAP, which added 6.2 percentage points to the standard federal match under the Families First Coronavirus Response Act and subsequent legislation, provided California with an estimated $8 billion to $12 billion in additional federal reimbursement per year during the period it was in effect. That enhancement expired in 2023, and the withdrawal of those funds required California to either increase state Medi-Cal spending or reduce program scope. The state chose to absorb the additional state cost rather than reduce eligibility, adding to the General Fund pressure that has driven California's recent budget deficits.

## The Congressional Threat: $20 to $40 Billion Per Year

In 2025, Congress debated significant modifications to Medicaid financing, including proposals to convert Medicaid from an open-ended entitlement with federal matching to a per capita cap or block grant model. The specific proposals varied, but the California Department of Finance estimated that the most aggressive versions of Medicaid per capita caps being discussed would reduce California's federal Medi-Cal funding by approximately $20 billion to $40 billion per year.

A $20 billion reduction in annual federal Medi-Cal funds would equal roughly 20 percent of the state's entire General Fund revenue. A $40 billion reduction would exceed the combined annual General Fund spending on K-12 education and higher education. California could not absorb cuts at this magnitude through other budget adjustments; it would be forced to reduce Medi-Cal eligibility, cut provider rates, or some combination of both. A reduction in eligibility that restored the pre-ACA expansion coverage levels would remove approximately 3 to 4 million Californians from Medi-Cal coverage.

The 2025 congressional debate did not produce enacted per capita caps, but it demonstrated the fiscal vulnerability California faces from the structure of its largest program. The state has no mechanism to guarantee its federal Medi-Cal matching rate in any future federal budget cycle. California's legislature can commit the state to universal healthcare coverage as a policy goal. It cannot commit Congress to matching that commitment with federal funds.

## The Dependency Map

The federal funding dependency is uneven across program categories. For Medi-Cal, California is at maximum exposure: the program is large, the match rate is high, and any federal cut flows directly into either a state budget deficit or a coverage reduction. For transportation, the dependency is also high but more predictable: federal highway aid flows through formula programs that are reauthorized on multi-year cycles and have bipartisan support, making them more stable than Medicaid.

For education, federal Title I and IDEA funding represent approximately 8 to 10 percent of total K-12 spending. A complete elimination of federal K-12 grants would be severe but survivable through state general fund substitution at a cost of approximately $10 billion to $12 billion per year. For nutrition assistance, CalFresh/SNAP is a federal entitlement with state administrative costs; a block grant conversion would shift risk to California in the same way a Medicaid per capita cap would. For housing, federal Section 8 vouchers support approximately 400,000 California households; their elimination would be devastating to those households but would not create a state budget crisis of Medicaid proportions because the state does not match federal housing vouchers with state funds.

The asymmetry matters: Medi-Cal is the dominant federal-dependency risk, and the 50 to 90 percent federal match rate means that state policy expansions in Medi-Cal are leveraged with federal dollars in a way that makes the program larger and the dependency deeper than any other state-federal program in California's budget.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has built a progressive policy architecture, including universal Medi-Cal coverage, expanded SNAP outreach, generous refugee assistance, and enhanced mental health services, on a federal funding foundation that the state does not control and that Congress has repeatedly proposed to modify or reduce. The state's General Fund is approximately $100 billion in annual revenues. The federal transfers are $150 billion. California's progressive ambitions are underwritten at a ratio of $1.50 in federal money for every $1.00 of state money, and the most ambitious of those programs, Medi-Cal and CalFresh, are most dependent on federal matching. When Congress debates Medicaid cuts, California is not at the negotiating table. Sacramento can lobby. It cannot veto. The state that portrays itself as the nation's progressive policy laboratory runs its most important experiments on borrowed federal money.

The generous read is that the federal matching structure was designed explicitly to encourage states to provide public goods at scale, and California using that structure fully is not recklessness, it is sophisticated fiscal management. A 50 percent Medi-Cal match means that every dollar California invests in healthcare for low-income residents generates an additional dollar of federal support, making the state's healthcare investment go twice as far as it would without the match. The concentration of federal dependency in Medi-Cal reflects a rational prioritization: healthcare for 14.5 million low-income Californians is the highest-value use of both state and federal public dollars, and leveraging the federal match to maximize coverage is what the program design is for. The federal dependency is a risk, but the ACA Medicaid expansion has now been in place for more than a decade and has survived multiple congressional repeal attempts. The probability of catastrophic federal Medicaid cuts is lower than the annual congressional debate makes it appear, and California's political influence in federal healthcare policy, given its size and the number of Medicaid beneficiaries at stake, is not negligible.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Governor's Budget and the Finance Bulletin publish federal fund receipts by program and department, but the public presentation does not consistently separate permanent formula entitlements (where federal funding is reliably tied to eligible expenditure) from discretionary grants (where Congress must annually appropriate funds) or from time-limited enhanced rates (where the federal match will revert at a future date). For fiscal planning purposes, these categories carry very different risk profiles. The California Department of Finance conducts internal risk analysis of federal funding scenarios, but detailed federal dependency stress tests have not been published in a form accessible to the public or the legislature in a consolidated way.
