# California Counts: The Tax Credit Apartment
## What $700,000 per unit, a 2-year approval process, 22 funding sources layered into a single project, and 10,000 units per year reveal about the affordable housing California builds at the world's highest cost

California produces approximately 10,000 to 15,000 deed-restricted affordable housing units per year through a financing system so complex that a single apartment can require 22 separate funding sources, a two-year approval process, and a per-unit development cost that averages $600,000 to $700,000 in the Bay Area and Los Angeles. The Terner Center for Housing Innovation at UC Berkeley has documented that the per-unit cost of affordable housing in California exceeds that of any comparable jurisdiction in the world, roughly 50 to 100 percent above the cost in other high-cost U.S. markets and two to three times the cost in European countries with robust social housing sectors.

The primary federal financing tool is the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC), a program that allocates tax credits to state housing agencies for distribution to affordable housing developers. The California Tax Credit Allocation Committee (TCAC) distributes approximately $200 million in 9 percent (competitive) tax credits and a larger volume of 4 percent (as-of-right) credits each year. Developers receive the credits, sell them to investors (typically banks seeking Community Reinvestment Act credit), and use the equity proceeds to finance construction. The tax credit investor receives a 10-year stream of federal tax credits in exchange for an equity investment that typically covers 30 to 50 percent of the project's capital cost. The remaining 50 to 70 percent comes from state and local sources: state tax credits, state bond funds (Proposition 1 of 2018, the Multifamily Housing Program), local affordable housing trust funds, community development block grants, HOME funds, project-based Section 8 vouchers, mental health housing funds, and other sources, each with its own application, timeline, compliance requirements, and audit obligations.

## The Cost Drivers

The Terner Center has identified the primary cost drivers for California affordable housing: land (10 to 20 percent of total cost in high-value urban areas), construction labor (approximately 40 percent, reflecting prevailing wage requirements, union labor agreements, and a labor shortage), materials (approximately 20 percent), soft costs (architecture, engineering, legal, environmental review, permitting, financing costs, approximately 15 to 20 percent), and developer fees (approximately 5 to 8 percent, capped by TCAC rules).

The prevailing wage requirement, which mandates that workers on publicly subsidized projects receive union-scale wages and benefits, adds approximately 15 to 30 percent to construction labor costs compared to non-prevailing-wage projects. The requirement is mandated by state law for any project receiving public subsidy, which includes virtually all affordable housing in California. The environmental review process (CEQA), while streamlined for many affordable housing projects through SB 35 and other legislation, adds 6 to 18 months and $50,000 to $500,000 in costs for projects that are not exempt. Local permitting requirements add further time and cost: the average time from project application to building permit issuance for an affordable housing project in California is approximately 2.5 years.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has built an affordable housing financing system so complex, so expensive, and so slow that it produces 10,000 units per year at $700,000 each in a state that needs over 100,000 affordable units per year. The $700,000 per-unit cost means that the RHNA allocation of 1.1 million affordable units would cost approximately $770 billion to build, roughly 2.5 times the state's total annual budget. At the current production rate, meeting the affordable housing need would take 73 to 110 years. The system is designed to minimize public spending per unit by layering multiple small funding sources, but the complexity of layering produces transaction costs, delays, and overhead that increase total cost per unit. The affordable housing financing system is a Rube Goldberg machine: it achieves its objective (building apartments for low-income people) through a process so circuitous that the cost of the process exceeds the cost of a simpler approach.

The generous read is that the 10,000 to 15,000 affordable units produced each year provide permanent, high-quality housing for approximately 30,000 to 45,000 low-income Californians who would otherwise be rent-burdened or homeless. The deed restrictions ensure that the units remain affordable for 55 years, creating a permanent affordable housing stock that market-rate development does not produce. The LIHTC program has financed more affordable housing nationally than any other program since the end of public housing construction. The cost is high, but the product, well-built, professionally managed, permanently affordable housing with on-site services for residents, is a fundamentally different product than the market-rate apartment that costs $450,000 to build. The comparison understates the quality and durability of the affordable housing product. The alternative to expensive affordable housing is not cheap affordable housing. The alternative is no affordable housing.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

TCAC publishes project-level data for tax credit projects, including cost, unit count, location, and target population. It does not publish a standardized cost-per-unit breakdown that would allow systematic comparison of cost drivers across projects, locations, and time periods. The Terner Center and the California Housing Partnership produce aggregate cost analyses from the TCAC data, but the project-level detail required for granular cost analysis is not uniformly reported.
