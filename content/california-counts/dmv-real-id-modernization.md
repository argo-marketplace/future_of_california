# California Counts: The DMV Line
## What 27 million licensed drivers, 55 million annual transactions, a $1.4 billion budget, a National Guard deployment to process paperwork, a $500 million IT modernization program, and 90 percent REAL ID compliance reveal about the agency that serves everyone and has been perpetually underfunded for the volume it handles

Every California resident who drives, registers a vehicle, or applies for a state identification card interacts with the Department of Motor Vehicles. The DMV is not a discretionary agency. There is no alternative provider, no competitive market, no option to skip the transaction and move on. For 27 million licensed drivers and the millions more who hold state ID cards, register commercial vehicles, or appear to resolve a license suspension, the DMV is a mandatory encounter with state government. The agency processes approximately 55 million transactions per year, making it one of the highest-volume service agencies in any state government in the nation. How California has funded and managed an agency of this scale is a case study in the consequences of chronic underinvestment in administrative infrastructure.

---

## The Volume and the Budget

The DMV's annual budget is approximately $1.4 billion, funded primarily through fees paid by drivers and vehicle registrants. The fee revenue model means that the DMV is largely self-funded: vehicle registration fees, driver's license fees, and transaction charges flow to the Motor Vehicle Account, which funds DMV operations. General Fund support is minimal. This funding structure has a consequence that shapes everything else about the agency: when transaction volume increases or when the agency must undertake new programs (such as REAL ID), the revenue does not automatically increase to match, because fee increases require legislation and create political friction.

Dividing the $1.4 billion budget by 55 million transactions produces an average cost per transaction of approximately $25.45. Some transactions are far cheaper than that (a registration renewal processed online costs very little); some are far more expensive (a complex driving test with a live examiner, or a hearing to resolve a license suspension, can cost several hundred dollars in staff time). The DMV has been pushing transactions toward lower-cost self-service channels, and online and kiosk transactions now account for a significant share of the total. But the complex, in-person transactions that require examiner time or complex verification have not moved online, and those are precisely the transactions that create wait times.

## REAL ID: The Paperwork Crisis

The REAL ID Act of 2005, passed by Congress in response to the September 11 Commission's recommendations, established federal standards for state-issued identification cards and driver's licenses. Airlines and federal facilities were required to accept only REAL ID-compliant identification for domestic air travel. California was one of the last large states to implement REAL ID, waiting until 2018 to begin issuing compliant licenses.

When California finally rolled out REAL ID in January 2018, the result was a demand surge the DMV was not prepared to handle. REAL ID required in-person document verification, specifically an original birth certificate or passport, a Social Security card, and two proofs of California residence. Millions of California drivers who had renewed their licenses remotely for years had to appear in person for the first time in a decade. DMV field offices, which had been staffed for a transaction mix that did not include mass in-person REAL ID upgrades, were overwhelmed. Wait times at some offices reached three to four hours. Appointment availability stretched weeks into the future.

By mid-2018, the situation had become politically untenable. Governor Brown directed the California National Guard to deploy personnel to DMV field offices to assist with document handling and queue management. The Guard deployment, approximately 400 personnel at peak, was a dramatic demonstration of an agency unable to handle a predictable demand surge with its existing workforce. The National Guard does not normally process driver's license paperwork. That it did so for several months in 2018 captured, in a single image, the gap between the DMV's authorized capacity and its actual workload.

By 2025, approximately 90 percent of California licensed drivers held REAL ID-compliant licenses. The transition is effectively complete. Whether the disruption of 2018 was a one-time implementation failure or a symptom of structural underinvestment is the question that the DMV's subsequent history illuminates.

## IT Modernization: $500 Million and Counting

The DMV's legacy technology systems date in significant part to the 1980s and 1990s. The core driver license and vehicle registration systems are built on programming languages and database architectures that are no longer taught in universities and are maintained by a shrinking population of programmers with legacy system expertise. The DMV has been aware of this vulnerability for decades, and modernization has been on the agency's to-do list for nearly as long.

A major IT modernization initiative has been underway since approximately 2019, with a projected cost of over $500 million. The initiative includes a new driver license system, a vehicle registration modernization, and the expansion of the Virtual Field Office, the DMV's online service platform. The Virtual Field Office has been one of the program's genuine successes: by 2024, California drivers could complete approximately 23 DMV transactions entirely online, including registration renewal, address changes, and license renewal for eligible drivers. The share of transactions completed without visiting a field office has increased meaningfully since 2019.

The modernization has proceeded more slowly and at higher cost than originally projected, a pattern that the previous California Counts installment on state IT failures would recognize. The Department of Technology, which provides oversight for major state IT projects, has flagged the DMV modernization as a project requiring close monitoring. Total spending through fiscal year 2024-25 has exceeded $400 million, with portions of the modernization still incomplete. The core legacy systems have not yet been fully replaced; they have been modernized at the edges while the central infrastructure remains on its 1990s foundation.

## Field Office Operations

The DMV operates 172 field offices across California, with locations in every county and dense coverage in metropolitan areas. Field office staffing is the agency's largest operating cost, and the DMV has been working to reduce field office demand by shifting eligible transactions online. The DMV's appointment system, which was overhauled after the 2018 crisis, now offers online appointment scheduling for most in-person transactions.

Wait times have improved substantially from their 2018 peak. The DMV's published wait time data for 2024 showed average in-office wait times of approximately 30 to 45 minutes for appointments and 60 to 90 minutes for walk-in service, compared to the three-to-four-hour waits of 2018. The improvement reflects both the completion of the REAL ID surge and operational changes: extended hours at some offices, more appointment slots, and improved queue management technology.

The agency's workforce of approximately 8,000 employees handles the remaining in-person transaction volume. Correctional officers aside, DMV employees are among the state employees who interact most directly with the public, and the quality of that interaction is one of the few service metrics that is regularly and informally evaluated by Californians who form opinions about state government from their own experiences. The DMV's reputation, which has long served as a cultural shorthand for bureaucratic inefficiency, is partly unearned and partly deserved. The 2018 National Guard deployment earned it. The Virtual Field Office expansion is slowly challenging it.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has chronically underfunded its highest-volume service agency relative to the transactions it must process. The REAL ID crisis of 2018 was not a surprise: California knew the federal deadline was coming, knew that millions of drivers would need to appear in person, and did not staff or prepare adequately. The solution was to call the National Guard, which is a governing failure of a particular kind, the kind where you solve an administrative problem with a military resource because you did not make ordinary administrative investments. The $500 million IT modernization has proceeded slowly and at cost overruns consistent with California's pattern of IT project challenges, producing incremental improvements on a system that still runs on 1980s infrastructure at its core. A $1.4 billion budget for an agency serving 27 million licensed drivers, processing 55 million transactions per year, works out to approximately $25 per transaction, which is not obviously lavish and may explain why the agency chronically struggles to handle demand surges without the kind of visible public failure that earns DMV its reputation.

The generous read is that the DMV is, in most years and for most transactions, a high-volume service agency that processes 55 million transactions with a relatively small staff and a budget that is primarily fee-funded rather than general-fund-dependent. The 2018 crisis was a genuine one-time surge driven by a decade of pent-up REAL ID demand; the National Guard deployment bought time while the agency hired and trained additional staff. By 2025, REAL ID compliance is at 90 percent, online transactions have expanded to 23 types, and field office wait times have improved substantially. The IT modernization, while slow and expensive, is producing real changes in the public-facing service model. And the 27 million California drivers who renewed their registrations online last year did not stand in any line.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The DMV publishes transaction volume data, budget information, and some wait time metrics, but the agency does not publish comprehensive data on transaction completion rates (how often a customer who starts a transaction completes it successfully versus returning for a second visit), error rates in license and registration records, or the share of field office visits that could have been completed online but were not. The IT modernization project's status is reported to the Department of Technology in project status reports that are not fully public-facing in a form that allows easy external tracking. The Virtual Field Office expansion is described in press releases, but a systematic audit of which transactions have moved online and which remain in-person has not been published in recent budget cycles.
