# California Counts: The Participation Gap
## What 5.4 million enrolled, 7.5 million eligible, and a participation rate below the national average reveal about the food assistance program California hasn't fully reached

California runs one of the most generous food assistance programs in the country. CalFresh, California's version of the federal Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, provides food benefits to 5.4 million residents as of 2025, the largest SNAP caseload of any state in the nation. The average monthly benefit is approximately $200 per person. The maximum monthly benefit for a family of four is approximately $975. And yet, by the USDA's own estimate, approximately 7.5 to 8 million Californians are eligible for CalFresh and do not receive it. California's participation rate, the share of eligible residents who are enrolled, runs approximately 68 to 72 percent, below the national average of roughly 80 percent. The state with the largest CalFresh caseload in the country is also the state that reaches the smallest share of its eligible population relative to the national norm.

---

## Who Is Eligible and Who Is Enrolled

SNAP eligibility in California, as in all states, is determined primarily by income: households with gross income at or below 130 percent of the federal poverty line are categorically eligible, with some exceptions and categorical eligibilities for households receiving other public benefits. For a family of four in 2025, 130 percent of the federal poverty line is approximately $40,560 per year. California has also expanded categorical eligibility for households with gross incomes up to 200 percent of the federal poverty line through a state option that simplifies enrollment for working families.

The 5.4 million enrolled Californians represent approximately $13 billion in annual CalFresh benefits issued, funded primarily by the federal government with state administrative matching funds. California's share of administrative costs runs approximately $800 million to $1 billion per year from the state general fund, funding county eligibility workers, outreach contractors, and systems maintenance.

The gap between 5.4 million enrolled and 7.5 to 8 million eligible is the participation gap: approximately 2 to 2.5 million Californians who qualify for CalFresh benefits and do not receive them. At an average benefit of approximately $200 per person per month, those 2 to 2.5 million unenrolled eligible individuals represent approximately $400 to $500 million per month, or $4.8 to $6 billion per year, in food assistance they are entitled to but are not receiving.

The USDA publishes state participation rates annually, derived from the Current Population Survey and administrative enrollment data. California's participation rate has ranged from approximately 68 to 72 percent over the past decade, consistently below the national average. States with higher participation rates include Missouri, Oregon, and West Virginia, which achieve 85 to 90 percent participation through a combination of simplified enrollment processes, aggressive outreach, and benefit structures that make CalFresh more attractive to eligible households at the margin of participation.

---

## Why Eligible Californians Don't Enroll

The barriers to CalFresh enrollment are well-documented. They fall into several categories, and the research suggests no single barrier dominates; rather, it is the combination that suppresses participation.

The application process is the first barrier. A CalFresh application in California requires documentation of income, identity, and residency. The application can be submitted online through BenefitsCal, in person at a county social services office, or by mail. An initial interview is required. For a working parent with irregular hours, two or three children, limited English proficiency, and a job that does not permit daytime phone calls, the application process is a significant obstacle even when the benefit itself is clearly worth obtaining.

Language access is a related barrier. California requires CalFresh materials in multiple languages, but the depth of language access varies by county, by document type, and by the specific language. A Punjabi-speaking farmworker in Yuba County or a Khmer-speaking household in Long Beach may find that the application is available in their language but that the interview, the renewal notice, or the error resolution process is not.

Mixed-status households present a specific enrollment challenge that is distinct from other barriers. Federal law prohibits SNAP benefits for undocumented immigrants, but US-citizen children of undocumented parents are eligible. Enrolling the eligible children in a household with an undocumented parent requires the family to submit information about all household members to a county office in a political environment where immigration enforcement is salient. Research on immigrant benefit program participation consistently finds that enrollment declines during periods of heightened immigration enforcement, regardless of the eligibility status of the specific household members applying.

Stigma remains a documented factor, particularly among elderly recipients and among households that have not previously received public benefits. Some eligible households perceive CalFresh as a program for people in more desperate circumstances than themselves, or fear that enrollment will affect immigration status (a concern that has fluctuated in relevance depending on federal "public charge" rule interpretations).

Renewal requirements contribute to churn: enrolled households that successfully navigate initial enrollment must recertify their eligibility at intervals of six to twelve months depending on household composition. Each renewal is an opportunity for administrative attrition, as households that are still eligible fail to complete the renewal paperwork and lose benefits until re-enrolling.

## What California Has Done About It

California has invested in CalFresh outreach and enrollment simplification through several mechanisms.

AB 1296 and related legislation appropriated funds for CalFresh outreach contractors: nonprofit organizations, community health centers, food banks, and other community-based entities that help eligible individuals apply. The California Department of Social Services oversees these contracts, which distribute outreach funding to entities with trusted relationships in the communities most likely to contain unenrolled eligible households.

BenefitsCal, California's unified online portal for CalFresh, Medi-Cal, and CalWORKs applications, launched in 2022 to replace a fragmented set of county-operated systems. The portal reduced the friction of applying online and enabled pre-population of eligibility information from other state data systems for households already enrolled in related programs. Early data on BenefitsCal's effect on enrollment rates is positive but limited.

California has also implemented simplified reporting requirements for some CalFresh households, reducing the frequency of required income reporting for households with stable incomes. This reduces administrative burden on both households and county eligibility workers.

The Golden State Stimulus and CalEITC expansions in 2021 and 2022 provided one-time payments and ongoing tax credits to low-income working Californians, many of them CalFresh-eligible. These programs did not directly enroll people in CalFresh but reached some of the same population through the tax filing system, which is a parallel enrollment channel.

## The Student Gap

One specific subgroup within the CalFresh participation gap is college students. Federal SNAP rules historically excluded most college students enrolled at least half-time, with limited exceptions. The COVID-19 relief legislation of 2020 and 2021 temporarily expanded student SNAP eligibility substantially. California implemented the temporary expansion and, through state legislation, has sought to maintain expanded student eligibility beyond the federal temporary period.

Approximately 2.7 million students attend California's public colleges and universities. Research has consistently found food insecurity rates of 30 to 45 percent among community college students in California, with rates above 20 percent at CSU and UC campuses. Many of these food-insecure students are technically SNAP-eligible but do not know it, have not applied, or face the same documentation and process barriers as other low-income households plus the additional complication of navigating enrollment while managing academic schedules.

The University of California and California State University systems have each established campus basic needs programs that include CalFresh enrollment assistance. Community colleges have been slower to build this infrastructure, despite serving the highest-food-insecurity population. The gap between food-insecure students and enrolled CalFresh students represents a significant slice of California's overall participation gap.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has the largest CalFresh caseload in the country and ranks below the national average on participation rate among eligible residents, which is a combination that should attract more policy attention than it does. The 2 to 2.5 million unenrolled eligible Californians are not a rounding error. They are a population larger than the entire population of 14 individual states, entitled to food assistance they are not receiving, in a state that has declared food security a priority and invested in outreach programs that demonstrably have not closed the gap. The mixed-status household chilling effect is real and documented. The application complexity is real. The language access gaps are real. California has addressed each of these barriers incrementally rather than comprehensively, with outreach funding that supplements but does not replace a county-administered eligibility system that was designed for a different era of documentation and in-person service.

The generous read is that California's 5.4 million CalFresh enrollees represent a genuine achievement: the state serves more people through food assistance than any other state, invests in outreach above the national norm, has implemented technological improvements to reduce enrollment friction, and has extended eligibility through state options (like the 200 percent poverty threshold categorical eligibility) that most other states have not adopted. The participation gap is a national problem, not a California-specific failure: no state achieves 100 percent participation among its eligible population, because enrollment requires action by the household, and some households choose not to participate for reasons that include privacy preferences, cultural norms, and assessments of the benefit relative to the enrollment cost that the state cannot simply override. The 68 to 72 percent participation rate is below the national average, but the national average of 80 percent is itself far from complete. And California's eligible population is disproportionately composed of mixed-status households, non-English-speaking households, and other groups where participation barriers are structurally higher than in states with smaller immigrant populations, which partly explains the rate gap without excusing it.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The USDA participation rate estimates are derived from survey data with substantial confidence intervals, particularly at the state level. The 68 to 72 percent range for California's participation rate is an estimate, not a count. The true number of eligible unenrolled Californians is uncertain within a range of several hundred thousand in either direction.

The data does not show why specific individuals who are eligible do not enroll, beyond the research on barriers conducted through surveys and focus groups. Administrative data shows who is enrolled; it does not show the decision-making of those who are not. Enrollment barrier research is conducted periodically by CDSS, by researchers, and by advocacy organizations, but it is not routinely collected as part of the CalFresh administrative data system.

The long-term effects of food insecurity on the unenrolled eligible population, on health outcomes, on children's educational performance, and on adult economic productivity, are documented in academic literature but are not tracked through the CalFresh data system itself. The cost to California of approximately 2 to 2.5 million eligible residents not receiving food assistance they are entitled to is not zero; it is manifested in emergency room visits, developmental outcomes, and economic productivity that the program was designed to protect. That cost is real but invisible in the participation rate.

---

California's CalFresh program covers 5.4 million people. It should cover approximately 7.5 to 8 million. The 2 to 2.5 million people in between are not hiding. They are working in the fields, attending community colleges, and raising citizen children in mixed-status households. They have not enrolled because the process is hard, because they are afraid, because they don't know they qualify, or because the renewal system let them fall off. California has the tools to find most of them, the outreach contractors, the online portal, the co-enrollment processes, the expanded eligibility rules. What it has not done is close the gap. The national average is 80 percent. California is at 68 to 72. That is not close enough for a state that declared food a human right and built the country's largest food assistance program to serve it.
