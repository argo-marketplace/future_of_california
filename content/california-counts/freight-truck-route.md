# California Counts: The Truck Route
## What $900 billion in goods, 1.2 million truck trips per day, a zero-emission truck mandate by 2045, and the most polluted freight corridors in the nation reveal about the supply chain California runs through its poorest neighborhoods

California moves approximately $900 billion in goods per year through a freight network that includes the nation's two largest container ports (Los Angeles and Long Beach), four major railroads, 14 commercial airports, and approximately 400,000 registered commercial trucks. The goods movement industry employs approximately 600,000 Californians directly and supports an additional 1 million jobs in warehousing, distribution, and related services. The freight network connects California's ports to the national economy: approximately 40 percent of all containerized imports entering the United States pass through California.

The freight system is also the state's largest stationary and mobile source of air pollution. Diesel trucks, which perform the "drayage" function of moving containers from ports to warehouses and the "line haul" function of moving goods across the state and nation, produce approximately 25 percent of the state's total nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions and a significant share of diesel particulate matter (PM2.5). The emissions are concentrated along freight corridors: the I-710 freeway connecting the ports of LA and Long Beach to the inland distribution network, the I-15 corridor through the Inland Empire, and the I-5/SR-99 corridor through the Central Valley. The communities along these corridors, predominantly low-income and Latino, have the highest diesel particulate exposure and among the highest asthma hospitalization rates in the state.

## The Warehouse Explosion

The Inland Empire (San Bernardino and Riverside counties) has become the warehousing capital of the United States. The region has added approximately 200 million to 250 million square feet of warehouse and distribution space since 2010, an area equivalent to roughly 4,000 football fields. Amazon, Walmart, FedEx, UPS, and third-party logistics companies operate massive distribution centers that receive containers from the ports, sort goods, and dispatch them across the country. The warehouse boom has transformed the region's economy, providing employment (approximately 150,000 warehouse and distribution jobs in the Inland Empire) but also generating truck traffic, diesel emissions, and noise in residential neighborhoods that were not designed for industrial-scale goods movement.

AB 2840 (2022) imposed new setback requirements for warehouse development near sensitive receptors (schools, homes, hospitals), and multiple Inland Empire cities have adopted local warehouse moratoria or environmental review requirements. The regulatory response reflects the tension between economic development (the warehouse industry provides jobs and tax revenue in a region with limited alternative employment) and environmental justice (the diesel emissions fall on communities that are already among the most pollution-burdened in the state).

## The Zero-Emission Mandate

The Advanced Clean Fleets regulation, adopted by CARB in 2023, requires that all drayage trucks entering California ports and railyards be zero-emission by 2035 and that all medium- and heavy-duty trucks sold in California be zero-emission by 2036 (for certain categories) to 2042 (for others). The regulation is the most aggressive zero-emission truck mandate in the world. Full fleet transition is expected by 2045.

The mandate faces significant practical challenges. A zero-emission Class 8 tractor (the type that hauls containers) currently costs approximately $400,000 to $500,000, roughly three times the cost of a diesel equivalent. The charging infrastructure required for a fleet of 400,000 commercial trucks does not exist: a single large trucking depot may require 5 to 10 megawatts of electrical capacity, equivalent to a small town. The electrical grid upgrades required to support commercial truck charging statewide have been estimated at $5 billion to $10 billion. And the range limitations of current battery-electric trucks (approximately 150 to 250 miles per charge for heavy loads) constrain the routes on which they can operate without mid-route charging stops.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has built its goods movement network through the cheapest, most polluting corridors available, which happen to run through communities of color that lack the political power to redirect them. The I-710, the most polluted freight corridor in the nation, runs through communities that are 90 percent Latino and have childhood asthma rates double the state average. The warehouse explosion in the Inland Empire provides jobs at $18 to $22 per hour while imposing diesel emissions, truck traffic, and noise on neighborhoods where residents have no say in the location of facilities that affect their health. The zero-emission mandate is the right policy, but the 2045 timeline means that communities along freight corridors will breathe diesel exhaust for another 20 years while the transition unfolds.

The generous read is that CARB's zero-emission truck mandate is the most consequential freight decarbonization policy in the world, and California is the only jurisdiction with both the regulatory authority and the market scale to force the global trucking industry to electrify. The I-710 Clean Truck Program has already reduced drayage truck emissions by approximately 90 percent since 2008 through progressively stricter emissions standards. The zero-emission mandate will eliminate the remaining emissions entirely. The warehouse industry provides 150,000 jobs in the Inland Empire at wages above the regional median, in a region with limited alternative employment for workers without college degrees. The environmental justice concerns are real and are being addressed through AB 617 community emissions reduction programs, AB 2840 setback requirements, and the accelerated deployment of zero-emission trucks in the most affected corridors.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CARB publishes truck emissions data by region and vehicle class. It does not publish a corridor-level analysis linking specific freight routes to specific health outcomes in adjacent communities. The health impact of diesel emissions along freight corridors is estimated from emissions modeling and epidemiological studies, not from direct measurement of individual exposure and health outcomes.
