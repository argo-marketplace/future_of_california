# California Counts: The Water That Doesn't Exist
## What 250 million acre-feet of paper rights, 21 critically overdrafted basins, and an 80-20 split reveal about California's most consequential ledger

California has allocated approximately five times more water than physically exists. The State Water Resources Control Board's database of water rights contains claims totaling roughly 250 million acre-feet per year against an average annual supply of approximately 75 million acre-feet in a wet year and far less in a dry one. Every acre-foot on paper represents a legal right to divert water from a river, a stream, or an underground basin. Most of those rights cannot all be exercised simultaneously because the water they claim is the same water, counted multiple times through a system of overlapping appropriations that dates to the Gold Rush. The gap between paper water and wet water is the founding fiction of California's economy. It built the most productive agricultural system in human history. It also created a ledger that cannot balance, a problem the state has deferred for 175 years and is now, under the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act, being forced to reconcile for the first time.

---

## The Rights System: First in Time, First in Right

California operates two water rights systems simultaneously. Riparian rights attach to land bordering a waterway: if your property touches a river, you can use a reasonable share of its flow. These rights have no permit and no expiration. They cannot be separated from the land. They exist by operation of common law inherited from England.

Appropriative rights are the California invention. Beginning in the Gold Rush, miners diverted water from streams to hydraulic mining operations miles away. The doctrine that emerged was "first in time, first in right": the earliest diverter has the most senior claim. In a shortage, junior rights are cut before senior rights, regardless of the use. A miner's claim from 1852 outranks a city's claim from 1952.

Rights filed before 1914, when the state first required permits, are called pre-1914 appropriative rights. They are the most valuable water assets in California because they predate state regulatory authority and carry the oldest priority dates. The State Water Resources Control Board administers post-1914 permits and licenses but has limited jurisdiction over pre-1914 rights, which are self-reported and largely self-enforced. The board's database contains roughly 35,000 active water rights of all types. But the largest and oldest rights, the ones that matter most in a drought, are the ones the state knows least about.

In 2015, during the last major drought, the board attempted for the first time to curtail water diversions based on priority. Senior rights holders sued. The legal battles over who has the right to what water, and how much, are still being resolved. The fundamental problem is that California never inventoried its water. It issued rights against an assumed supply that was generous, did not account for climate variability, and did not limit total appropriations to total availability. The result is a system where the rights on paper exceed the water in the rivers by a factor of five, a ratio that functions only because most rights are not exercised in most years. In a drought, the fiction collapses.

## The 80-20 Split: Agriculture and the Water Budget

California's total developed water supply averages approximately 75 to 80 million acre-feet per year, depending on precipitation. Of that, approximately 50 percent is dedicated to environmental flows: water required by law or regulation to remain in rivers and wetlands for fish, wildlife, and habitat. The remaining human-use water, roughly 35 to 40 million acre-feet, divides approximately 80-20 between agriculture and urban use. Agriculture consumes roughly 29 to 34 million acre-feet. Cities and industry consume 7 to 9 million acre-feet for 40 million residents.

The 80-20 split is the most politically charged number in California. Environmentalists and urban advocates point out that agriculture generates approximately $59 billion in gross revenue, roughly 2.5 percent of California's $3.6 trillion GDP, while consuming 80 percent of developed water. Agricultural interests counter that California leads the nation in farm output, produces more than a third of the country's vegetables and three-quarters of its fruits and nuts, and that the raw GDP comparison ignores the multiplier effects, food security value, and rural economic dependence on irrigation.

The water intensity of California's crops varies enormously. Alfalfa, used primarily as livestock feed, consumes approximately 5.2 million acre-feet per year, more than any other single crop, across roughly 15 percent of the state's irrigated acreage. Almonds consume approximately 3.8 million acre-feet across 1.3 million bearing acres. Rice uses approximately 2.5 million acre-feet in the Sacramento Valley, where fields are intentionally flooded. Pistachios, grapes, and cotton each consume one to two million acre-feet.

The economic return per acre-foot varies by orders of magnitude. A study by the Pacific Institute found that some crops generate less than $100 per acre-foot of water consumed, while others generate more than $2,000. Alfalfa, the largest single water consumer, generates among the lowest returns per acre-foot. Almonds, despite their high total consumption, generate among the highest. The water market increasingly reflects this disparity. Farmers growing low-value crops on senior water rights can sell their water to cities or higher-value agriculture for $500 to $2,000 per acre-foot, often earning more from the water sale than from the crop.

## SGMA: The Groundwater Reckoning

For 162 years after statehood, California did not regulate groundwater. While surface water required permits, anyone who owned land above a groundwater basin could pump as much as they wanted. The result was predictable. The San Joaquin Valley sank by as much as 28 feet in some areas as groundwater was extracted faster than it was replenished, a phenomenon called subsidence that permanently compressed the underground aquifers, reducing their capacity to store water in the future. The Central Valley's subsidence damaged canals, roads, bridges, and pipelines, including the California Aqueduct itself.

The Sustainable Groundwater Management Act, signed by Governor Brown in 2014, required the formation of Groundwater Sustainability Agencies for all medium- and high-priority groundwater basins. California has 515 alluvial groundwater basins. Of those, 21 are designated as critically overdrafted, meaning they are being pumped at rates that significantly exceed natural and artificial recharge. Another 103 are classified as medium- or high-priority. SGMA required GSAs to develop Groundwater Sustainability Plans that bring basins into balance by 2040 for critically overdrafted basins and 2042 for all others.

The math of bringing basins into balance is stark. The Department of Water Resources and the Public Policy Institute of California have estimated that achieving groundwater sustainability will require fallowing between 500,000 and one million acres of irrigated farmland in the San Joaquin Valley alone. That represents 10 to 20 percent of the valley's irrigated acreage. The economic impact on farming communities, many of which are already among the poorest in California, could be severe: reduced employment, lower property values, shrinking tax bases, and the social dislocation that follows when an agricultural economy contracts.

More than 260 GSAs have been formed. The critically overdrafted basins submitted their sustainability plans in 2020 and 2022, with DWR reviewing them for adequacy. Several plans were found to be inadequate and sent back for revision. The state has intervention authority if a GSA fails to achieve sustainability, but the political and practical barriers to state intervention in local groundwater management are high. SGMA is the most significant water reform in California since the creation of the State Water Project in the 1960s. Whether it will be implemented fully, or whether the compliance deadlines will slip as the economic costs become visible, is the open question.

## The Colorado River: 4.4 Million Acre-Feet and Counting Down

California holds the largest single allocation on the Colorado River: 4.4 million acre-feet per year under the 1922 Colorado River Compact and the 1963 Supreme Court decision in Arizona v. California. The Imperial Irrigation District, in the southeastern desert, holds the single largest water right on the river: approximately 2.6 million acre-feet per year, enough to serve a city of 10 million people. IID uses it to irrigate 500,000 acres of farmland in a desert that receives three inches of rain per year.

The Colorado River supplies water to 40 million people across seven states and Mexico. It has been over-allocated since the 1922 Compact, which divided the river based on flow measurements from an abnormally wet period. Average annual flows are approximately 12 to 13 million acre-feet, roughly 20 percent less than the 16.4 million acre-feet the compact assumed. Climate change is reducing flows further. Studies project a 10 to 30 percent decline in Colorado River flows by 2050.

Lake Mead, the nation's largest reservoir, fell to its lowest level on record in 2022, dropping to 1,040 feet above sea level, roughly 25 percent of capacity. Lake Powell, the second-largest, approached minimum power pool elevation, threatening hydroelectric generation at Glen Canyon Dam. The Bureau of Reclamation declared the first-ever Tier 1 shortage on the Colorado in 2021 and Tier 2 in 2022, triggering mandatory cutbacks for Arizona and Nevada but not for California, whose senior priority status protects it from the first rounds of cuts.

California's protection is not permanent. The current operating guidelines for the Colorado River expire in 2026, and negotiations for a new framework are underway. The other basin states, particularly Arizona, have argued that California's 4.4 million acre-feet allocation must be renegotiated. California's position is that the Law of the River protects its allocation. The negotiating leverage, however, has shifted: Arizona and Nevada have made voluntary conservation agreements, while California's consumption has remained near its full entitlement. IID's 2.6 million acre-feet, used to grow alfalfa, bermuda grass, and vegetables in a desert, is the allocation that every other water user in the West wants to repurpose.

## The State Water Project: 700 Miles of Ambition

The State Water Project, approved by voters in 1960, is the largest state-built water and power delivery system in the United States. It spans 700 miles from Lake Oroville in the northern Sierra to Lake Perris in Riverside County, lifting water over the Tehachapi Mountains at a pumping cost that makes it the single largest consumer of electricity in California. The SWP delivers water to 29 contracting agencies that serve 27 million Californians and 750,000 acres of farmland.

The SWP's contracted delivery capacity is 4.2 million acre-feet per year. It has never delivered that much. In an average year, it delivers roughly 2.5 to 3 million acre-feet. In drought years, allocations can fall to 5 or 10 percent of contracted amounts. In 2014, the initial allocation was zero percent before being revised upward to 5 percent. The gap between contracted capacity and actual delivery is another manifestation of the paper water problem: the water exists on paper but not in the rivers in the years when it is most needed.

The Central Valley Project, the federal counterpart operated by the Bureau of Reclamation, delivers approximately 5 to 7 million acre-feet per year through 500 miles of canals to farms and cities in the Central Valley and the Bay Area. Together, the SWP and CVP constitute the engineered plumbing system that moves water from where it falls (the north) to where it is used (the south and the Central Valley), at a combined capital cost measured in the tens of billions and an annual operating cost measured in billions more.

The Delta conveyance project, a proposed tunnel beneath the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta, would create a new intake for the SWP to reduce the vulnerability of water exports to Delta flooding, earthquake risk, and sea level rise. The project's estimated cost is $20 to $30 billion. Environmental groups oppose it as a Northern California water grab. Southern California water agencies support it as essential infrastructure. The project has been in planning, environmental review, and political debate in various forms since the 1980s. Governor Newsom has advanced the current single-tunnel proposal. Whether it will be built depends on whether the state can secure financing, survive litigation, and maintain political support across multiple gubernatorial administrations.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California built its economy on a water accounting fiction that is now unwinding. The state issued rights to five times more water than exists, pumped its aquifers into subsidence, grew the most water-intensive crops in its driest regions, and deferred the reckoning until SGMA forced it. The 500,000 to one million acres that must be fallowed to achieve groundwater sustainability are not abstractions. They are farms, communities, and livelihoods in the San Joaquin Valley, the poorest region of the state, communities that are already food deserts and unemployment zones. The 80-20 agricultural split persists not because the market values the water that way but because senior rights, federal project contracts, and political power protect the allocation. The Imperial Irrigation District uses 2.6 million acre-feet to farm a desert while cities ration. The Colorado River Compact is based on flow measurements from a century ago that overstated the river's capacity. The entire system was designed for a climate that no longer exists and a population one-quarter the size of today's.

The generous read is that California's water system, however imperfect its accounting, feeds the nation and sustains the world's fifth-largest economy. The state produces over a third of America's vegetables and three-quarters of its fruits and nuts. That productivity exists because water rights, however overallocated on paper, created the legal certainty that allowed farmers to invest in irrigation, orchards, and permanent crops. SGMA, for all its pain, is the first honest accounting of groundwater in state history, and it was enacted voluntarily, not under federal compulsion. The water market is beginning to reallocate water from low-value to high-value uses, a transition that market economists have advocated for decades. The Sustainable Groundwater Management Act, the Colorado River negotiations, and the Delta conveyance project are all evidence that the state is confronting the fiction, slowly, imperfectly, but with more honesty about the numbers than at any point in its history.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The State Water Resources Control Board's water rights database is incomplete. Pre-1914 rights are self-reported and unverified. Riparian rights are not registered at all. The total claimed water rights of 250 million acre-feet is an estimate assembled by researchers, not an official state figure, because no official state figure exists. California does not have a comprehensive water budget that accounts for all rights, all diversions, all return flows, and all consumption in a single ledger. Individual agencies, districts, and basins track their own water, but no statewide system integrates the data. The state knows less about its water supply than it knows about its fiscal expenditures, which is a remarkable statement given the limitations of Open FI$Cal.

The data also does not capture the human cost of the SGMA transition. The communities most affected by land fallowing, Tulare, Kings, Kern, and Fresno counties, are majority-Latino, low-income, and already disadvantaged by air pollution, water contamination, and limited public services. The Drinking Water Needs Assessment found that more than 370 water systems serving more than one million people fail to meet safe drinking water standards. The communities that will lose farmland to groundwater sustainability are often the same communities that cannot drink their tap water. SGMA does not cause these conditions. But it will compound them.

And the data says nothing about what California's water would be worth if it were priced like a commodity rather than allocated like a political right. The price of water in voluntary transfers, $500 to $2,000 per acre-foot, is a fraction of what it would fetch in an open market if all uses competed equally. The entire allocation system rests on the principle that water rights are property rights, not market commodities, and that historical priority determines access. That principle has distributed water in California for 175 years. Whether it can continue to do so as the climate reduces supply and SGMA reduces pumping is the question that no dataset can answer, because it is not a question about water. It is a question about who gets to decide.

---

California's water system is a masterpiece of engineering and a catastrophe of accounting. The aqueducts work. The dams hold. The pumps lift water over mountains. The farms produce. The cities drink. And the ledger that makes it all possible adds up to five times more water than the state actually has. For a century and a half, the fiction held because the surplus between what was claimed and what was used was large enough to absorb the contradictions. That surplus is gone. The aquifers are sinking. The snowpack is declining. The Colorado River is shrinking. And for the first time, under SGMA, the state is asking the question it has spent 175 years avoiding: if there is not enough water for everyone who has a right to it, who loses theirs?
