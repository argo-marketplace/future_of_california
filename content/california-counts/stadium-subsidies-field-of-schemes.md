# California Counts: The Field of Schemes
## What $10 billion in stadium construction since 2010, zero public subsidies for the NFL's two newest stadiums, the A's departure to Las Vegas, and the economic impact studies that consistently overstate returns reveal about the sports facility investments California's cities once funded and have mostly stopped making

California hosts 18 major professional sports franchises across five metropolitan areas, playing in venues that range from publicly financed (Oracle Arena, built with public bonds in 1966; Dodger Stadium, built on publicly acquired land in 1962) to entirely private (SoFi Stadium in Inglewood, $5.5 billion, no public subsidy; Chase Center in San Francisco, $1.4 billion, no public subsidy; Intuit Dome in Inglewood, $2 billion, no public subsidy). The recent pattern is striking: California's newest major sports facilities have been built without the direct public subsidies (bond-financed construction, dedicated tax revenues, infrastructure grants) that characterized stadium deals in the 20th century and that continue in other states.

The shift away from public stadium subsidies in California reflects several factors: Proposition 13's constraints on local government borrowing, the political difficulty of justifying public spending on billionaire-owned sports franchises, and the availability of private capital (team owners and their development partners can finance stadium construction through naming rights, premium seating revenue, and real estate development surrounding the facility). SoFi Stadium's $5.5 billion cost was financed entirely by Rams owner Stan Kroenke. Chase Center was financed by the Warriors ownership group. The model demonstrates that private financing of professional sports facilities is feasible in high-revenue markets.

## The Exception

The Oakland Athletics' departure to Las Vegas in 2024 is the counterexample. The A's sought a new publicly subsidized stadium in Oakland, were offered various sites and partial public financing packages, rejected them, and accepted a $380 million public subsidy from Nevada to build a new stadium in Las Vegas. Oakland was left with an empty Coliseum and the loss of a franchise that had played in the city since 1968.

The A's departure illustrates the hostage dynamic that defines stadium economics: a team owner credibly threatens to relocate unless the public provides a subsidy, and the city must decide whether the economic and cultural value of the team exceeds the subsidy cost. Economic research consistently finds that stadium subsidies do not generate net positive economic impact for the subsidizing jurisdiction: the jobs are low-wage and seasonal, the spending is mostly diverted from other local entertainment (substitution rather than new spending), and the returns on public investment are negative. But the loss of a team is a civic wound that politicians find difficult to accept, and the threat of departure gives team owners leverage that the economics do not justify.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that professional sports stadiums are private entertainment facilities for billionaire-owned businesses that generate economic returns for their owners and minimal net economic benefit for the communities in which they are located. The academic consensus (represented by dozens of peer-reviewed studies from economists at Stanford, University of Chicago, University of Maryland, and other institutions) is clear: stadium subsidies are a transfer of public wealth to private owners, justified by inflated economic impact studies commissioned by the teams themselves. California's recent movement toward private financing (SoFi, Chase Center, Intuit Dome) is the correct model: if a stadium generates sufficient economic value, the private sector will finance it. If it does not, the public should not.

The generous read is that professional sports franchises provide cultural and civic value that economic impact studies do not capture: community identity, collective experience, national visibility, and the intangible benefit of civic pride. The loss of the A's to Las Vegas was experienced by Oakland as a loss of identity, not just a loss of economic activity. California's approach, allowing private financing for teams that can afford it while refusing to match the subsidies offered by competing jurisdictions, is economically rational but culturally costly. It means California will lose franchises to states willing to subsidize. Whether the avoided subsidy cost exceeds the cultural loss is a question economics cannot answer.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

No California agency tracks the economic impact of professional sports franchises or venues. The economic impact studies cited in stadium debates are commissioned by interested parties (team owners, local governments seeking to justify subsidies) and use multiplier assumptions that academic economists consistently find inflated. An independent, state-funded assessment of the actual economic impact of California's 18 major professional sports franchises does not exist.
