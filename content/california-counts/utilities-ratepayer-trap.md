# California Counts: The Ratepayer Trap
## What $0.30 per kilowatt-hour, $36 billion in wildfire liabilities, a utility that killed 84 people, and a rate structure that punishes electrification reveal about the electric bills California cannot make affordable

California's average residential electricity rate is approximately $0.30 per kilowatt-hour, roughly 80 percent above the national average of $0.17 and the highest or second-highest of any state in the continental United States (behind only Connecticut in some months). The three investor-owned utilities (IOUs) that serve the majority of California's electricity customers, Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E, 5.5 million electric customers), Southern California Edison (SCE, 5 million), and San Diego Gas and Electric (SDG&E, 1.5 million), charge rates that have increased approximately 50 to 70 percent since 2019. SDG&E's average residential rate exceeds $0.40 per kilowatt-hour, the highest of any major utility in the nation. A typical California household using 700 kilowatt-hours per month pays approximately $210 to $280 per month for electricity, depending on utility territory and rate tier.

The rates are high because the bills carry costs that have nothing to do with the commodity price of electricity. The cost of generating and purchasing electricity accounts for approximately 35 to 40 percent of a typical California electricity bill. The remaining 60 to 65 percent is transmission and distribution infrastructure, wildfire liability costs, public purpose programs (energy efficiency, low-income assistance, renewable energy mandates), nuclear decommissioning, and the return on equity guaranteed to the IOUs by the California Public Utilities Commission. The California bill is not a bill for electricity. It is a bill for electricity plus the accumulated cost of wildfires, infrastructure underinvestment, policy mandates, and guaranteed utility profits, bundled into a single monthly charge.

## The Wildfire Surcharge

PG&E's equipment has been identified as the cause of some of California's most destructive wildfires, including the Camp Fire (2018), which destroyed the town of Paradise, killed 84 people, and remains the deadliest and most destructive wildfire in California history. PG&E's total wildfire-related liabilities have exceeded $36 billion, including settlements, judgments, and the costs of the 2019 bankruptcy that resulted from them. The bankruptcy reorganization plan, approved in 2020, included a $13.5 billion wildfire victims' fund and imposed ongoing obligations for vegetation management, equipment upgrades, and system hardening (undergrounding power lines, replacing wooden poles with steel, installing covered conductors).

The costs of these safety investments are recovered through rates. PG&E's General Rate Case filings with the CPUC request authorization to increase rates to fund system hardening, vegetation management, and other safety expenditures. The CPUC approves rate increases through a regulatory process that balances utility cost recovery with ratepayer protection, but the trajectory is clear: every dollar spent on wildfire prevention and system hardening is ultimately paid by ratepayers. The utility that caused the fires and the ratepayers who suffered from them are, through the rate structure, the same population.

AB 1054 (2019) created a Wildfire Fund, capitalized at $21 billion through a combination of utility contributions and ratepayer charges, to cover future wildfire liabilities. The ratepayer contribution to the fund is embedded in electricity rates as a non-bypassable charge, meaning that even customers who generate their own solar electricity and use net metering pay into the wildfire fund. The fund provides utilities with access to capital to cover wildfire liabilities without bankruptcy, but it is funded, ultimately, by the same customers whose electricity the utilities deliver.

## The Electrification Paradox

California's climate policy depends on electrification: replacing gasoline vehicles with electric vehicles, replacing gas furnaces with heat pumps, replacing gas stoves with induction cooktops. The state has mandated that all new vehicles sold by 2035 be zero-emission. All-electric building codes are being adopted by dozens of cities. The Air Resources Board's strategy for achieving 2045 carbon neutrality depends on electrifying approximately 80 percent of end uses that currently burn fossil fuels.

The paradox is that California's electricity rates make electrification economically irrational for many consumers. A homeowner considering replacing a natural gas furnace with an electric heat pump will pay more per unit of useful heat because electricity at $0.30 per kWh costs more than natural gas at $1.50 per therm on a heat-equivalent basis, even accounting for the heat pump's efficiency advantage. An EV owner charging at home pays $0.30 per kWh for electricity that delivers approximately 3.5 miles per kWh, producing a per-mile fuel cost of approximately $0.086. A fuel-efficient gasoline car getting 35 mpg at $4.50 per gallon costs approximately $0.129 per mile. The EV is cheaper per mile, but the margin is thinner than in states with lower electricity rates, and for high-mileage drivers, the absolute electricity bill can be startling.

The CPUC recognized this paradox and approved AB 205 (2022) income-graduated fixed charges, which reduce the per-kWh rate and replace a portion of revenue with a fixed monthly charge that varies by household income. The fixed charge, set at $24.15 per month for most households (with lower rates for qualifying low-income customers), lowers the volumetric rate, making electrification more economically attractive by reducing the marginal cost of each additional kWh consumed. The reform is directionally correct but politically contentious: customers who use little electricity see their bills increase, while high-usage customers (including those who have already electrified) see savings.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's electricity rate structure is a policy failure that undermines the state's own climate strategy. The state that requires electrification charges the highest electricity rates in the continental U.S. The bills carry costs, wildfire liabilities, infrastructure hardening, utility profits, that should arguably be funded through taxation or insurance rather than embedded in the commodity price of the clean energy source the state is trying to promote. Every dollar added to the electricity rate makes electrification less attractive, gas more competitive, and rooftop solar (which allows affluent homeowners to reduce their grid purchases and shift costs to remaining ratepayers) more appealing. The rate spiral, higher rates drive departures from the grid, remaining customers bear higher costs, which drives more departures, is the utility death spiral that energy economists have warned about for a decade. PG&E's response to causing the deaths of 84 people was to pass the cost of preventing future deaths to the people it serves. The regulatory system that approved this outcome is not protecting ratepayers. It is protecting the utility.

The generous read is that California's high electricity rates reflect the real cost of operating an electric grid in a state with extreme wildfire risk, ambitious renewable energy mandates, and the infrastructure investment required to serve 40 million people reliably. The rates include costs that other states defer or externalize: vegetation management that prevents wildfires, grid hardening that improves reliability, renewable energy procurement that reduces emissions, and low-income assistance programs that help 4 million customers afford their bills. The income-graduated fixed charge is a structural reform that addresses the electrification paradox by lowering marginal rates. And the alternatives to the IOU model, municipal utilities, community choice aggregators, microgrids, are growing: approximately 12 million Californians now receive electricity from CCAs, public utilities, or cooperatives rather than IOUs. The market is restructuring, slowly, and the direction is toward more local control, more renewable generation, and more competition.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The CPUC publishes rate case decisions, utility financial reports, and rate comparisons. It does not publish a disaggregated breakdown of the average residential electricity bill showing exactly how much of each dollar goes to generation, transmission, distribution, wildfire costs, public purpose programs, and utility profit. Utilities publish this information in rate case filings but not in a consumer-friendly format that would allow a typical ratepayer to understand why their bill is $250. The opacity of the bill's composition makes public discussion of rate reform difficult: you cannot have a productive debate about which costs should be in the rate and which should be funded elsewhere when most participants do not know what is in the rate.
