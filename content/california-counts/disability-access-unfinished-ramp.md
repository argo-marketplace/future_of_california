# California Counts: The Unfinished Ramp
## What 6.8 million Californians with disabilities, a 35 percent employment rate, an ADA that turned 35, and 15,000 accessibility lawsuits per year reveal about the civil rights promise California made to one-sixth of its population and has not finished building

Approximately 6.8 million Californians (approximately 17 percent of the population) have a disability, as defined by the U.S. Census Bureau (difficulty with hearing, vision, cognition, ambulation, self-care, or independent living). The disability population spans all ages (approximately 8 percent of children, 12 percent of working-age adults, and 35 percent of adults over 65) and is disproportionately low-income: approximately 25 percent of Californians with disabilities live below the federal poverty line, compared to approximately 11 percent of those without disabilities.

The employment rate for working-age Californians with disabilities is approximately 35 percent, compared to approximately 75 percent for those without disabilities. The gap, approximately 40 percentage points, has remained essentially unchanged since the ADA was enacted in 1990. The employment gap reflects both demand-side barriers (employer discrimination, inaccessible workplaces, reluctance to provide accommodations) and supply-side barriers (inadequate education and training, transportation inaccessibility, health limitations, and the "benefits cliff" in which earning above certain thresholds causes loss of Medi-Cal, SSI, or IHSS benefits that may be worth more than the wages earned).

## The Litigation Model

California leads the nation in ADA accessibility litigation, with approximately 15,000 lawsuits filed per year (approximately 40 percent of all ADA lawsuits nationally). The lawsuits target businesses (restaurants, retailers, hotels, medical offices) that fail to comply with ADA and California's Unruh Civil Rights Act accessibility requirements: missing ramps, inaccessible restrooms, inadequate signage, non-compliant parking, and inaccessible websites. The California Unruh Act provides statutory damages of $4,000 per violation (compared to no monetary damages under the federal ADA), creating an economic incentive for serial litigation. A subset of plaintiffs and their attorneys file hundreds or thousands of lawsuits per year, a practice that business groups call predatory and disability advocates call the enforcement mechanism that the government fails to provide.

The litigation model is a consequence of the enforcement gap: no government agency in California systematically inspects businesses for ADA compliance. The ADA places the burden of enforcement on the individuals who are denied access: a wheelchair user who cannot enter a restaurant must file a complaint or lawsuit to compel the restaurant to build a ramp. The government builds neither the ramp nor the enforcement system. The litigation fills the gap, inefficiently and contentiously, because no public institution occupies the space.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has spent 35 years since the ADA's passage and has not achieved physical accessibility, employment equality, or service access for its 6.8 million residents with disabilities. The 35 percent employment rate is a civil rights failure. The 15,000 lawsuits per year are evidence that the built environment remains inaccessible three decades after the law required accessibility. The regional center system serves 400,000 Californians with developmental disabilities at a cost of $14 billion per year but pays direct support professionals $18 to $22 per hour, producing turnover rates of 40 to 50 percent that destabilize the care on which clients depend. The state has built a disability services infrastructure that maintains dependency (IHSS, SSI, Medi-Cal, regional centers) while failing to build the systems (accessible transit, accessible workplaces, inclusive education, assistive technology) that would enable independence.

The generous read is that California has the most extensive disability rights framework of any state: the Unruh Civil Rights Act (broader than the federal ADA), the Lanterman Developmental Disabilities Act (guaranteeing services for developmental disabilities), IHSS (the largest home-care program in the nation), and the Department of Rehabilitation's vocational services. The 15,000 lawsuits per year, while contentious, have produced measurable improvements in physical accessibility: the percentage of California businesses with accessible entrances, restrooms, and parking has increased substantially since the ADA's passage. The employment gap is persistent but narrowing: the growth of remote work since 2020 has particularly benefited workers with mobility disabilities, and the state's disability employment programs served approximately 50,000 individuals per year pre-pandemic. The ramp is unfinished. The ramp is being built.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Census Bureau publishes disability prevalence and employment data. No California agency publishes data on the accessibility of the state's built environment (what percentage of businesses, government buildings, transit stops, and public spaces are ADA-compliant), the outcomes of the 15,000 annual ADA lawsuits (how many produce actual accessibility improvements versus monetary settlements), or the economic independence of people with disabilities (income, housing stability, transportation access, community participation). The disability rights framework establishes legal obligations. Whether those obligations are fulfilled, for how many people, and with what effect on quality of life is not systematically measured.
