# California Counts: The Government Ad
## What $100 million at Covered California, a COVID-era lottery campaign, and $250 to $400 million in uncentralized ad spending reveal about the state government that markets itself

California state government runs advertisements. You have seen them on freeway billboards, television, radio, and social media: Covered California enrollment reminders, highway safety campaigns, anti-vaping public service announcements, COVID vaccination appeals. The government's media presence is substantial, and it is not paid for from a single budget line that any analyst can easily find. State advertising spending is fragmented across dozens of agencies and programs, funded through a mix of general fund appropriations, federal grants, special fund revenues, and enterprise fees. A rough estimate of the total runs $250 to $400 million per year. Nobody inside California state government publishes that number.

---

## Covered California: The Biggest Spender

Covered California, the state's Affordable Care Act marketplace, is almost certainly the single largest advertiser in California state government. The agency spends approximately $100 to $120 million per year on advertising, marketing, outreach, and communications, funded primarily by the fees it charges health insurance plans to participate in the marketplace rather than state general fund dollars. Because Covered California is a self-funded public entity, its advertising budget is not subject to the same annual appropriations scrutiny that general fund spending receives.

The rationale for the advertising investment is actuarial as much as civic. An ACA marketplace that attracts primarily sick enrollees and repels healthy ones develops adverse selection problems that destabilize the risk pool and raise premiums. Covered California's enrollment outreach is therefore a direct investment in keeping the risk pool balanced and premiums stable. By this logic, the advertising budget is not a communications cost but an actuarial intervention. Whether $100 to $120 million is the correct amount, relative to the size and health of the enrolled population, is a question that Covered California's board evaluates annually but that is not independently benchmarked against other ACA marketplaces.

Covered California's enrollment advertising has been widely regarded as effective: the state's marketplace has among the highest enrollment rates and lowest uninsured rates in the country for its eligible population. Enrollment reached approximately 1.8 million plan selections for the 2024 plan year. The cost per enrolled person attributable to advertising, roughly $55 to $67 per enrollee at $100 million against 1.8 million enrollees, is not a directly published metric.

## The COVID Campaigns

The most visible state advertising spending in recent California history came during the COVID-19 pandemic. Between 2020 and 2022, California spent hundreds of millions on public health communications: mask-wearing campaigns, social distancing campaigns, vaccine outreach, testing location promotion, and behavioral messaging designed to slow transmission.

The "Vax for the Win" campaign, launched in spring 2021, is the most distinctive example. California partnered with the State Lottery to offer a series of lottery drawings for vaccinated Californians. The advertising component, handled by Foote, Cone and Belding (FCB), promoted both the lottery and the vaccination drive simultaneously. The total campaign, including the prize money and the advertising contract, cost approximately $116 million. The lottery component alone paid out $116.5 million in prizes across multiple drawings.

The campaign attracted approximately 4 million unique registrations. Whether those registrations represented people who were vaccinated specifically because of the campaign, or people who were already vaccinated and registered for the prize, is not determinable from the registration data alone. The state reported that California's vaccination rate exceeded its pre-campaign trajectory during the campaign period, which is consistent with causation but does not establish it. At $116 million, it was one of the most expensive single state public health campaigns in the country.

COVID-related public health advertising broadly, including California Department of Public Health contracts for general pandemic messaging, produced a spike in state advertising spending in 2020 through 2022 that is not representative of baseline spending in non-pandemic years.

## The Rest of the Portfolio

Beyond Covered California and the COVID campaigns, California's advertising spending is distributed across numerous agencies with distinct programs and funding sources.

Caltrans runs highway safety campaigns, including distracted driving, drunk driving, and seatbelt reminders. These campaigns receive funding from both state highway funds and federal safety grants from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration. Annual spending is in the range of $30 to $50 million.

The California Department of Public Health administers the state's anti-tobacco and anti-vaping programs, funded largely by the Tobacco Tax and Health Protection Act (Proposition 99 and subsequent measures). Tobacco control advertising, which California pioneered in the 1990s as the first major statewide tobacco counter-marketing program, runs approximately $20 to $30 million per year.

The California Department of Motor Vehicles runs awareness campaigns around license renewal, REAL ID compliance, and electric vehicle registration. The Employment Development Department runs campaigns directing unemployed workers to its services. The Department of Consumer Affairs and the Department of Financial Protection and Innovation run fraud prevention and consumer awareness campaigns. The Franchise Tax Board and the California Department of Tax and Fee Administration remind taxpayers to file.

The Governor's Office of Business and Economic Development (GO-Biz) promotes California as a business destination, particularly for film and television production recruitment. The California Film Commission, which administers the California Film and TV Tax Credit program, engages in marketing to the entertainment industry.

None of these budgets are collected in a single annual report. The Office of State Publishing coordinates some government printing and publications. The Department of General Services negotiates some advertising contracts. The Governor's Office of Planning and Research coordinates communications strategy in a limited way. But the advertising budget of California state government as a whole is not a number that any single office knows or publishes.

---

## The Transparency Gap

The absence of consolidated advertising spending data is not an accident or an oversight. It is a structural feature of how California government is organized. Each agency and department is appropriated its own budget, within which communications and advertising spending are typically aggregated into a broader "operating expenses and equipment" line or a program-specific budget category. Disentangling advertising and media buys from general operating expenses requires agency-by-agency review of detailed expenditure records.

Open FI$Cal, California's financial transparency portal, contains payment-level data that would allow a researcher to identify vendors associated with advertising, media, and public relations contracts: entries paid to media buying agencies, advertising firms, public relations consultants, and digital advertising platforms. A comprehensive pull of vendor payments to entities in these categories across all state agencies would produce a rough estimate of total advertising spending. That analysis has not been systematically published by any state agency or the Legislature, and the exercise requires significant data cleaning because many payments combine advertising with other services.

The Government Operations Agency, which coordinates operational functions across multiple departments, does not publish a consolidated communications spending report. The Department of Finance, which controls the state budget process, does not produce a crosscutting analysis of advertising and communications spending. The State Auditor has not conducted a recent audit of state advertising spending as a whole.

---

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California state government spends an estimated $250 to $400 million per year on advertising and public communications without publishing a consolidated accounting of that spending, making it impossible for the public, the Legislature, or outside analysts to evaluate whether the portfolio is cost-effective, whether it is appropriately bounded, or whether any individual campaign delivers results proportionate to its cost. Covered California's $100 to $120 million advertising budget is self-funded and therefore subject to less legislative scrutiny than general fund advertising, yet it is ultimately funded by insurance premiums paid by Californians. The COVID vaccination lottery campaign cost $116 million for a behavioral outcome that the data cannot cleanly attribute to the campaign. Caltrans and CDPH each spend tens of millions on campaigns whose effectiveness is assessed, if at all, through measures the agencies themselves report. The absence of consolidated reporting is not a minor technical gap. It is the kind of accountability blind spot that allows spending to continue indefinitely without systematic evaluation.

The generous read is that state government advertising serves genuine public purposes that have documented value. Covered California's outreach keeps the risk pool healthy and premiums stable. Anti-tobacco advertising has contributed to a decline in California smoking rates from approximately 23 percent in 1990 to approximately 9 percent in 2024, a public health outcome worth billions in avoided healthcare costs. Highway safety campaigns have measurable correlations with seatbelt use and impaired driving rates. Vaccine outreach during the pandemic reached populations that would otherwise have been underserved by clinical channels. The lack of consolidated reporting reflects the genuine difficulty of aggregating spending across a government of California's complexity, not a deliberate effort to evade accountability. And the total figure, estimated at $250 to $400 million against a total state budget of $310 billion, represents less than two-tenths of one percent of state spending on communications that arguably enables all the other programs to function by informing the public of their existence.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

Open FI$Cal contains payment records that are a necessary but not sufficient basis for estimating total advertising spending. The data shows payments to vendors but does not categorize those payments by purpose without additional processing. A vendor that receives a payment from CDPH might provide advertising services, laboratory equipment, or professional training; the vendor name alone does not establish the category.

The data also does not show the outcome of advertising campaigns in a form that allows comparison across campaigns. Covered California tracks enrollment. CDPH tracks tobacco use rates. Caltrans tracks traffic fatality rates. Each uses its own metrics, over its own time series, without a common evaluation framework. Whether any given campaign produced its behavioral outcome or whether the outcome would have occurred without the advertising is the central evaluation question, and answering it requires experimental or quasi-experimental designs that are rarely applied to government advertising programs in California.

The $250 to $400 million range for total annual advertising spending is a rough estimate, not an audited figure. The true number could be higher, depending on how COVID-era spending is treated and what program categories are included in the definition.

---

California state government is a large, complex institution that does things most Californians do not know about, and it spends money to tell them. Some of that spending changes behavior in ways that save lives and reduce healthcare costs. Some of it may primarily sustain the agencies that commissioned it. The data necessary to distinguish between those categories does not exist in a form that anyone has published. At $250 to $400 million per year, the government's advertising portfolio is big enough to merit a consolidated accounting. The fact that no such accounting exists, in a state with a financial transparency portal sophisticated enough to track individual vendor payments, is itself a data point worth noting.
