# California Counts: The Parallel System
## What 1,300 charter schools, 680,000 students, a $7.5 billion share of LCFF funding, and the most permissive charter law in the nation reveal about the parallel school system California built inside its public one

California has approximately 1,300 charter schools enrolling roughly 680,000 students, approximately 11 percent of the state's K-12 public school enrollment. It is the largest charter school sector in the nation by enrollment. Charter schools are publicly funded, tuition-free, and open to all students through a lottery if demand exceeds capacity. They are operated by nonprofit (and, until recently, for-profit) organizations under a charter petition approved by a local school district, county office of education, or the State Board of Education. The charter petition specifies the school's educational program, governance structure, and accountability measures. In exchange for operational flexibility, including exemption from many provisions of the Education Code, charter schools are theoretically held accountable for the outcomes specified in their charters.

Charter schools receive approximately $7.5 billion per year through the Local Control Funding Formula, the same per-pupil funding that traditional public schools receive. The funding follows the student: when a student transfers from a traditional public school to a charter school, the per-pupil LCFF allocation transfers with them. This is the core financial tension in the charter-traditional relationship. A district that loses 500 students to charter schools loses approximately $6.5 million in LCFF funding but cannot reduce its fixed costs (buildings, administration, debt service, tenured staff) by the same proportion. The marginal cost of each departing student is lower than the average funding per student, meaning that the district's per-pupil spending for remaining students decreases. The charter school's gain is the traditional school's loss, a zero-sum dynamic that has produced decades of political conflict between charter operators and school districts.

## Performance: The Contested Evidence

The academic performance of charter schools relative to traditional public schools is the most contested question in California education policy. The Stanford Center for Research on Education Outcomes (CREDO) studies, the most methodologically rigorous comparisons available, have found that California charter school students gain approximately 14 additional days of learning per year in reading and 7 additional days in math compared to their traditional public school counterparts, after controlling for student demographics. The gains are concentrated in urban charter schools serving low-income students of color; suburban and rural charter schools show smaller or no differences.

The statewide averages conceal enormous variation. The highest-performing charter networks (KIPP, Aspire, Alliance, Green Dot) produce outcomes significantly above district averages for comparable student populations. Other charter schools perform at or below district levels. Approximately 15 to 20 percent of charter schools have been closed or non-renewed for academic or fiscal reasons since the charter school law was enacted in 1992. The closure rate is part of the charter model's theory of change: underperforming schools are closed through the accountability provisions of the charter petition, a consequence that traditional public schools rarely face.

AB 1505 (2019) tightened the charter authorization process, giving school districts more authority to deny charter petitions based on the fiscal impact on the district, the quality of the proposed program, and the needs of the community. The law also restricted the State Board of Education's authority to override local denials and prohibited new for-profit charter management organizations. The effect has been a reduction in new charter school openings: annual new charter petitions have declined approximately 40 percent since AB 1505's enactment.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has allowed a parallel school system to develop within its public education system, one that siphons funding from traditional schools, operates with less transparency and public governance accountability (charter boards are not elected), employs a largely non-unionized workforce (approximately 70 percent of charter school teachers are not represented by unions), and produces academic outcomes that, at the statewide level, are marginally better for some students and no different for others. The 680,000 students in charter schools represent 680,000 per-pupil LCFF allocations that are no longer available to the traditional schools the remaining 5.2 million students attend. The charter model's promise, that competition and innovation would improve outcomes for all students, has not been demonstrated at scale. Traditional schools in districts with significant charter enrollment are not measurably improving in response to competition; they are losing resources.

The generous read is that charter schools provide 680,000 California students, disproportionately low-income and students of color, with educational options that did not previously exist. The highest-performing charter networks have demonstrated that school-level factors (curriculum, instruction, culture, leadership) can produce dramatically better outcomes for students from the same neighborhoods and demographics as the lowest-performing traditional schools. The closure of underperforming charters, however painful, is an accountability mechanism that traditional schools lack. And the 11 percent of students enrolled in charter schools represents a market signal: families are choosing these schools because they offer something (longer school days, different instructional approaches, school culture, safety) that the assigned traditional school does not. The charter sector is imperfect, unevenly regulated, and in tension with the traditional system. It is also the product of 680,000 family choices.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Department of Education publishes charter school enrollment, demographics, and academic performance data through DataQuest. It does not publish a comprehensive fiscal impact analysis showing the net financial effect of charter schools on traditional school districts. Individual districts have produced such analyses (LAUSD's Independent Financial Review Panel estimated the district's annual charter-related fiscal impact at approximately $500 million), but no statewide, standardized methodology exists. The charter sector's fiscal impact on the system it operates within is estimated, debated, and litigated rather than measured.
