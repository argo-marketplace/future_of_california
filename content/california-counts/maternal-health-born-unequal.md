# California Counts: Born Unequal
## What a 50 percent decline in maternal mortality, a 4-to-1 racial disparity that survived the decline, 420,000 annual births, and a Medi-Cal system that covers half of all deliveries reveal about who lives and who dies in California childbirth

California's maternal mortality rate has declined approximately 50 percent since 2006, from approximately 17 deaths per 100,000 live births to approximately 8 to 10, making it one of the lowest in the nation and an outlier against the national trend (the U.S. maternal mortality rate has approximately doubled over the same period, reaching approximately 32 per 100,000). The decline is the result of a specific institutional intervention: the California Maternal Quality Care Collaborative (CMQCC), a partnership between the state, hospitals, and clinical organizations that developed standardized protocols (toolkits) for managing the leading causes of maternal death, obstetric hemorrhage, preeclampsia, and venous thromboembolism, and implemented them across nearly every delivering hospital in the state.

The CMQCC model is a genuine California success story. It has been studied and replicated by other states and endorsed by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists as a national model. The 50 percent decline in maternal mortality saved approximately 200 to 300 lives over a decade. It was achieved through institutional coordination, data-driven quality improvement, and the willingness of hospitals to adopt standardized protocols, a combination of factors that is rare in American healthcare and that demonstrates what the state's healthcare infrastructure can accomplish when it is directed toward a specific, measurable goal.

---

## The Disparity That Survived

The decline in maternal mortality was not equally distributed. Black women in California die in childbirth at approximately four times the rate of white women: approximately 28 to 35 deaths per 100,000 live births for Black women, compared to approximately 7 to 9 for white women. The disparity has persisted even as the overall rate declined. The CMQCC's protocols reduced mortality across all racial groups, but the gap between Black and white women has remained approximately 4 to 1 throughout the improvement period.

The disparity is not explained by income, insurance status, or education. Studies published in the American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology and other peer-reviewed journals have found that college-educated Black women have higher maternal mortality rates than white women without a high school diploma. Black women with private insurance have higher rates than white women on Medi-Cal. The disparity persists after controlling for every observable risk factor that the clinical and demographic data can measure.

The leading explanations in the maternal health literature center on structural racism as a health determinant: the cumulative physiological toll of experiencing discrimination over a lifetime (the "weathering" hypothesis), differential treatment by healthcare providers (implicit bias in clinical decision-making, dismissal of Black women's reported symptoms), and the concentration of Black births in hospitals with lower quality metrics. A 2023 study found that Black women in California were more likely to deliver at hospitals with higher severe maternal morbidity rates, even after controlling for geographic access, suggesting that hospital quality and the distribution of Black patients across hospitals contribute to the disparity.

## Medi-Cal and Maternal Care

Approximately 45 to 50 percent of all births in California are covered by Medi-Cal, the state's Medicaid program. For first births to mothers under 25, the percentage exceeds 60 percent. Medi-Cal's reimbursement rates for obstetric services are approximately 40 to 60 percent of Medicare rates and 20 to 30 percent of private insurance rates. A vaginal delivery reimbursed at approximately $2,500 by Medi-Cal would be reimbursed at approximately $5,000 by Medicare and $8,000 to $12,000 by private insurance.

The low reimbursement rates limit the obstetric providers willing to accept Medi-Cal patients. Approximately 40 percent of California obstetricians do not accept new Medi-Cal patients, according to California Health Care Foundation surveys. The access constraint is most acute in rural areas, where the combination of low reimbursement, small patient volume, and high malpractice insurance costs has driven many rural hospitals to close their obstetric units. Approximately 30 California hospitals have closed their labor and delivery units since 2012, creating "maternity care deserts," counties where no hospital provides obstetric services and the nearest delivering hospital is 30 minutes to two hours away.

SB 65 (2021), the California Momnibus Act, extended Medi-Cal postpartum coverage from 60 days to 12 months, ensuring that low-income mothers retain health insurance through the first year after delivery. The extension addresses the postpartum period, which accounts for approximately one-third of pregnancy-related deaths: cardiac events, infection, and mental health crises that occur weeks or months after delivery. The 12-month extension was a significant policy change, adopted by California ahead of the federal requirement and now covering an estimated 150,000 postpartum women per year.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's maternal health success story has a racial asterisk. The state cut its maternal mortality rate in half, but a Black woman giving birth in California is still four times as likely to die as a white woman giving birth in the same state, in the same year, with the same education and insurance. The CMQCC model improved clinical quality across the board but did not close the racial gap, suggesting that the disparity is not primarily a clinical quality problem but a structural one rooted in the healthcare system's differential treatment of Black patients, the distribution of Black patients across lower-quality hospitals, and the accumulated health burden of experiencing racism in a society that has not dismantled its effects. The 50 percent decline in maternal mortality is a genuine achievement. It is an achievement that applies the word "California" to an average that conceals the experience of Black women, who live in a different California when they enter a delivery room.

The generous read is that California has done more than any other state to reduce maternal mortality, and the institutional infrastructure it built, the CMQCC, the quality collaboratives, the standardized protocols, is now being turned toward the racial disparity specifically. The CMQCC's Birth Equity Collaborative, launched in 2018, targets the hospitals and communities where Black maternal mortality is highest with interventions designed to address implicit bias, improve clinical communication, and increase the representation of Black women in obstetric and midwifery care. The Momnibus Act addresses the postpartum coverage gap that disproportionately affected Black and low-income women. And the data infrastructure that made the mortality reduction possible, hospital-level tracking of severe maternal morbidity, standardized case review, and public reporting, is the same infrastructure that can measure whether the equity interventions are working. The gap is unconscionable. The tools to close it exist. Whether the state will apply them with the same urgency it brought to the overall mortality reduction is the test.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Department of Public Health publishes maternal mortality data by race, age, and county. It does not publish hospital-level maternal mortality data in a publicly accessible format, making it impossible for patients or researchers to identify which hospitals have the highest and lowest mortality rates by race. The CMQCC collects hospital-level quality data but reports it to participating hospitals confidentially as a quality improvement tool, not publicly as a consumer information resource. A pregnant woman choosing a hospital cannot access data showing that hospital's maternal mortality rate, severe morbidity rate, or C-section rate by racial group.

The data also does not capture severe maternal morbidity, the near-misses that do not result in death but cause lasting health consequences (emergency hysterectomy, massive blood transfusion, intensive care admission). For every maternal death, an estimated 50 to 100 women experience severe maternal morbidity. The racial disparity in morbidity mirrors the disparity in mortality. The denominator of harm is far larger than the death count suggests.
