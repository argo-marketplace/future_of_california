# California Counts: The Deferred Bill
## What $100 billion in accumulated deferred maintenance, $1.3 billion at state parks, $3.4 billion at the UC system, $2.8 billion at CSU, a $4-to-$8 return on every dollar of neglect, and 20 million square feet of aging state buildings reveal about the spending California never shows you in the budget

Every year California publishes a budget that runs to hundreds of pages and accounts for billions in spending on education, healthcare, transportation, and public safety. What that budget does not show is the counter-budget: the growing bill for maintenance that has been deferred, the infrastructure that has been used but not repaired, the roofs that leaked last winter and will leak again next winter and will eventually collapse at a cost that dwarfs the repair that was never made. The Legislative Analyst's Office has estimated California's total deferred maintenance backlog, across state parks, UC and CSU campuses, courts, correctional facilities, and general state buildings, at over $100 billion. That figure does not appear as a line item in the budget. It accumulates off-balance-sheet, visible only to the auditors and engineers who walk through buildings and report that the electrical panel is from 1967, the HVAC system is beyond its service life, and the elevator has been out of compliance for four years.

---

## The State Building Inventory

The Department of General Services manages approximately 20 million square feet of state-owned buildings across California, including offices, laboratories, warehouses, and specialized facilities. The portfolio ranges from the Capitol complex in Sacramento to regional offices operated by regulatory agencies across all 58 counties. DGS estimates the replacement value of the buildings it manages at approximately $60 billion to $80 billion. The deferred maintenance on this subset of the portfolio alone runs to several billion dollars, though DGS has not published a comprehensive single-figure estimate in recent years.

The building stock is old. Many of the state's office buildings were constructed in the 1950s through 1980s, during a period of rapid state government expansion. Infrastructure lifespans for major building systems typically run 20 to 40 years for mechanical and electrical systems, 15 to 25 years for roofing, and longer for structural elements. A building constructed in 1970 is now 55 years old. If its HVAC system was last replaced in 1995, it is 30 years old and likely beyond its useful life. If the roof was last replaced in 2000, it is 25 years old. A comprehensive survey of the state-managed building portfolio would find that a large fraction of major building systems are at or past their recommended replacement age.

The capital outlay appropriation for state building maintenance and repair has typically run $200 million to $400 million per year in recent budget cycles, representing a fraction of what would be required to bring the portfolio to a state of good repair. The gap between annual appropriation and annual maintenance need is what accumulates as deferred maintenance. Every year the appropriation is below the need, the backlog grows.

## The Sector Breakdown

The deferred maintenance problem is distributed across the state's institutional portfolio, and each sector has its own accounting, its own governance, and its own political constituency for repairs.

State parks present the most visible public face of the problem. The Department of Parks and Recreation has identified a deferred maintenance backlog of approximately $1.3 billion as of the most recent comprehensive assessment, covering approximately 280 state park units and 340 miles of coastline. The needs include deteriorating roads, trails, and campground infrastructure; aging visitor centers and restroom facilities; and deteriorating interpretive structures at historical sites. The parks backlog has been identified, assessed, and reported for years. It has been addressed in part through periodic one-time appropriations, including a $200 million allocation in the 2021-22 budget and additional targeted funding. But the annual maintenance budget for parks has not been set at a level that prevents the backlog from re-accumulating.

The University of California system has quantified its deferred maintenance more precisely than most state entities. The UC's 10-campus system has an estimated deferred maintenance backlog of approximately $3.4 billion, covering laboratories, academic buildings, housing, and research infrastructure that in some cases dates to the 1960s. The UC's total facilities replacement value is approximately $84 billion. Deferred maintenance at 4 percent of replacement value is roughly the threshold at which facilities managers characterize a portfolio as in serious long-term decline; the UC's $3.4 billion represents approximately 4 percent of replacement value, placing it at that threshold.

The California State University system's 23-campus portfolio carries a deferred maintenance backlog of approximately $2.8 billion. The state auditor has flagged CSU's facilities condition as a significant concern, noting that many campuses have buildings that are below the condition index threshold that would normally trigger capital investment. Courts, managed by the Judicial Council of California, have identified hundreds of millions in deferred courthouse maintenance and seismic retrofitting needs. Correctional facilities operated by CDCR carry maintenance backlogs that are folded into the department's capital outlay requests but are not consolidated in a single public total.

## The Multiplier: $1 Deferred Becomes $4 to $8

The standard facilities management rule of thumb is that every dollar of deferred maintenance becomes $4 to $8 in future repair costs if the condition is allowed to deteriorate further. A roof that costs $500,000 to replace when first identified as failing may cost $2 million to $4 million to replace after several additional years of water intrusion have damaged the underlying structure, the insulation, the electrical systems adjacent to the infiltration point, and the finishes of occupied spaces below. A deferred HVAC replacement that leads to a winter system failure in a building housing state employees produces emergency repair costs, potential facility closure, lost productivity, and possibly employee health claims that dwarf the cost of scheduled replacement.

The multiplier is not uniform, and the $4 to $8 range is a planning heuristic rather than a precise coefficient. But it is directionally robust: deferred maintenance is not a cost avoided, it is a cost deferred and compounded. California's $100 billion backlog, if the 4x multiplier applies to the portion that has passed the point of simple repair, implies a potential future cost exposure well beyond the face value of the deferred work.

The political economy that produces this accumulation is consistent and well-documented. Annual appropriations favor visible, discretionary spending over maintenance of existing assets. A new building generates a ribbon-cutting. A repaired roof generates nothing visible to voters. Capital outlay requests for maintenance compete with requests for new programs, healthcare spending, and education in a budget process that runs on annual cycles. The entities that own the most deteriorating facilities, parks, universities, correctional departments, tend to make their maintenance case in their own budget silos, without a consolidated cross-sector view that would make the total backlog politically visible.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has been spending money on new programs and new facilities while allowing its existing assets to deteriorate, effectively borrowing from future generations of taxpayers who will pay the compounded cost of deferred maintenance. The $100 billion backlog is a form of hidden debt: unlike bond issuances, it does not appear on balance sheets, does not require legislative authorization, and accumulates silently year by year. The state's annual capital outlay appropriation has historically run at levels that prevent catching up; at $300 million per year for DGS buildings, addressing a $3 billion backlog takes a decade even if the backlog stops growing, which it does not. Meanwhile the $4 to $8 cost multiplier means that the eventual bill is larger than the avoided expenditure would have been. California is making a choice, year after year, to defer costs onto future budgets. It is presented as prudent fiscal management. It is actually the opposite.

The generous read is that capital maintenance competes with genuine competing needs, including healthcare for low-income Californians and education for children, that have immediate human consequences and strong moral claims on public resources. A state with unlimited resources would maintain all its buildings at a state of good repair. California does not have unlimited resources, and the people sleeping in state parks and studying in CSU classrooms generally cannot tell whether the HVAC system is five or fifteen years old. The LAO's $100 billion estimate is a long-run planning figure, not an emergency call for immediate appropriation. Some portion of that backlog can be deferred further without material consequence; the triage that facilities managers perform every year is not irrational given budget constraints. And the periodic large one-time investments, the 2021-22 parks appropriation, the UC capital bond programs, the courthouse construction funded by the 2008 and 2014 bonds, represent genuine efforts to address the backlog when fiscal conditions allow.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

California does not maintain a single consolidated public database of deferred maintenance across all state-owned and state-managed facilities. Each sector, DGS, DPR, UC, CSU, Judicial Council, CDCR, conducts its own facilities condition assessments on its own schedule and reports through its own budget process. The LAO's $100 billion estimate is a synthesis of available sector-level data, extrapolated to cover sectors that have not formally quantified their backlogs, and should be read as an order-of-magnitude estimate rather than an audited figure. The state does not routinely publish facilities condition indexes that would allow year-over-year tracking of whether the backlog is growing, shrinking, or holding steady. Without that baseline tracking, neither the Legislature nor the public has a reliable way to assess whether capital outlay appropriations are keeping pace with need.
