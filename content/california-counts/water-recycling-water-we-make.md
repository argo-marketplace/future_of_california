# California Counts: The Water We Make
## What 1.8 million acre-feet of recycled water, a $16 billion desalination plant that was never built, direct potable reuse regulations 50 years in the making, and a 4 million acre-foot supply gap reveal about California's reluctant turn to manufactured water

California recycles approximately 1.8 million acre-feet of treated wastewater per year, roughly 15 percent of its total municipal wastewater flow. The recycled water is used primarily for landscape irrigation, agricultural irrigation, industrial processes, and groundwater recharge. The remaining 85 percent of treated wastewater, approximately 10 million acre-feet per year, is discharged to rivers, bays, and the ocean. In a state that faces a structural supply gap of 3 to 4 million acre-feet per year between demand and reliable supply in average conditions, and as much as 6 to 8 million acre-feet in drought years, California sends enough treated wastewater to the ocean each year to close a significant portion of that gap.

The potential is enormous and largely untapped. The State Water Resources Control Board estimates that California could increase recycled water production to approximately 3.1 million acre-feet per year by 2035 with aggressive investment, more than doubling current production. The technology is proven: indirect potable reuse (purifying treated wastewater to drinking water standards and injecting it into groundwater basins for later extraction) has been practiced in Orange County since 1976. The Orange County Water District's Groundwater Replenishment System, the largest indirect potable reuse facility in the world, purifies approximately 130 million gallons per day, providing drinking water for approximately 850,000 people at a cost of approximately $1,200 per acre-foot, competitive with imported water from the State Water Project or Colorado River.

## Direct Potable Reuse: The Last Taboo

Direct potable reuse (DPR), purifying treated wastewater and sending it directly into the drinking water distribution system without the intermediate step of groundwater storage, is the logical next step. It eliminates the need for groundwater injection, reduces the infrastructure footprint, and can be implemented at any wastewater treatment plant with sufficient advanced treatment capacity. Multiple countries (Singapore, Namibia, South Africa) have practiced DPR for years or decades.

California's State Water Board adopted DPR regulations in December 2023, making California the second state (after Colorado) to authorize direct potable reuse. The regulations establish treatment standards, monitoring requirements, and operational protocols for DPR facilities. No DPR facility is yet operating in California; the first projects are expected to come online in the late 2020s. The timeline reflects both the regulatory complexity (DPR regulations were 50 years in the making, with study mandates dating back to the 1970s) and the persistent public resistance to what opponents have labeled "toilet to tap," a phrase that is technically inaccurate (the water undergoes purification to standards exceeding those of bottled water) but emotionally potent.

The cost of advanced purified water through DPR is approximately $1,500 to $2,500 per acre-foot, depending on the scale of the facility and local conditions. The cost is above the current price of State Water Project deliveries (approximately $1,000 to $1,500 per acre-foot) but below the cost of seawater desalination ($2,500 to $3,500 per acre-foot) and below the full cost of developing new surface storage. For coastal cities that face rising imported water costs and declining reliability, DPR offers a drought-proof, locally controlled supply at a competitive price.

## Desalination: The Road Not Taken

Seawater desalination has been proposed and debated in California for decades. The Carlsbad Desalination Plant, which began operating in 2015, is the largest desalination plant in the Western Hemisphere, producing approximately 50 million gallons per day (56,000 acre-feet per year) at a cost of approximately $2,500 per acre-foot. The plant was controversial during its decade-long permitting process and remains controversial: environmental groups opposed the marine life impacts of its ocean intake and brine discharge, and ratepayer advocates questioned whether the cost was justified when cheaper alternatives (conservation, recycled water, stormwater capture) remained underutilized.

The Poseidon Huntington Beach desalination project, proposed to produce a similar volume, was rejected by the California Coastal Commission in 2022 after 24 years of permitting, environmental review, and litigation. The rejection was based on environmental impacts (marine life entrainment, greenhouse gas emissions, brine discharge effects on the marine environment) and the determination that the water was not needed given the availability of less environmentally damaging alternatives. The project had an estimated capital cost of approximately $1.4 billion and would have produced water at approximately $2,800 to $3,500 per acre-foot.

California currently has approximately 12 small desalination plants operating, with a combined capacity of approximately 70,000 acre-feet per year. The total contribution of desalination to California's water supply is less than 1 percent. The state that borders the Pacific Ocean has functionally declined to desalinate seawater at scale, choosing instead to import water from the Colorado River (250 miles away) and the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta (400 miles from Southern California) through the most expensive water conveyance infrastructure ever built.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California discharges 10 million acre-feet of treated wastewater to the ocean each year in a state that does not have enough water. The technology to purify that water to drinking water standards has been proven for nearly 50 years. The cost is competitive with imported water. The regulations are now in place. And yet, as of 2026, California recycles 15 percent of its wastewater while Israel recycles approximately 85 percent and Singapore recycles approximately 40 percent. The desalination option has been effectively foreclosed by a permitting process that took a quarter-century to reject a single project. California's water crisis is, in significant part, a water reuse crisis: the state has the technology, the demand, and now the regulatory framework to produce millions of acre-feet of drought-proof local water, and it has not done so because the infrastructure investment, public acceptance campaigns, and institutional coordination required have not been forthcoming.

The generous read is that California's cautious approach to water reuse and desalination reflects legitimate concerns about public health, environmental impact, and cost. DPR regulations required decades of research to ensure that the treatment standards were sufficient to protect public health, and the precautionary approach was appropriate for a technology that, if it fails, puts contaminants in the drinking water supply. The Huntington Beach desalination rejection reflected genuine environmental costs that the project's proponents minimized. The Orange County GWRS demonstrates that indirect potable reuse works, and the DPR regulations will enable the next generation of projects. California is moving toward manufactured water, deliberately, with the regulatory rigor that public health demands. Speed is less important than getting the treatment standards right.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The State Water Board publishes recycled water production data by facility and use type. It does not publish a comprehensive cost comparison across water sources (recycled, desalinated, imported, groundwater, conservation) that would allow policymakers and the public to compare the full lifecycle cost of each supply option on a consistent basis. The cost figures cited for recycled water, desalination, and imported water come from different sources, use different methodologies, and include or exclude different cost components (capital, operating, environmental mitigation, conveyance). A standardized, publicly available cost-per-acre-foot comparison across all supply options does not exist.
