# California Counts: The Highway State
## What 50,000 lane-miles, 13,100 bridges, a $22 billion annual budget, and 340 billion vehicle miles traveled reveal about the road system that California built for the car and now cannot afford to maintain

The California Department of Transportation (Caltrans) manages the nation's largest state highway system: approximately 50,000 lane-miles of state highways and freeways and 13,100 state-owned bridges. The system carries approximately 340 billion vehicle miles traveled (VMT) per year, more than any other state. Caltrans' annual budget is approximately $22 billion, funded primarily by federal highway funds, the state gas tax (including the SB 1 increase of 2017), weight fees on commercial trucks, and general fund transfers. The department employs approximately 22,000 workers, making it one of the three largest state departments by headcount.

SB 1 (2017), the Road Repair and Accountability Act, increased the gas tax by $0.12 per gallon, increased the diesel excise tax by $0.20 per gallon, and imposed a new transportation improvement fee on vehicle registrations scaled by vehicle value. The combined measures generate approximately $5.4 billion per year, roughly doubling the state's prior transportation funding. The gas tax is adjusted annually for inflation. The fee structure was intended to address a backlog of road and bridge repairs that had accumulated during decades of flat per-gallon gas tax revenue: as vehicles became more fuel-efficient, the per-gallon tax produced less revenue even as VMT and maintenance needs grew.

## The Bridge Condition Problem

Of California's 13,100 state-owned bridges, approximately 1,500 (roughly 12 percent) are classified as structurally deficient or functionally obsolete under federal highway bridge inspection standards. A structurally deficient bridge is not necessarily unsafe; the classification means that one or more structural elements are in poor condition and require repair, rehabilitation, or replacement. The bridges are inspected every two years under federal requirements, and bridges with known deficiencies are inspected more frequently.

The seismic vulnerability of California's bridges is a separate and more urgent concern. The 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake collapsed a section of the Bay Bridge and the Cypress Street Viaduct in Oakland, killing 63 people. The 1994 Northridge earthquake collapsed sections of the Santa Monica Freeway, the Golden State Freeway, and the Antelope Valley Freeway. The Caltrans Seismic Retrofit Program, launched after Loma Prieta, has spent approximately $12 billion to $14 billion over three decades retrofitting approximately 2,200 state-owned bridges to current seismic standards. The retrofit program is largely complete for state bridges, but local agency bridges (owned by cities and counties) have a significant remaining retrofit backlog.

The replacement cost of California's state-owned bridge inventory, the cost to rebuild all 13,100 bridges at current construction costs, is estimated at approximately $60 billion to $80 billion. That figure is relevant not because all bridges need replacement but because it represents the value of the asset that ongoing maintenance must preserve. At current funding levels, the state allocates approximately $2 billion to $3 billion per year for bridge repair and replacement, a rate that addresses the most urgent deficiencies but does not keep pace with the overall aging of the inventory.

## The Gas Tax Paradox

California's transportation funding model is structurally undermined by the state's climate and vehicle policies. The gas tax generates revenue per gallon consumed. California's Zero Emission Vehicle mandate requires that all new vehicles sold in the state be zero-emission by 2035. As the vehicle fleet electrifies, gas tax revenue per VMT will decline toward zero. An electric vehicle that drives 12,000 miles per year on California roads pays no gas tax, no diesel tax, and only a modest registration surcharge ($100 per year for ZEVs, established by SB 1). The same miles driven in a gasoline vehicle generate approximately $180 to $240 per year in gas tax revenue.

The transition is already underway. Electric vehicles represent approximately 25 percent of new vehicle sales in California and approximately 8 percent of the registered fleet. The revenue impact is still modest: the vast majority of VMT is still generated by gasoline and diesel vehicles. But the trajectory is clear. By 2035, when the ZEV mandate takes full effect, and certainly by 2045, when the current fleet will have substantially turned over, gas tax revenue will be a fraction of its current level while the roads and bridges the revenue maintains will still require maintenance.

The replacement funding mechanism does not yet exist. A road user charge, also known as a vehicle miles traveled (VMT) fee, is the most commonly discussed alternative. Caltrans and the California Transportation Commission have conducted pilot programs testing per-mile fees, but no legislation has been enacted to implement a VMT fee at scale. The political obstacles are substantial: a VMT fee requires vehicle tracking technology that raises privacy concerns, imposes higher costs on rural drivers who travel longer distances, and replaces a tax that is invisible (embedded in the pump price) with a fee that is visible (a periodic bill or per-mile charge). Every transportation economist agrees that a VMT fee is the inevitable replacement for the gas tax. No California politician has been willing to enact one.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California built a highway system for the automobile, spent decades expanding and maintaining it, and is now simultaneously trying to reduce automobile dependence (through transit investment, land use reform, and ZEV mandates) while depending on automobile use to fund the infrastructure. The gas tax paradox is a fiscal time bomb: the state is mandating the transition to electric vehicles while funding roads with a tax on gasoline. SB 1 was a patch, not a solution: it increased a declining revenue source rather than replacing it. The $22 billion annual Caltrans budget is large, but the system it maintains was designed for a state of 20 million people driving gasoline vehicles and now serves 40 million people in a state that is electrifying its fleet. The math does not work, and the political system has not produced the replacement revenue model that the math requires.

The generous read is that California's highway system is in better condition than its deferred maintenance narrative suggests. The SB 1 investment has funded thousands of road and bridge repair projects since 2017. The seismic retrofit program has addressed the most critical bridge vulnerabilities. Pavement condition on the state highway system has improved measurably since SB 1 funding began flowing. And the gas tax revenue decline, while real, is a problem for the 2030s and 2040s, not an immediate crisis. The VMT fee pilot programs are building the technical and political foundation for a transition that is years away from necessity. California has time to solve this problem, and the SB 1 model (the legislature raising a regressive tax and surviving a repeal attempt via Proposition 6 in 2018) demonstrated that transportation funding can survive political opposition when the public sees the results in repaired roads and bridges.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

Caltrans publishes extensive data on road and bridge conditions, project status, and spending through the Caltrans Performance Report and the State Highway Operation and Protection Program (SHOPP). It does not publish a comprehensive asset management plan that estimates the total cost to bring all state highway assets to a state of good repair and maintain them there. The deferred maintenance backlog, estimated at $60 billion to $100 billion depending on methodology and scope, is a figure derived from multiple sources and not published as a single Caltrans estimate. The data infrastructure for answering the question "how much would it cost to fix everything?" is itself incomplete.
