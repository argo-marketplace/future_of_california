# California Counts: The Last Free Place
## What 1,130 library branches, 130 million annual visits, $1.7 billion in combined funding, and the highest per-capita circulation in the nation reveal about the public institution California uses the most and funds the least

California operates approximately 1,130 public library branches through 186 library systems, serving a population of 40 million. The branches received approximately 130 million in-person visits in the most recent reporting year and circulated approximately 200 million items (books, audiobooks, e-books, DVDs, and other materials). The per-capita circulation rate, approximately 5 items per resident per year, is among the highest in the nation. California's public libraries are heavily used. They are lightly funded.

Combined public library funding in California is approximately $1.7 billion per year, derived primarily from local government general funds (approximately 80 percent), state funding (less than 5 percent), federal grants (approximately 2 percent), and other sources (donations, fines, endowments, approximately 13 percent). Per-capita library funding is approximately $43, below the national average of approximately $50 and far below states like Ohio ($75), New York ($70), and Colorado ($63). The state's direct contribution to library funding is negligible: the California State Library receives approximately $50 million per year, most of which funds statewide services (interlibrary loan, digital resources, literacy programs) rather than direct grants to local libraries.

## The Mission Creep That Isn't

Public libraries have expanded their missions dramatically over the past two decades, a transformation driven not by bureaucratic empire-building but by the collapse of other public institutions. Libraries now function as de facto social service agencies, providing services that other systems do not: free internet access (approximately 30 percent of library visitors use public computers or Wi-Fi), job search assistance, tax preparation help, citizenship and immigration information, homework help, English language classes, and, in an increasing number of locations, social workers who connect patrons with housing, mental health, and substance use services. The San Francisco Public Library employs a full-time social worker. The Los Angeles Public Library's Source program provides case management for patrons experiencing homelessness. Libraries in dozens of California cities distribute naloxone, provide warming and cooling center services during extreme weather, and serve as emergency information hubs during disasters.

The expansion of services has occurred without a proportional expansion of funding. Librarians, who hold master's degrees and earn median salaries of approximately $70,000 to $85,000 in California, now perform roles that combine information science, social work, education, and community organizing. The paraprofessional staff who constitute the majority of library employees earn $35,000 to $50,000 per year, below the living wage in most California metropolitan areas.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has quietly loaded its public libraries with the functions of a half-dozen other public agencies without funding any of them. Libraries provide internet access because the state has not achieved universal broadband. They provide social work because the county mental health system has three-month wait lists. They provide job training because the workforce development system is fragmented and inaccessible. They provide childcare-adjacent services because the childcare system has a 63 percent supply gap. The libraries do not refuse the mission because they cannot refuse anyone: they are free, open, require no appointment, ask no eligibility questions, and serve whoever walks through the door. The generosity of that mandate is also its vulnerability. A service that serves everyone is a service that no constituency fights for with the ferocity reserved for services that serve specific populations. Libraries have no union of beneficiaries, no lobby, and no political action committee. They have 130 million annual visits and $43 per capita.

The generous read is that public libraries are the most cost-effective public investment in California. For $1.7 billion per year, the state gets 1,130 branches that provide free access to information, technology, education, cultural programming, and community space to every resident regardless of income, immigration status, or need. The American Library Association estimates that public libraries return approximately $4 to $6 in economic value for every $1 invested, through increased educational attainment, workforce readiness, small business support, and avoided costs in social services. The libraries' ability to absorb new missions, internet access, social work, emergency services, reflects institutional adaptability, not institutional failure. Libraries are the most democratic institution in American public life: free to enter, free to use, and available to everyone. In a state where nearly every other public service requires an appointment, an eligibility determination, or a fee, the library asks nothing.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California State Library publishes annual library statistics including circulation, visits, programming attendance, computer use, and revenue by source. It does not publish data on the social service functions that libraries increasingly perform: the number of patrons connected to housing, the number of naloxone doses distributed, the number of tax returns prepared, or the number of crisis interventions conducted by library social workers. These services are tracked locally and inconsistently. The data infrastructure describes libraries as they were (book circulation institutions) rather than as they are (multifunction community service centers).
