# California Counts: The Price of Freedom
## What 46,000 people in pretrial detention, a $0 bail experiment during COVID, a $2 billion commercial bail industry, and a Supreme Court ruling that ability to pay must be considered reveal about the cash bail system California reformed incompletely and then retreated from

On any given day, approximately 46,000 people sit in California county jails awaiting trial. They have not been convicted of a crime. Approximately 75 percent of California's jail population is pretrial, held because they cannot afford bail. The median bail amount in California is approximately $50,000, meaning that the commercial bail bond (10 percent of face value, non-refundable) costs $5,000. For the roughly 40 percent of Americans who cannot cover a $400 emergency expense, $5,000 is an impossible sum. The result is a system in which pretrial freedom is determined primarily by wealth: those who can pay go home, and those who cannot remain in jail for weeks to months awaiting trial, losing jobs, housing, custody of children, and the ability to participate in their own defense.

In 2018, California passed SB 10, which would have eliminated cash bail entirely and replaced it with a risk-assessment-based pretrial release system. The bail industry funded a referendum (Proposition 25, November 2020) to overturn SB 10, and voters rejected the reform 56 to 44 percent. California's cash bail system remained intact. In 2021, the California Supreme Court ruled in In re Humphrey that judges must consider a defendant's ability to pay when setting bail and must consider non-monetary conditions of release (electronic monitoring, check-ins, supervision) before imposing unaffordable money bail. The Humphrey decision did not eliminate cash bail but required individualized affordability assessments, shifting judicial practice in many counties.

## The COVID Experiment

During the COVID-19 pandemic, California implemented emergency $0 bail schedules (April 2020 to June 2022) for most misdemeanors and lower-level felonies, releasing thousands of pretrial detainees to reduce jail populations and prevent viral spread. The Judicial Council's emergency rule reduced the statewide jail population by approximately 20,000 (from approximately 72,000 to approximately 52,000). Research by the Public Policy Institute of California found that the $0 bail policy did not produce a measurable increase in failures to appear in court or new crimes committed by released individuals during the policy's duration. The PPIC study was contested by law enforcement organizations and some district attorneys, who pointed to individual cases of released defendants committing serious offenses.

When the $0 bail order expired in June 2022, jail populations returned to approximately pre-pandemic levels within 12 months. The natural experiment (mass pretrial release without adverse public safety consequences measured in aggregate data) did not translate into permanent policy change. The political dynamics of criminal justice reform in California shifted toward the tougher-on-crime posture that produced Proposition 36 in 2024.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's pretrial detention system incarcerates 46,000 unconvicted people at a cost of approximately $75,000 to $150,000 per person per year (totaling $3.5 billion to $7 billion annually) because they are poor. The Humphrey decision requires ability-to-pay assessments, but compliance varies dramatically by county and individual judge. The $0 bail experiment demonstrated that mass pretrial release does not increase crime rates in aggregate, but the state chose to reinstate wealth-based detention anyway. The commercial bail industry, which generates approximately $2 billion per year in California premiums, successfully defeated the only legislative attempt to eliminate cash bail. The system produces predictable racial disparities: Black defendants are detained pretrial at approximately twice the rate of white defendants charged with comparable offenses.

The generous read is that Humphrey represents genuine, durable reform through the judicial branch after legislative reform failed at the ballot. The ruling requires every judge in California to make individualized determinations about ability to pay and to prefer non-monetary conditions of release for defendants who cannot afford bail. Compliance is imperfect but measurable: pretrial detention rates have declined approximately 10 to 15 percent statewide since Humphrey. Multiple counties (Santa Clara, San Francisco, Contra Costa) have implemented pretrial services programs that provide supervision and support as alternatives to detention. The reform is incremental rather than transformational, but it is constitutional law rather than statute, which means it cannot be overturned by referendum.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Board of State and Community Corrections publishes jail population snapshots. It does not publish a statewide database of individual bail amounts set, bail amounts paid, pretrial detention durations, or outcomes (failure to appear, re-arrest, case disposition) by bail status. The fundamental question of whether pretrial detention produces better case outcomes (lower flight risk, fewer crimes committed while awaiting trial) than supervised release cannot be answered from state-published data because the comparison groups are not systematically tracked.
