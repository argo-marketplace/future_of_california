# California Counts: The Port That Feeds the Machine
## What 20 million container units, $470 billion in cargo value, a 72-hour chassis shortage, and a dockworker making $218,000 reveal about the bottleneck where the global economy meets the American driveway

The ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach sit side by side on San Pedro Bay, separated by a municipal boundary that divides a single economic organism into two competing bureaucracies. Together they constitute the largest port complex in the Western Hemisphere. In 2024, the twin ports handled approximately 20.2 million twenty-foot equivalent units, the standard measure of container volume, representing approximately 31 percent of all containerized cargo entering or leaving the United States. The cargo that passes through San Pedro Bay, measured by value, exceeded $470 billion in 2024: consumer electronics from Shenzhen, auto parts from Nagoya, furniture from Ho Chi Minh City, fast fashion from Dhaka, and the ten thousand other categories of manufactured goods that constitute the material layer of American daily life. One in three containers imported to the United States touches the concrete of San Pedro Bay. The avocado on your counter, the television on your wall, the shirt on your back: the statistical likelihood that each of them passed through these two ports is not trivial. It is the default.

---

## The Geography of Dependency

San Pedro Bay's dominance is geographical. The Pacific Ocean is the shortest route between the manufacturing economies of East and Southeast Asia and the consuming economy of the United States. The Panama Canal, the alternative for reaching East Coast ports, adds 7 to 14 days of transit time and imposes vessel size limitations that exclude the largest container ships. The Suez Canal route to the East Coast is even longer. For time-sensitive, high-value cargo, the math is straightforward: Los Angeles and Long Beach are five to seven days closer to Shanghai, Busan, and Kaohsiung than any East Coast or Gulf port.

The ports' hinterland, the domestic territory they serve, extends far beyond Southern California. The Alameda Corridor, a 20-mile dedicated freight rail line completed in 2002 at a cost of $2.4 billion, connects the ports to the transcontinental rail network. From there, intermodal trains operated by BNSF and Union Pacific carry containers to distribution centers in Chicago, Dallas, Memphis, Kansas City, and points throughout the interior. A container unloaded in Long Beach on Monday can be on a warehouse loading dock in Chicago by Thursday. The ports are not a California asset. They are a national chokepoint.

The concentration creates vulnerability. When the ports slow down, the economy notices. When they stop, the economy seizes. The 2002 West Coast port lockout, a labor dispute that shut the ports for 10 days, cost the national economy an estimated $1 billion per day. The 2014-15 slowdown during ILWU contract negotiations caused weeks of vessel queuing, with dozens of ships anchored offshore waiting to berth. And the 2021 supply chain crisis, when the pandemic-era surge in consumer goods imports collided with labor shortages, truck chassis scarcity, and warehouse saturation, produced a queue of over 100 container ships anchored off the coast, visible from shore, each carrying thousands of containers that could not be unloaded because there was nowhere to put them.

## The 2021 Crisis: What Broke

The supply chain crisis of 2021-22 was not a port failure. It was a system failure in which the ports were the most visible symptom. American consumers, confined to homes during the pandemic, redirected spending from services (restaurants, travel, entertainment) to goods (home electronics, furniture, exercise equipment, home improvement). Import volume at the San Pedro Bay ports surged by approximately 16 percent in 2021 over pre-pandemic levels, a spike that overwhelmed a logistics system designed for predictable, incremental growth.

The ports themselves could unload ships at roughly normal speed. The bottleneck was downstream. Truck chassis, the wheeled frames that carry containers between the port and warehouses, were in short supply because containers were dwelling at warehouses longer than usual, tying up the chassis that would otherwise cycle back to the port. Warehouse space in the Inland Empire, the vast logistics zone east of Los Angeles that houses more than one billion square feet of distribution center capacity, was at effectively zero vacancy. Trucking companies could not hire enough drivers because the pandemic had reduced the driver workforce through illness, retirement, and competition from other sectors. Railroads were short of crew and equipment.

The result was a 72-hour average container dwell time at the ports, roughly triple the normal 24 to 36 hours, which meant that berths were occupied longer, which meant ships waited longer to dock, which meant the queue grew. At its peak in January 2022, 109 container ships were anchored or drifting off the Southern California coast, each paying $50,000 to $100,000 per day in demurrage and delay costs. The total cost of the supply chain disruption to the U.S. economy was estimated by various analysts at $200 billion to $400 billion in 2021 alone, through a combination of delayed deliveries, higher shipping rates, inventory shortages, and consumer price increases.

President Biden appointed a supply chain envoy. The ports implemented 24/7 gate operations. The Federal Maritime Commission investigated. Container shipping rates from Shanghai to Los Angeles, which had historically averaged $1,500 to $2,500 per forty-foot container, spiked to $15,000 to $20,000 during the crisis. They have since returned to approximately $2,000 to $4,000, but the memory of the crisis reshaped the logistics strategies of every major retailer and manufacturer in the country: more inventory, more diversification, more nearshoring, and a willingness to pay for resilience that did not exist before 2021.

## The Labor Question: ILWU and the $218,000 Dockworker

The International Longshore and Warehouse Union represents approximately 15,000 dockworkers at the West Coast ports, including roughly 6,500 at the San Pedro Bay complex. The ILWU's West Coast contract with the Pacific Maritime Association, the employer group representing terminal operators and shipping lines, is the single most consequential labor agreement in American commerce. When the contract expires and negotiations stall, the ports slow or stop, and the national economy feels it within days.

ILWU dockworkers are among the highest-paid blue-collar workers in the United States. The average annual compensation for a full-time registered longshore worker at the Los Angeles and Long Beach ports is approximately $195,000 to $218,000, including base wages, overtime, benefits, and pension contributions. Base hourly wages for experienced workers are approximately $54 per hour, with overtime rates of $81 and holiday rates of $108. The compensation reflects the union's bargaining power, the physical demands and hazards of the work (dockwork has among the highest injury rates of any occupation), and the economic leverage the workers hold: every hour the ports are idle costs the national economy millions.

The 2023 contract negotiations between the ILWU and PMA were the most closely watched labor talks in the country. The previous contract expired in July 2022, and workers continued operating without a contract for over a year while negotiations proceeded. The eventual six-year agreement, ratified in September 2023, included a 32 percent wage increase over the contract term, maintained existing health and pension benefits, and addressed the most contentious issue: automation.

Automation is the existential question for the ILWU. Automated container terminals, which use robotic cranes, automated guided vehicles, and remote-operated equipment to move containers with minimal human labor, have been deployed at ports worldwide: Rotterdam, Shanghai, Qingdao, and Abu Dhabi all operate highly automated terminals. At San Pedro Bay, the TraPac terminal in Los Angeles and the Long Beach Container Terminal are partially automated. The ILWU has historically resisted automation, viewing it as a direct threat to membership. The 2023 contract included provisions for joint labor-management review of new technology and protections for existing workers, but did not prohibit automation outright. The tension between the ports' need to increase throughput and the union's need to protect jobs is the defining labor conflict in American logistics.

## The Pollution Equation

The ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach are the single largest fixed source of air pollution in Southern California. The diesel trucks, locomotives, cargo-handling equipment, and ocean-going vessels that move containers generate nitrogen oxides, particulate matter, sulfur dioxide, and volatile organic compounds at concentrations that make the surrounding communities, Wilmington, San Pedro, Carson, and West Long Beach, among the most polluted neighborhoods in the state.

The communities adjacent to the ports are predominantly low-income, Latino, and Black. The CalEnviroScreen tool, which measures cumulative environmental burden by census tract, ranks the neighborhoods surrounding San Pedro Bay in the top 5 to 10 percent statewide for pollution exposure. Residents experience elevated rates of asthma, cardiovascular disease, and cancer relative to the county average. A 2023 study by the South Coast Air Quality Management District attributed approximately 1,300 premature deaths per year in the South Coast Air Basin to port-related pollution.

The ports have invested significantly in emission reduction through the San Pedro Bay Ports Clean Air Action Plan, first adopted in 2006 and updated multiple times. The plan has achieved substantial reductions: diesel particulate matter from port-related sources declined by approximately 90 percent between 2005 and 2023. Sulfur oxide emissions declined by 97 percent, largely due to international vessel fuel standards. Nitrogen oxide emissions declined by approximately 62 percent.

The remaining challenge is the transition to zero-emission equipment. The California Air Resources Board has mandated that all new drayage trucks (the short-haul trucks that carry containers between the port and local warehouses or rail yards) be zero-emission by 2035, and that all trucks serving the ports be zero-emission by 2040. The ports have adopted a goal of zero-emission cargo handling equipment by 2030. Meeting these targets requires replacing approximately 35,000 drayage trucks, thousands of pieces of cargo-handling equipment, and the electrical infrastructure to charge them, at a combined cost estimated at $10 billion to $15 billion. The trucks alone are the sticking point: a zero-emission Class 8 truck costs approximately $400,000 to $500,000, roughly three times a comparable diesel truck, and the charging infrastructure for a fleet of 35,000 electric trucks does not yet exist.

The environmental justice question is stark. The communities bearing the health burden of port pollution are not the communities that benefit from the cargo passing through. The beneficiaries are the consumers and businesses throughout the country who receive goods at lower cost because of the ports' efficiency. The costs, measured in asthma rates, cancer incidence, and premature death, are borne by the 300,000 people who live within the ports' pollution footprint. The Clean Air Action Plan is narrowing this disparity, but the zero-emission transition timeline extends to 2040, meaning another 15 years of diesel exposure for communities that have already endured decades of it.

## The Competition: Who Wants California's Cargo

San Pedro Bay's 31 percent market share is not guaranteed. East Coast and Gulf Coast ports have invested billions in infrastructure to capture trans-Pacific cargo rerouted through the expanded Panama Canal, which since 2016 can accommodate vessels carrying up to 14,000 TEUs (compared to 5,000 under the old locks). The Port of Savannah has expanded its container capacity and is now the third-largest port in the country. The Port of Houston has grown rapidly, fueled by proximity to distribution centers serving the southern and central United States. The Port of Virginia has deepened its channels to accommodate the largest vessels.

The ILWU's labor actions have accelerated this diversification. During the 2014-15 slowdown and again during the 2022 contract uncertainty, major importers including Walmart, Home Depot, and Amazon shifted container volume to East Coast and Gulf Coast ports served by the International Longshoremen's Association, a separate union with its own contract dynamics. Some of that volume has not returned. San Pedro Bay's market share has declined from approximately 37 percent of U.S. containerized imports in 2005 to 31 percent in 2024, a gradual erosion driven by East Coast port investment, Panama Canal expansion, and shipper diversification strategies.

The ports are responding with their own capital programs. The Port of Los Angeles has a $2.6 billion capital improvement plan. The Port of Long Beach completed its $1.5 billion Middle Harbor terminal redevelopment, creating one of the most modern container terminals in the world. Both ports are deepening berths to accommodate the largest vessels. But the competitive dynamic has permanently changed. Before 2021, many importers used San Pedro Bay as their default gateway. After 2021, most major importers maintain active routing through multiple ports, treating concentration risk as a supply chain vulnerability to be managed rather than a cost saving to be optimized.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has allowed the nation's most critical trade chokepoint to operate as two competing municipal agencies with separate governance, separate labor relations, separate capital plans, and no unified management. The ports' combined throughput of 20 million TEUs passes through a pollution corridor that kills 1,300 people per year in the surrounding communities, communities that are overwhelmingly poor and nonwhite. The ILWU's monopoly on West Coast port labor gives 15,000 workers the power to slow the national economy, a power they have exercised or threatened in every contract cycle. Dockworker compensation of $218,000 per year is paid for by every American consumer through higher goods prices, while the communities adjacent to the ports pay in lung function and life expectancy. The 2021 crisis revealed that the entire national import system depends on a 20-mile stretch of California coastline with no redundancy, no unified command, and no plan for what happens when it fails. The ports have been losing market share for two decades and responding with capital projects that address yesterday's bottleneck rather than tomorrow's.

The generous read is that the San Pedro Bay port complex is one of the great infrastructure achievements of the 20th century and has adapted more effectively than its critics acknowledge. Throughput has nearly doubled since 2000 despite essentially the same footprint, through operational improvements, technology investment, and terminal modernization. The Clean Air Action Plan has achieved a 90 percent reduction in diesel particulate matter, the most aggressive port emission reduction program in the world. The ILWU's compensation reflects the genuine economic value the workers produce: $470 billion in cargo moved through the ports in a year. The 2021 crisis was resolved without a single container being lost, and the recovery produced structural improvements, 24/7 operations, chassis management reforms, and shipper diversification, that have made the system more resilient. The ports compete with each other on service quality, which benefits shippers, and cooperate on environmental standards, which benefits communities. The model is imperfect. It also moves one in three containers that enter the United States, at lower cost than any alternative, through a complex that occupies less than 10,000 acres of coastal land. Name another piece of California infrastructure that delivers more economic value per acre.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The ports publish container volume data monthly, trade value data annually, and air quality monitoring data continuously. What they do not publish, and what no public source aggregates, is the end-to-end cost of moving a container from a factory in Asia to a store shelf in America. The ocean freight rate is public. The terminal handling charge is semi-public. The drayage cost, rail cost, warehouse cost, and last-mile delivery cost are private, negotiated between individual shippers and service providers. The total logistics cost of delivering an imported product to an American consumer, of which port costs are a fraction, is not tracked by any public agency. Estimates suggest that the total landed cost of a container from Shanghai to a Midwest distribution center is approximately $4,000 to $8,000, of which port-related costs (terminal handling, drayage, chassis, demurrage) represent roughly 15 to 25 percent.

The data does not capture the informal economy of the ports. The drayage trucking industry, which moves containers between the port and local destinations, is dominated by independent owner-operators, many of them immigrants, who own or lease their trucks and are classified as independent contractors rather than employees. AB 5, the 2019 law that restricted independent contractor classification, and Proposition 22, the 2020 initiative that exempted app-based drivers, did not directly address port trucking, but the broader classification debate has implications for the approximately 12,000 drayage truckers at San Pedro Bay. Many earn $40,000 to $60,000 per year after expenses, a fraction of the ILWU dockworkers they interact with daily. The contrast between the unionized dockworker earning $218,000 and the independent trucker earning $50,000, both handling the same container, is visible at the port gate every morning.

The data also does not capture what happens if the ports fail permanently rather than temporarily. The 2021 crisis lasted months. An earthquake on the Palos Verdes fault, which runs beneath San Pedro Bay, could shut the ports for years. The USGS estimates a 31 percent probability of a magnitude 6.7 or greater earthquake on the Palos Verdes fault in the next 30 years. The liquefaction risk in the landfill on which much of the port infrastructure sits is significant. No public estimate exists for the national economic cost of a multi-year closure of San Pedro Bay. The closest analogy, the 2011 Tohoku earthquake's effect on Japanese ports, suggests GDP impacts measured in percentage points. For an economy that imports 31 percent of its containerized goods through one complex, the scenario is not academic. It is actuarial.

---

Every container that crosses San Pedro Bay carries two invoices. The first is the bill of lading: what is in the box, who sent it, who receives it, what it costs. The second is the externality ledger: the diesel particulate settling into the lungs of a child in Wilmington, the wage premium paid to the dockworker who unloads it, the carbon emitted by the vessel that carried it, the earthquake risk embedded in the landfill beneath the crane. The first invoice is paid by the importer. The second is paid by the neighbors. Twenty million containers per year, $470 billion in cargo, 31 percent of the nation's imports, all passing through a chokepoint smaller than a mid-sized cattle ranch, governed by two cities that cannot agree on how to count the same ships, in a pollution corridor where the median household income is $45,000 and the median dockworker makes nearly five times that. The port complex is the most productive piece of infrastructure in California. It is also the most honest illustration of how the state distributes the costs and benefits of prosperity: the goods flow inland, the profits flow upward, and the particulate matter settles where it always has.
