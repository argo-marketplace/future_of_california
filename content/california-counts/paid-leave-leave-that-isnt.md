# California Counts: The Leave That Isn't
## What a 60 percent wage replacement, $8.4 billion in annual Paid Family Leave claims, a 40 percent take-up rate, and no job protection for workers at small employers reveal about the family leave California pioneered and never finished building

California was the first state in the nation to establish a Paid Family Leave (PFL) program, enacted in 2002 and paying benefits since 2004. The program provides up to eight weeks of partial wage replacement (60 to 70 percent of weekly wages, capped at approximately $1,620 per week) to workers who take time off to bond with a new child, care for a seriously ill family member, or assist with a family member's military deployment. The program is funded entirely by employee payroll deductions through the State Disability Insurance (SDI) system at a rate of approximately 1.1 percent of wages up to a taxable wage limit of approximately $153,000. No employer contribution is required. The EDD administers claims, and the program paid approximately $8.4 billion in benefits to approximately 550,000 claimants in the most recent fiscal year.

The program was groundbreaking when created. Two decades later, its limitations have become its defining features. The wage replacement rate of 60 to 70 percent means that a worker earning $60,000 per year who takes eight weeks of PFL receives approximately $5,500 in benefits instead of the $9,200 they would have earned at full pay, a gap of approximately $3,700 that many low-income workers cannot absorb. The take-up rate, the percentage of eligible workers who actually use PFL, is approximately 35 to 40 percent, with the lowest rates among low-income workers, workers of color, and workers in industries (agriculture, food service, retail) where the cultural expectation is that employees do not take leave.

## The Job Protection Gap

The most significant limitation of PFL is the absence of universal job protection. The California Family Rights Act (CFRA) provides job protection (the right to return to the same or equivalent position after leave) to employees of employers with five or more employees. But PFL itself does not provide job protection; it provides only wage replacement. A worker at an employer with fewer than five employees can receive PFL benefits but has no legal right to return to their job after the leave ends. The employer can fill the position while the worker is on leave, and the worker has no recourse.

Even for workers at larger employers who have CFRA job protection, the interaction between PFL (eight weeks of partial wage replacement) and CFRA (12 weeks of job-protected leave) creates complexity. A new parent who takes 12 weeks of CFRA-protected leave receives PFL wage replacement for only eight of those weeks. The remaining four weeks are unpaid unless the employer provides paid leave benefits, which most low-wage employers do not. The result is a leave structure that provides partial wage replacement for part of the leave and no wage replacement for the rest, a framework that the workers who need leave most, low-wage workers in physically demanding jobs, are least able to afford.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's Paid Family Leave program is a pioneer that has been surpassed by the programs it inspired. New York (12 weeks at 67 percent wage replacement), New Jersey (12 weeks at 85 percent), and Washington (12 weeks at 90 percent for low-wage workers) all provide longer leave at higher wage replacement. California's eight weeks at 60 to 70 percent is the shortest duration and lowest replacement rate among the states that have adopted paid family leave. The 40 percent take-up rate reveals the gap between program availability and program accessibility: the workers who most need paid leave, those in low-wage jobs with limited financial reserves and employer cultures hostile to leave-taking, are the least likely to use it. California invented paid family leave and then stopped improving it while other states built better versions.

The generous read is that California's PFL program has provided $8.4 billion in wage replacement benefits to 550,000 workers in a single year, enabling hundreds of thousands of new parents to bond with their children and hundreds of thousands of family caregivers to care for ill relatives without total income loss. The program has been shown in peer-reviewed research to increase breastfeeding duration, reduce infant hospitalizations, and improve maternal mental health. The expansions that have occurred, increasing the duration from six to eight weeks, raising the wage replacement from 55 to 70 percent for low-wage workers, expanding the definition of family member, demonstrate a trajectory of improvement. The program is not finished. It is the foundation on which a more comprehensive leave policy can be built.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The EDD publishes PFL claims data by county, gender, claim type, and benefit amount. It does not publish take-up rate data by industry, employer size, or worker demographics, which would identify the specific populations and workplaces where PFL is underutilized. The reasons for non-use, whether workers do not know about the program, cannot afford the wage reduction, fear employer retaliation, or are ineligible, are studied through surveys rather than measured through administrative data.
