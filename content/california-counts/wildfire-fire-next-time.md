# California Counts: The Fire Next Time
## What $117.4 billion in losses, 4.2 million acres burned in a single year, 14 million residents in the wildland-urban interface, and a 54,000-person inmate firefighting program reveal about the state that builds where it burns

In August 2020, a series of dry lightning storms ignited more than 900 fires across Northern and Central California in 72 hours. By September, the state had burned 4.2 million acres, the most in recorded modern history, an area larger than Connecticut. The sky over San Francisco turned orange at midday. The smoke plume was visible from space. Five of the six largest fires in California's recorded history occurred in 2020 and 2021. The total insured and uninsured losses from California wildfires between 2017 and 2025 exceeded $150 billion by most estimates, with the 2017-2021 period alone accounting for $117.4 billion according to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's adjusted disaster cost database. The January 2025 Palisades and Eaton fires in Los Angeles County added an estimated $250 billion to $275 billion in combined losses, potentially the costliest natural disaster in American history. California does not have a wildfire problem. California has a land-use problem, an insurance problem, a climate problem, and a vegetation management problem that collectively express themselves as fire.

---

## The Budget: What CalFIRE Costs

The California Department of Forestry and Fire Protection, known as CalFIRE, is the largest fire agency in the nation. Its budget for fiscal year 2025-26 is approximately $5.1 billion, more than tripling from $1.7 billion a decade earlier. CalFIRE employs approximately 12,000 permanent and seasonal personnel and operates 233 fire stations, 58 fire crews, 58 conservation camps, 11 air attack bases, and a fleet of fixed-wing aircraft and helicopters that constitutes the largest civil aerial firefighting force in the world. CalFIRE's jurisdiction covers State Responsibility Areas, the roughly 31 million acres of private and state-owned wildlands where the state, rather than federal or local agencies, has primary fire protection responsibility.

The budget growth reflects both increasing fire severity and expanding scope. Emergency fire suppression costs, which are inherently unpredictable, consumed $2.6 billion in fiscal year 2020-21, a record driven by the August Complex, the SCU Lightning Complex, and the Dixie Fire. The state maintains an emergency fund for suppression costs that exceed the base appropriation, and that fund has been drawn down in every recent fire year. In addition to suppression, CalFIRE's budget includes approximately $1.2 billion for fire prevention and forest health programs: fuels reduction, prescribed burning, forest thinning, defensible space inspections, and community fire planning.

The federal contribution is substantial but operates in parallel rather than integration. The U.S. Forest Service manages 20 million acres of national forest land in California, roughly one-fifth of the state's total area. Federal fire suppression spending in California has averaged $1 billion to $2 billion per year over the past decade. Total public spending on wildfire in California, combining state, federal, and local expenditures, exceeds $7 billion per year before accounting for disaster recovery costs. The combined firefighting budget of CalFIRE and the Forest Service's California operations exceeds the total military budget of most nations.

## The WUI: 14 Million People in the Fire Zone

The wildland-urban interface, the zone where human development meets wildland vegetation, is where wildfire becomes catastrophe. California has approximately 4.5 million housing units in the WUI, sheltering roughly 14 million residents, more than any other state. The WUI has expanded continuously since the 1950s as subdivisions pushed into foothills, canyons, and forested ridgelines throughout Southern California, the Sierra foothills, the East Bay hills, and the Santa Cruz Mountains.

The expansion was enabled by the same forces that drove California's suburban growth: cheap land at the urban edge, automobile-dependent development, local zoning that permitted low-density construction in fire-prone terrain, and a homeowner's insurance market that until recently did not price wildfire risk accurately. Communities like Paradise (destroyed by the Camp Fire in 2018, with 85 deaths and 18,804 structures lost), Santa Rosa's Coffey Park (Tubbs Fire, 2017, 22 deaths), and the Pacific Palisades and Altadena neighborhoods (January 2025, at least 29 deaths) were built in landscapes that have burned repeatedly throughout recorded and archaeological history.

Building codes have tightened significantly since 2008, when Chapter 7A of the California Building Code imposed fire-resistant construction requirements for new buildings in fire hazard severity zones: non-combustible roofing, enclosed eaves, tempered glass, fire-resistant exterior walls, and ember-resistant vents. The code applies only to new construction or substantial remodels. The existing housing stock, the 4 million homes already in the WUI, predates these requirements. Retrofitting existing homes is the responsibility of individual homeowners. Compliance with defensible space requirements (clearing vegetation within 100 feet of structures, as mandated by Public Resources Code 4291) is enforced by CalFIRE inspectors, but inspection capacity covers only a fraction of the roughly 2 million parcels in State Responsibility Areas annually.

AB 38 (2019) created a disclosure requirement for homes in fire hazard zones and established a framework for defensible space inspections upon property sale. AB 3074 (2020) expanded the defensible space zone to include an "ember-resistant zone" within five feet of structures. The regulations exist. The enforcement capacity does not match the scale of the problem: 4.5 million homes, 14 million residents, and an inspection force that can reach perhaps 200,000 parcels per year.

## Prescribed Fire: The Treatment Gap

California's forests evolved with fire. Indigenous peoples burned deliberately for thousands of years to manage vegetation, improve hunting grounds, and reduce fuel loads. European settlement suppressed both Indigenous burning and natural fire, allowing fuel to accumulate in forests and woodlands that were historically maintained by regular, low-intensity fire. A century of fire suppression has produced forests with fuel loads four to ten times their historical density, creating the conditions for the megafires that now characterize California's fire seasons.

The remedy is prescribed fire and mechanical fuel treatment: intentionally burning or thinning vegetation to reduce fuel loads before wildfire arrives. Governor Newsom's Wildfire and Forest Resilience Action Plan, announced in 2021, set a target of treating 500,000 acres per year by 2025, combining state, federal, and private efforts. The target is ambitious. In 2023, CalFIRE and its partners treated approximately 380,000 acres through a combination of prescribed burning, mechanical thinning, and hand crews. The shortfall reflects chronic constraints: air quality regulations limit burn windows, liability exposure deters private landowners from burning, permitting for prescribed burns requires coordination across air districts and fire agencies, and the workforce for conducting burns safely is limited.

The U.S. Forest Service faces parallel challenges on federal land. The agency has increased prescribed burning in California from approximately 20,000 acres per year in the 2010s to roughly 50,000 to 70,000 acres in recent years, but the backlog remains enormous. Estimates of the total acreage needing treatment across California's fire-prone landscape range from 10 million to 20 million acres. At 380,000 acres per year, fully treating the state's fire-prone landscape would take 26 to 53 years, during which additional fuel would accumulate in areas not yet treated. The math of prescribed fire operates on a timeline that fire does not respect.

The prescribed fire dilemma encapsulates a broader pattern: the activities that prevent catastrophic wildfire (prescribed burns, mechanical thinning, strategic grazing, Indigenous cultural burning) are small, distributed, unglamorous, and require sustained funding over decades. The activities that respond to catastrophic wildfire (air tankers, hotshot crews, emergency evacuations, disaster relief) are dramatic, concentrated, heavily funded, and politically rewarding. California spends approximately four dollars on fire suppression for every one dollar on fire prevention. The ratio has improved in recent years but remains inverted relative to what fire science recommends.

## The Inmate Firefighters

California's Conservation Camp program deploys approximately 1,600 to 2,000 incarcerated individuals to wildfire lines each fire season, organized into hand crews that cut firebreaks, clear brush, and conduct mop-up operations alongside CalFIRE professional crews. At its peak before criminal justice reforms reduced the prison population, the program fielded more than 4,000 inmate firefighters. The program currently operates approximately 35 camps jointly administered by CalFIRE and the California Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation.

Inmate firefighters earn between $5.80 and $10.24 per day during non-emergency work, plus an additional $1 per hour when assigned to active fire lines. During a multi-day fire deployment, an inmate firefighter might earn $50 to $100 for work that a CalFIRE firefighter is compensated hundreds of dollars per day to perform. The program saves the state an estimated $100 million to $200 million per year in labor costs by substituting inmate labor for what would otherwise require paid firefighters or contractors.

AB 2147 (2020) created a pathway for formerly incarcerated firefighters to have their records expunged, enabling them to apply for professional firefighter positions that their criminal records had previously barred them from. Before AB 2147, the paradox was complete: the state trained inmates to fight fires, relied on them during emergencies, and then prevented them from doing the same work as free citizens. The law has enabled several hundred formerly incarcerated firefighters to pursue professional careers, though the exact number is not publicly tracked.

The program's defenders point to its voluntary nature (inmates must apply and meet physical fitness standards), its rehabilitative value (lower recidivism rates for participants), and its practical necessity (CalFIRE cannot staff its fire lines without the program during major fire events). Critics describe it as the exploitation of captive labor at wages that would violate minimum wage laws for any free worker, performing some of the most dangerous work in the state, for an institution that profits from their incarceration. Both descriptions are accurate. They describe the same program.

## The 2025 Los Angeles Fires: A New Category

The Palisades and Eaton fires of January 2025 rewrote the catastrophic fire playbook. Driven by Santa Ana winds exceeding 80 miles per hour into dense urban neighborhoods, the fires destroyed more than 16,000 structures and killed at least 29 people in one of the most expensive zip codes on Earth. The Palisades fire burned through Pacific Palisades, Malibu, and surrounding communities. The Eaton fire devastated Altadena and parts of Pasadena. Combined insured losses were estimated at $35 billion to $40 billion. Total economic losses, including uninsured property, business interruption, infrastructure damage, debris removal, and long-term displacement, were projected at $250 billion to $275 billion.

The fires exposed several systemic failures. The Los Angeles Department of Water and Power's water system lost pressure during the fires as hydrants ran dry in hillside neighborhoods, a failure the department attributed to unprecedented simultaneous demand exceeding the system's design capacity. Multiple fire hydrants in the Palisades area were inoperative. The city's fire department was stretched across multiple simultaneous fires, with mutual aid from dozens of agencies unable to prevent structure losses in wind-driven conditions that made aerial firefighting impossible.

The fires also revealed the consequence of building $3 million to $10 million homes in a landscape that burns. The Pacific Palisades neighborhood sits in a coastal canyon surrounded by chaparral that has burned in 1938, 1961, 1978, and 2025. The Santa Ana wind corridor that channeled the fire through the community is a known, mapped, and predictable meteorological phenomenon. The homes were built there anyway because the ocean views are worth millions and the fire interval was long enough to forget. The January 2025 fires did not surprise fire scientists. They surprised homeowners, which is a different kind of failure.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has spent a century building in terrain that burns, suppressing the fires that would have kept the fuel in check, and then expressing surprise when the inevitable occurs. The state has 14 million people in the WUI, 4.5 million homes built before modern fire codes, an inspection capacity that reaches a fraction of the parcels that need it, and a prescribed fire program treating one-third of the annual target. CalFIRE's $5.1 billion budget is the cost of managing the consequences of land-use decisions the state continues to permit: new subdivisions in fire hazard zones are still approved by local governments because property tax revenue and developer fees are more immediate than fire risk. The inmate firefighter program saves $200 million per year by paying imprisoned workers $5.80 a day. The 2025 LA fires destroyed $250 billion in wealth in neighborhoods where the fire history was public record. The state knows where the fires will occur. It allows people to build there. It cannot protect them when the fires arrive. And it bills the result to a homeowner's insurance system that is collapsing under the weight of claims the premiums never anticipated.

The generous read is that California has mounted the most aggressive wildfire response and prevention program of any jurisdiction on Earth. CalFIRE's aerial fleet, professional crews, and mutual aid system have saved thousands of lives and tens of thousands of structures in fire after fire. The 2020 fire season burned 4.2 million acres but killed 33 people, a fatality rate far below what comparable acreage would have produced without the evacuation systems, alert networks, and fire response that CalFIRE coordinates. The 500,000-acre annual treatment target, even if not yet fully met, represents a tripling of prevention investment in five years. Building code Chapter 7A has been shown to reduce structure loss by 40 to 60 percent in WUI fires for homes built to its standards. The AB 2147 pathway for formerly incarcerated firefighters is a genuine reform. And the prescribed fire movement, including the growing recognition of Indigenous cultural burning as both ecologically necessary and culturally significant, represents a paradigm shift from suppression to coexistence with fire that is more advanced in California than anywhere else in the country.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CalFIRE publishes fire perimeter data, structure loss counts, suppression costs, and acres treated through prevention programs. What it does not publish in an integrated format is a cost-benefit analysis of prevention versus suppression: how many structures were saved by fuels treatment in a given fire, how many acres of treated land performed as designed when fire arrived, and what the return on prevention investment was relative to suppression spending. Individual studies have demonstrated that fuels treatment reduces fire severity and structure loss, but the state does not systematically track treatment effectiveness across its portfolio.

The data does not capture the health effects of smoke exposure, which extend far beyond the fire perimeter. Wildfire smoke produces PM2.5 particulate matter that travels hundreds of miles and affects millions of people who are nowhere near the fire. A 2021 study by Stanford researchers estimated that wildfire smoke exposure causes 1,200 to 3,000 excess deaths per year in California, a toll that dwarfs direct fire fatalities. The health costs of smoke exposure, including emergency department visits, hospitalizations, lost productivity, and premature death, are estimated at $10 billion to $50 billion per year but are not attributed to specific fires in CalFIRE's accounting.

The data also does not capture the feedback loop between wildfire and climate change. California's wildfires release tens of millions of metric tons of carbon dioxide annually, quantities that in severe fire years rival the state's total fossil fuel emissions. The 2020 fire season released an estimated 112 million metric tons of CO2, roughly equivalent to the annual emissions of the entire state's transportation sector. These emissions are not counted in the state's greenhouse gas inventory under current accounting rules because they are classified as biogenic. The carbon released by wildfire is, under current policy, invisible to the state's climate targets, even as those fires are intensified by the climate change the targets are meant to address.

---

California burns because California has always burned. The archaeological record shows fire return intervals of 5 to 30 years in most of the state's grassland, chaparral, and mixed-conifer ecosystems. Fire is not an anomaly in this landscape. It is the landscape. What is anomalous is the 14 million people living in the fire zone, the century of fuel accumulation from suppression, the 4.5 million homes built before anyone required a fire-resistant roof, and the insurance system that priced premiums as though fire were an aberration rather than an inevitability. The state has the best firefighting force on the planet. It has the largest aerial fleet, the most experienced crews, and the most advanced evacuation systems. And it has discovered, at a cost now measured in hundreds of billions, that no amount of suppression capacity can substitute for a land-use policy that treats fire as what it is: not a disaster to be prevented, but a process to be managed, in a landscape that will burn whether or not anyone builds a house on it.
