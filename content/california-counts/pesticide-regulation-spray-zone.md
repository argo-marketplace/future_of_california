# California Counts: The Spray Zone
## What 200 million pounds of pesticides applied per year, the most comprehensive pesticide use database in the world, a 2,500-school buffer zone debate, and the farmworker health effects that are documented but not prevented reveal about the chemical regulation California leads on and lives with

California's agricultural industry applies approximately 200 million pounds of pesticide active ingredients per year, the most of any state. The California Department of Pesticide Regulation (DPR) maintains the Pesticide Use Report (PUR) database, which records every commercial pesticide application in the state: the product, the amount, the location (section, township, and range), the crop, and the date. The PUR is the most detailed pesticide use database in the world; no other jurisdiction requires reporting at this level of specificity. The database contains approximately 60 years of data and billions of individual application records.

DPR regulates pesticide registration (deciding which products may be sold), use (how products may be applied, by whom, and under what conditions), and enforcement (investigating violations and imposing penalties). California's pesticide regulations are more stringent than federal EPA standards in several areas: California requires additional health and environmental review for products registered by the EPA, maintains a list of chemicals known to cause cancer or reproductive harm (Proposition 65) that restricts pesticide use, and imposes application-specific restrictions (buffer zones, weather conditions, notification requirements) that the federal government does not require.

---

## The Exposure Problem

Despite the regulatory framework, pesticide exposure remains a significant health issue for farmworkers and rural communities. Approximately 800,000 California farmworkers are routinely exposed to pesticides through direct application, drift (airborne movement of spray from the application site to adjacent areas), and residue contact (touching treated crops and soil). DPR's Pesticide Illness Surveillance Program reports approximately 500 to 800 confirmed pesticide-related illness cases per year, but this figure is widely acknowledged as a significant undercount: the actual incidence is estimated at 5,000 to 10,000 cases per year, with underreporting driven by fear of employer retaliation, lack of access to healthcare, language barriers, and the difficulty of attributing chronic health effects (cancer, neurological disease, respiratory illness) to pesticide exposure.

The buffer zone debate illustrates the tension. DPR proposed restrictions on pesticide application near schools and daycare centers (approximately 2,500 facilities near agricultural fields), including notification requirements and application timing restrictions. The regulations, finalized in 2017 after years of debate, require notification of school authorities before application within a quarter-mile and restrict certain fumigant applications near schools. Environmental justice advocates argued the regulations were insufficient (they apply to only a subset of pesticides and do not prohibit application near schools). Agricultural interests argued they were unnecessary and costly. The final regulations represent a compromise that neither side considers adequate.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California regulates pesticides more than any other state and still has 200 million pounds of chemicals applied to its fields each year, with documented health effects on the workers who apply them and the communities that breathe the drift. The PUR database is a monument to data collection without commensurate data-driven action: the state knows exactly what is sprayed, where, and when, and the knowledge has not produced the exposure reductions that the health evidence demands. The 500 to 800 confirmed illness cases per year represent the visible fraction of a much larger burden. The chronic effects (cancer, Parkinson's disease, developmental delays in children exposed in utero or in early childhood) emerge over decades and are never attributed to specific applications. California has the world's best pesticide data and the nation's most exposed agricultural workforce.

The generous read is that California's pesticide regulation has produced measurable improvements in safety over time: the phase-out of the most toxic organophosphates, the restriction of fumigants near populated areas, the requirement of closed-cab tractors for certain applications, and the training and certification requirements for applicators. The PUR database enables research that identifies hazards and drives regulatory action: academic studies using PUR data have documented associations between pesticide exposure and health effects that have led to product bans, use restrictions, and buffer zone requirements. California's regulatory approach, regulation and monitoring rather than prohibition, reflects the reality that agriculture requires pest control and the objective is to minimize harm while maintaining food production. The system is imperfect. It is more protective than any comparable system in the world.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

DPR publishes the PUR database and the Pesticide Illness Surveillance Program data. It does not publish comprehensive exposure assessment data (how much pesticide Californians in different regions actually absorb through inhalation, dermal contact, and dietary intake), long-term health outcome data for pesticide-exposed populations, or environmental fate data (what happens to the 200 million pounds after application: how much enters waterways, groundwater, soil, and air, and at what concentrations). The PUR tells the state what was applied. It does not tell the state what the application did.
