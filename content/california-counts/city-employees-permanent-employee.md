# California Counts: The Permanent Employee
## What a 0.5 percent annual for-cause termination rate, 50,000 city workers in Los Angeles, Skelly hearing requirements, civil service commission appeal rights, and a comparison to five other major cities reveal about how rarely American municipal governments fire anyone and why removing legal protections does not change the pattern

The City of Los Angeles employs approximately 50,000 people across more than 40 departments, from the LAPD to the Bureau of Sanitation to the Department of Water and Power. In a typical year, fewer than one in 200 of those employees is terminated for cause. The rate is not unique to Los Angeles. It is the norm across American municipal government, in cities with strong civil service protections and cities without them, in California and in at-will employment states, in large bureaucracies and small ones. The government employee who passes probation has, for practical purposes, a permanent job.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS) provides the cleanest available comparison. The monthly "layoffs and discharges" rate for state and local government runs approximately 0.3 to 0.4 percent per month, compared with approximately 1.0 to 1.2 percent for the private sector. Annualized, state and local government employees are involuntarily separated at roughly one-third to one-quarter the rate of private sector workers. When you isolate for-cause terminations (excluding layoffs), the gap widens further. The Office of Personnel Management's FedScope data, the most granular public workforce dataset in the country, shows that federal employees are removed for cause at a rate of approximately 0.5 percent per year. Private sector for-cause termination rates typically run 1.5 to 3 percent. Government fires at somewhere between one-third and one-sixth the rate of comparable private employers.

## Six Cities, One Pattern

The six cities in this comparison span three states with different employment law regimes, different union densities, and different civil service traditions. They range from a workforce of 50,000 to one of approximately 3,500. The for-cause termination pattern is remarkably consistent.

The California cities share a legal architecture that makes termination formally difficult. The Texas, Utah, and Florida cities operate under at-will employment frameworks that make termination formally easy. The termination rates, to the extent they can be estimated from published workforce data, do not differ as dramatically as the legal frameworks would predict.

## The California Architecture

California municipal employees who have completed their probationary period (typically six to twelve months) are "permanent" civil service employees with constitutional due process protections that originate in the California Supreme Court's 1975 decision in Skelly v. State Personnel Board. The Skelly framework requires that before any serious disciplinary action, an employee must receive written notice of the charges, the right to review the evidence, and the opportunity to respond to a designated hearing officer. This is not a statutory protection that the legislature can revoke; it is a constitutional requirement under the Fourteenth Amendment's due process clause as interpreted by the California courts. Changing it would require a change in constitutional interpretation.

In Los Angeles, the process for terminating a permanent city employee typically involves progressive discipline (verbal counseling, written reprimand, suspension, demotion, and finally termination), a Skelly hearing, a decision by the appointing authority, and then the right to appeal to the City's Civil Service Commission, which can uphold, modify, or reverse the termination. For LAPD officers, terminations go through a Board of Rights hearing (two command staff officers and one civilian panelist since Charter Amendment C passed with 70 percent voter approval in March 2019). The full process from initial documentation of problems to final resolution routinely takes one to three years. Employees typically remain on the payroll during proceedings, often on paid administrative leave.

San Francisco's system, established under Article X of the city charter and dating to Progressive Era reforms in the early 1900s, operates similarly. The Civil Service Commission hears appeals, can overturn terminations, and adds months to the timeline. The San Francisco Chronicle's investigation into the difficulty of firing city employees documented cases where the process spanned years. In 2024, Mayor London Breed placed Proposition D on the ballot to streamline the city's hiring and firing processes, a tacit acknowledgment that the existing system was not working as intended.

San Jose, with a leaner city government of approximately 7,000 employees, operates under the same Skelly framework but with somewhat less bureaucratic complexity by virtue of scale. The legal protections are identical; the institutional layers are thinner.

## The At-Will Cities

Austin, Salt Lake City, and Miami operate in states where at-will employment is the default. In theory, an at-will employer can terminate an employee for any reason or no reason, provided the termination does not violate anti-discrimination law. In practice, municipal employees in these cities retain significant protections.

In Austin, police officers and firefighters are covered by Chapter 143 of the Texas Local Government Code, which provides civil service protections including just-cause requirements, progressive discipline, and appeal rights that mirror the California framework in substance if not in name. General city employees are at-will but are covered by city personnel policies that impose procedural requirements, and many are represented by employee associations (Texas prohibits public-sector collective bargaining but permits "meet and confer" agreements). The result is a system that is formally at-will but operationally constrained.

Salt Lake City and Miami present similar patterns. Utah and Florida are at-will states, and Florida's Service First Act of 2001 explicitly converted most state employees from career service to at-will status. But the municipal workforces in these cities still operate under internal personnel policies, union agreements (where applicable), and the practical reality that terminating a public employee generates media coverage, potential litigation, and political consequences in ways that private sector terminations rarely do.

## The Georgia Finding

The most revealing evidence on whether legal protections actually drive the low termination rate comes from natural experiments in states that eliminated civil service protections entirely. In 1996, Georgia Governor Zell Miller abolished civil service protections for all new state employees, making them at-will. Researchers J. Edward Kellough and Lloyd Nigro studied the effect and published their findings in the Review of Public Personnel Administration in 2002. Their conclusion: the reform did not significantly change termination rates. Managers in Georgia's at-will system fired at approximately the same rate as managers in the old civil service system.

Florida's Service First Act of 2001 produced a similar result. After Governor Jeb Bush converted the majority of state workers to at-will status, firing rates did not meaningfully increase.

The explanation, supported by both studies, is that the barriers to termination in government are primarily cultural and institutional, not legal. Public sector managers rarely fire employees because the organizational norms emphasize due process and fairness even when the law does not require it, because public terminations generate scrutiny that private terminations do not, because replacement hiring in government is itself slow and uncertain, and because managers often lack confidence that they can recruit someone better. A 2016 Merit Systems Protection Board survey found that 79 percent of federal supervisors had managed a poorly performing employee in the prior three years. Only 15 percent had initiated formal action. The most commonly cited reason was that the process was "not worth the effort," a judgment that persists whether or not the law requires a formal process.

## The Los Angeles Specifics

Within the City of Los Angeles, certain departments and employee groups have attracted particular scrutiny. The Department of Water and Power (DWP), a semi-autonomous utility with approximately 10,000 employees and its own memorandum of understanding with IBEW Local 18, has been repeatedly cited by the LA City Controller's office and by Los Angeles Times investigations for weak discipline and accountability. Reports have documented DWP employees engaged in time theft and other misconduct who kept their positions or were reinstated after termination through the appeal process.

The LAPD's Board of Rights proceedings illustrate the reinstatement dynamic. Before the 2019 charter reform, the Board of Rights (composed of two command staff officers and one civilian) frequently overturned terminations recommended by the Chief of Police, including cases involving dishonesty, excessive force, and driving under the influence. The 2019 reform (Charter Amendment C) allowed the Chief to opt for an all-civilian panel, an attempt to reduce the rate at which fellow officers reversed discipline. The reform passed with approximately 70 percent voter approval, reflecting public frustration with the reinstatement pattern.

The broader Los Angeles city workforce of approximately 50,000 is represented by multiple unions, including SEIU Local 721 (the largest), IBEW Local 18 (DWP), the Los Angeles Police Protective League, and United Firefighters of Los Angeles City. Each union's contract includes grievance and arbitration provisions that layer additional procedural requirements on top of the civil service and Skelly frameworks. The cumulative effect is a system in which a determined supervisor can terminate a poorly performing employee, but the determination required is substantial: six to eighteen months of documentation, multiple hearings, the risk of reversal on appeal, and the certainty that the process will consume significant management time.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that a system in which fewer than one in 200 employees is terminated for cause annually is a system that has abandoned accountability. The City of Los Angeles spends approximately $12 billion per year, much of it on personnel. A for-cause termination rate of less than 0.5 percent implies either that the city has achieved near-perfect employee performance (which no resident of Los Angeles believes) or that the system has made accountability so costly and uncertain that managers have rationally concluded it is not worth pursuing. The MSPB survey finding that 79 percent of supervisors identified poor performers but only 15 percent acted is the definition of a broken accountability system. The Skelly framework and civil service commission appeals do not merely protect employees from arbitrary termination; they protect employees from termination, full stop. The Georgia and Florida findings are not reassuring; they suggest that even removing the legal protections would not fix the problem because the culture of non-termination is deeper than the rules that sustain it.

The generous read is that civil service protections exist because the alternative was worse. Before the Pendleton Act of 1883 created the federal civil service system, government employment was pure patronage: every change of administration produced mass firings of competent employees and their replacement with political allies. California's civil service system, and Los Angeles's charter-based protections, were explicit responses to the same corruption. The low termination rate reflects not a failure of accountability but a system in which employees are carefully selected through competitive examination, serve a probationary period during which they can be more easily released, and then receive the due process protections that the Constitution requires before the government takes away a property interest. The private sector fires at higher rates in part because it hires less carefully, invests less in training, and treats labor as more disposable. Government's lower termination rate is not a bug; it is the intended consequence of a system designed to build a professional, stable, politically insulated workforce. The LAPD Board of Rights reforms and San Francisco's Proposition D demonstrate that when specific accountability failures are identified, the democratic process can and does produce targeted reforms.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

City-specific for-cause termination data is not published in a standardized, comparable format across American municipalities. The BLS JOLTS provides sector-level data (state and local government as a category) but does not break it down by individual city. Individual cities publish workforce reports with varying levels of detail; some include separation data by type, others do not. The estimated termination rates in this analysis are derived from the best available published data (BLS JOLTS, OPM FedScope, city controller reports, and investigative journalism) but should be treated as approximations rather than precise counts. The fundamental question of whether the low termination rate reflects a well-functioning merit system or an accountability failure cannot be answered by the termination rate alone. It requires outcome data: Are city services delivered effectively? Are residents satisfied? Are taxpayer dollars spent efficiently? Those questions are answered, imperfectly, by every other installment of this series.
