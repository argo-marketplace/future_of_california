# California Counts: The Appointed Bench
## What 700+ appointments in five years, 60 percent female appointees, and a first Latina Chief Justice reveal about one governor's transformation of California's courts

Governors appoint judges. In California, where there are approximately 2,200 state court positions across the Supreme Court, the six Courts of Appeal, and 58 Superior Courts, the accumulation of those appointments over time is how the judiciary changes. Governor Gavin Newsom made more than 700 judicial appointments in his first five years in office, a pace that put him on track to have selected roughly half the state bench by the end of two terms. The numbers also reveal something unusual: in demographic terms, Newsom's appointments represent the most significant transformation of California's judiciary in its history.

---

## The Appointment Pipeline

California has approximately 2,200 judicial positions: 7 on the Supreme Court, 106 on the Courts of Appeal, and roughly 2,000 on the 58 Superior Courts. The Superior Courts are trial courts; the Courts of Appeal hear appeals; the Supreme Court hears discretionary appeals and certain mandatory appeals, including death penalty cases.

Judges in California are appointed by the governor when a vacancy arises through retirement, elevation (a Superior Court judge elevated to the Court of Appeal, which creates a downstream Superior Court vacancy), death, or new judgeships created by the Legislature. Appointed judges then face a retention election at the next general election: a yes/no vote on whether to retain the judge in office. Fewer than 1 percent of California judges lose retention elections. The practical effect is that an appointment is a lifetime appointment for as long as the judge chooses to remain on the bench.

A governor who serves two full terms, eight years, can expect to make approximately 400 to 600 appointments depending on retirement rates, legislative creation of new seats, and the pace of elevation. Newsom, who took office in January 2019, made more than 700 appointments in his first five years, a pace above historical averages, partly because a cohort of judges appointed in the 1990s and early 2000s reached senior status and retired during his tenure.

The Judicial Nominations Evaluation Committee, a State Bar body, evaluates nominees and rates them as Exceptionally Well Qualified, Well Qualified, Qualified, or Not Qualified. The governor's office reviews these ratings as part of the nomination process. Nominees rated Not Qualified are occasionally appointed nonetheless, which is within the governor's authority but typically attracts attention from the bar and legal community.

## The Demographic Transformation

Newsom's judicial appointments are approximately 60 to 65 percent women, compared to roughly 30 to 35 percent female in the pre-Newsom California judiciary. People of color represent over 50 percent of his appointees, compared to approximately 25 to 30 percent of the existing bench when he took office. Approximately 30 percent of Newsom's appointees publicly identify as LGBTQ, a figure that has no reliable historical precedent because prior governors did not track or publicly report this characteristic.

The aggregate effect of 700-plus appointments is not immediate transformation of the full bench, because Newsom's appointees represent roughly one-third of the 2,200 total positions filled over five years. But the marginal effect of each appointment class moving the demographic composition of the judiciary is cumulative: as pre-Newsom judges continue to retire and be replaced by Newsom appointees, the overall composition of the bench shifts.

The most visible single appointment in this transformation is Chief Justice Patricia Guerrero, appointed in November 2022 after the retirement of Chief Justice Tani Cantil-Sakauye. Guerrero became the first Latina to serve on the California Supreme Court and the first Latina to serve as Chief Justice of any state supreme court in American history. She was rated Exceptionally Well Qualified by the JNE Committee and confirmed by the Commission on Judicial Appointments. Her appointment is the most prominent individual signal of the demographic direction Newsom's appointments have collectively taken.

## How Judges Are Actually Selected

The formal appointment process involves the governor's Legal Affairs office soliciting applications, reviewing JNE ratings, conducting interviews, and making selections. In practice, the process is more politically textured than the formal description suggests.

Judgeship recommendations in California flow through a set of informal channels: state legislators, county bar associations, local political figures, and community organizations all participate in signaling preferred candidates to the governor's office. A sitting assemblymember or state senator who strongly recommends a candidate from their district exerts meaningful influence. County Democratic and Republican parties, community legal organizations, and major law firms also play roles. The governor's office does not publish a description of how these inputs are weighted, because the weighing is discretionary.

What Newsom's office has stated publicly is that diversity, broadly defined to include gender, race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, professional background, and geographic distribution, is an explicit criterion in evaluating candidates. The appointment data bears this out. Whether the diversity emphasis has come at the expense of other criteria, including years of legal experience, trial or appellate practice background, or JNE qualification ratings, is contested.

A 2023 analysis by the California Court Watchers organization found that the share of Newsom appointees rated Exceptionally Well Qualified, the highest JNE rating, was approximately 22 percent, compared to approximately 29 percent for appointees of Governor Jerry Brown. The share rated Qualified, the lowest passing rating, was approximately 18 percent for Newsom versus approximately 11 percent for Brown. These comparisons are imprecise because JNE rating distributions vary by year, court level, and how many seats were competed. But the directional finding, that Newsom appointees received somewhat lower JNE ratings on average, has been cited in the debate over whether the diversity emphasis has affected selection of the highest-rated candidates.

## Retention Elections: The Non-Threat

California's judicial retention election system was designed as a democratic check on the appointment power. In practice, it functions as a formality. Judges appointed by the governor appear on the next general election ballot, and voters decide whether to retain them. Since 1986, when Rose Bird, Cruz Reynoso, and Joseph Grodin were removed from the California Supreme Court in a highly politicized retention campaign over death penalty rulings, no California appellate judge and almost no Superior Court judge has lost a retention election.

The three 1986 removals stand as an outlier, produced by a coordinated campaign funded by business interests and agricultural groups that spent approximately $5 million (in 1986 dollars) against the three justices on death penalty grounds. The episode had a chilling effect on judicial decision-making that legal scholars have documented. It also produced the current dynamic, in which governors make appointments knowing that retention elections pose essentially no electoral risk unless a judge becomes a focal point of a coordinated political campaign.

The practical irreversibility of judicial appointments concentrates enormous consequence in the appointment itself. A governor who serves eight years and makes 600 to 800 appointments shapes the judiciary for the next two to three decades. Newsom's appointments will be deciding cases in California courts into the 2040s and 2050s. The demographic choices embedded in those appointments are, in that sense, long-run commitments.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that Newsom's appointments have prioritized demographic diversity over the traditional meritocratic criteria the JNE rating system is designed to assess, producing a judiciary that looks more like California's population but may include a higher share of judges whose professional qualifications were secondary to their identity characteristics. The 22 percent Exceptionally Well Qualified rate versus 29 percent under Brown suggests a tradeoff, however imprecisely measured. California's judiciary is the institution that interprets the law, protects rights, and resolves disputes for 40 million people. Its demographic composition matters, but its competence matters more, and the two are not automatically aligned by an appointment process that treats diversity as an explicit selection criterion rather than a desirable outcome of a fair merit-based process. The near-irrevocability of judicial appointments, with retention elections as a check that almost never activates, means that any systematic underweighting of qualification in the selection process compounds over decades.

The generous read is that the pre-Newsom California judiciary was predominantly white and male in a state that is majority nonwhite and 51 percent female, a mismatch that had persisted for 170 years not because white men were uniquely qualified to be judges but because the informal networks through which judicial candidates were recruited and selected systematically disadvantaged everyone else. Newsom's appointments have corrected that mismatch faster than any governor before him, and the correction is not merely symbolic: research in judicial behavior finds that the demographic composition of courts affects outcomes in ways that are visible in the data, particularly for cases involving gender discrimination, criminal sentencing, and civil rights. Chief Justice Guerrero was rated Exceptionally Well Qualified. Most Newsom appointees with lower JNE ratings were still rated Qualified, meaning the bar passed the minimum competence threshold. The JNE rating itself reflects criteria, including extensive prior experience in a specific practice area, that historically advantaged candidates from large law firms and prosecutorial offices over public defenders, legal aid attorneys, and in-house counsel, all of whom bring equally relevant experience to the bench.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The most significant thing the appointment data does not show is judicial quality after appointment. California does not systematically evaluate judicial performance beyond the initial JNE rating process, the retention election that almost never fails, and the Commission on Judicial Performance, which handles misconduct complaints. Whether Newsom's appointments decide cases well, manage courtrooms effectively, or produce legally sound decisions that survive appellate review is not aggregated into any public dataset.

Appellate reversal rates by trial judge are in principle computable from case management data but are not routinely published. Law review studies of judicial decision-making that track individual judges are produced by academics but are not systematic evaluations of the full bench. The quality of Newsom's judicial appointments is, at this point in time, a question that the data does not answer.

The data also does not show the full distribution of candidates who applied and were not selected. The pool from which Newsom made 700 appointments included thousands of applicants. Whether the selected appointees were the best-qualified candidates from a diverse pool or whether diversity criteria resulted in skipping over higher-rated candidates in favor of lower-rated ones with different demographic characteristics requires access to application data the governor's office does not publish.

---

Seven hundred appointments in five years. A first Latina Chief Justice. A bench that for the first time in California history has more female judges than male, in the aggregate of Newsom's selection class. These are facts. They sit alongside a JNE rating distribution that suggests Newsom's appointees received lower qualification ratings on average than those of his predecessor, which is also a fact. Whether the two facts add up to a net improvement in California's judiciary or a net tradeoff depends on what one believes about the relationship between demographic representation and judicial quality, a question that the appointment data can frame but cannot resolve. The bench Newsom is building will be deciding cases in California for the next thirty years. The data on whether it decides them well is still being generated, one ruling at a time.
