# California Counts: The Babel Problem
## What 220 languages spoken, 7 million limited-English-proficient residents, a Dymally-Alatorre Act that requires translation into 12 languages, and a government services system where comprehension determines survival reveal about the multilingual state California became before its institutions adapted

California is the most linguistically diverse state in the nation. Approximately 44 percent of California residents (approximately 17 million people) speak a language other than English at home. Approximately 7 million Californians are classified as limited English proficient (LEP), meaning they report speaking English "less than very well." The LEP population is approximately 18 percent of the total state population, the highest share of any state. Spanish speakers constitute approximately 65 percent of the LEP population, followed by Chinese languages (approximately 8 percent), Vietnamese (approximately 4 percent), Korean (approximately 3 percent), Tagalog (approximately 3 percent), and approximately 215 other languages.

The Dymally-Alatorre Bilingual Services Act (1973) requires state agencies to provide services in any language spoken by a "substantial number" of the population served (defined as 5 percent or more of the agency's clientele). The act requires agencies to hire bilingual staff, translate vital documents, and ensure that LEP individuals can access government services. In practice, compliance has been inconsistent for the law's entire 50-year history. The State Personnel Board's surveys have consistently found that state agencies do not have sufficient bilingual staff to meet the act's requirements, that vital documents are not translated into all required languages, and that LEP individuals face significant barriers to accessing services in health care, social services, courts, and regulatory agencies.

---

## The Stakes

Language access is not an abstract equity concern; it determines whether people receive the government services they qualify for. A LEP Spanish speaker who cannot understand a Medi-Cal renewal form may lose health coverage. A LEP Chinese speaker who cannot navigate the EDD unemployment system may not receive benefits during a layoff. A LEP Vietnamese speaker who receives a court notice in English may fail to appear and face a warrant. A LEP Mixtec-speaking farmworker who cannot read a pesticide safety label may be exposed to chemicals that cause chronic illness.

The stakes are highest in the systems that serve the most vulnerable populations. Medi-Cal serves approximately 14 million Californians, approximately 40 percent of whom speak a language other than English at home. The courts process millions of cases per year involving unrepresented LEP litigants. CPS (child protective services) investigates families in which parents may not understand the allegations, the process, or their rights. The gap between the legal requirement (language access) and the operational reality (English-dominant systems with patchwork translation) means that language status functions as a de facto eligibility barrier: people who cannot communicate with the system cannot access the system.

---

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California is a multilingual state governed by monolingual institutions. The Dymally-Alatorre Act is 50 years old and still inadequately implemented. The courts have invested in interpreter services but cannot provide interpreters for all LEP litigants in all proceedings. Medi-Cal managed care plans are required to provide language services but the quality and availability of medical interpretation varies dramatically by plan and provider. The public school system serves 1.1 million English Learners but the shortage of bilingual teachers (approximately 30 percent below need) means that many English Learners receive instruction they cannot fully understand. The language access gap is a structural inequity that compounds every other inequity: LEP residents are disproportionately low-income, immigrant, and members of communities of color, and the language barrier reduces their access to the services designed to address those disadvantages.

The generous read is that California has the most extensive language access framework of any state: the Dymally-Alatorre Act, the Kopp Act (court interpreters), the Health and Safety Code language access requirements for health plans, the education system's English Learner programs, and executive orders requiring language access in state agencies. The state certifies court interpreters in approximately 18 languages, provides voter materials in 10 languages, and funds language access coordinators in major agencies. The system is imperfect but its existence reflects a commitment that most states have not made. The challenge is scale: serving 7 million LEP residents in 220 languages exceeds the capacity of any translation and interpretation infrastructure. Technology (machine translation, AI-assisted interpretation) may close the gap in ways that human staffing alone cannot.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The State Personnel Board surveys language capability in state agencies. No agency tracks LEP individuals' actual experiences accessing government services: how often they encounter language barriers, how frequently those barriers result in denied services, missed benefits, or adverse outcomes, and which agencies and programs have the most severe access gaps. The language access gap is defined by the law's requirements but not measured by the law's outcomes.
