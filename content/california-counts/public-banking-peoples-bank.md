# California Counts: The People's Bank
## What AB 857's authorization of public banks, zero operational public banks since passage, the Bank of North Dakota as the only precedent, and $100 billion in idle local government deposits reveal about the public banking movement California enabled in law and has not yet built in practice

AB 857 (2019) authorized the creation of public banks in California, making it one of the first states to establish a legal framework for government-owned banking institutions. A public bank is a bank owned by a city, county, or joint powers authority that accepts deposits from the government entity and uses those deposits to make loans that serve public purposes: affordable housing, small business lending, infrastructure, and community development. The model is the Bank of North Dakota (BND), established in 1919, which holds all of North Dakota's state deposits and has operated profitably for over a century, generating approximately $150 million to $200 million per year in revenue for the state.

California's local governments hold approximately $100 billion in deposits (tax revenues, bond proceeds, reserve funds) in commercial banks. These deposits are currently managed through the Local Agency Investment Fund (LAIF) or invested in commercial bank accounts that earn market returns. Public banking proponents argue that a public bank could use these deposits as a base for lending that addresses policy priorities (affordable housing, small business, clean energy) at below-market rates, generating both financial returns for the government and community development benefits that commercial banks do not provide.

## The Implementation Gap

Despite AB 857's 2019 passage, no California public bank has opened as of 2026. Several efforts are underway: the San Francisco Public Bank, the Los Angeles Public Bank (authorized by city voters in 2018), and the East Bay Public Bank (a multi-jurisdictional effort). Each has encountered the same obstacles: capitalization (a public bank needs $50 million to $200 million in initial capital, depending on size, which must come from government appropriation or bond issuance), federal regulatory approval (obtaining a bank charter from the FDIC or the OCC, a process that takes two to five years and requires demonstrating financial viability), and political sustainability (the public bank must survive changes in local government leadership, opposition from the commercial banking industry, and the slow pace of regulatory approval).

The commercial banking industry opposes public banking through lobbying and public argument. The California Bankers Association contends that public banks would compete unfairly with private banks (using government deposits that are currently placed in commercial banks), that government entities lack the expertise to manage banking operations, and that the risk of loss would fall on taxpayers. The opposition has not prevented AB 857's passage or local authorization votes, but it has contributed to the slow pace of implementation by raising concerns among local officials about financial risk and political exposure.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that public banking in California is a policy innovation that exists in law but not in reality. Seven years after AB 857's passage, no public bank has opened, no charter has been obtained, and no capital has been deployed. The implementation challenges (capitalization, regulation, expertise, political sustainability) are not trivial: starting a bank is a multi-year, multi-million-dollar undertaking, and doing it within a government bureaucracy adds layers of procurement, civil service, and political accountability that private banks do not face. The risk is that the public banking movement spends a decade building institutions that, when they finally open, are too small to materially affect the housing, lending, or community development challenges they were designed to address. The Bank of North Dakota works because it was established in 1919 and has had a century to build its balance sheet. A new public bank in San Francisco would start with a fraction of the capital and no track record.

The generous read is that institutional innovation takes time, and the foundational work (legal authorization, governance design, community engagement, feasibility studies) is necessary precursor to operation. The Bank of North Dakota did not achieve its current scale immediately; it grew over decades. The value proposition of public banking is structural: redirecting $100 billion in government deposits from commercial banks (which invest for shareholder return) to public banks (which invest for community benefit) changes the flow of capital in ways that compound over time. The first California public bank, whenever it opens, will establish a model that other jurisdictions can replicate and improve. The movement is slower than its advocates hoped, but institutional reform in a complex regulatory environment is inherently slow.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

No California agency tracks the investment and lending activity of local government deposits in commercial banks: what those deposits fund, who benefits, and whether the returns to government and community would be higher through a public bank. The comparison (commercial banking returns versus hypothetical public banking returns) is modeled by advocates and critics using assumptions that produce different conclusions. The empirical test (an operating California public bank with measurable results) does not yet exist.
