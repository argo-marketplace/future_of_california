# California Counts: The Apprenticeship Gap
## What 80,000 registered apprentices, 90 percent male enrollment, and a five-year path to $100,000 reveal about the workforce training system California has and the one it needs

California runs the largest registered apprenticeship system in the United States. Approximately 75,000 to 80,000 people are enrolled in registered apprenticeship programs overseen by the Division of Apprenticeship Standards at any given time, a number that dwarfs the next-largest state systems. The programs that feed most of those slots are the building and construction trades: electricians, plumbers, pipe fitters, carpenters, ironworkers, operating engineers, sheet metal workers, and a dozen adjacent crafts. They produce journeyworkers who earn $90,000 to $110,000 a year and who, in a state perpetually short of skilled construction labor, are never unemployed for long. The system works remarkably well for the people inside it. The question that the data raises is how many people it was ever designed to let in.

---

## The System: How Registered Apprenticeship Works

Registered apprenticeship in California is a federal-state partnership. The U.S. Department of Labor's Office of Apprenticeship sets minimum standards for registered programs, and California's Division of Apprenticeship Standards, housed within the Department of Industrial Relations, registers, monitors, and approves apprenticeship programs operating in the state.

A registered apprenticeship program is a formal training arrangement in which an apprentice works under journeyman supervision while also attending related technical instruction, typically 144 to 288 hours of classroom time per year. Programs last between one and six years depending on the occupation. Upon completion, apprentices receive a journey-level card recognized across the industry, a credential that is portable, stackable, and unambiguous.

Apprenticeship programs in the building trades are predominantly run by joint apprenticeship training committees: partnerships between a union and its signatory contractors. The JATC model is the dominant one in California construction trades. The union recruits applicants, the contractors provide jobs and pay wages, and the JATC provides instruction. Starting wages for apprentices run approximately $18 to $25 per hour depending on the trade and year of apprenticeship, with wages increasing at each anniversary. A fifth-year electrician apprentice in California earns approximately $42 to $48 per hour, plus benefits, before receiving a journeyman card.

Employer-run programs, not affiliated with unions, are a growing but still smaller portion of the registered apprenticeship universe. These programs are common in healthcare, information technology, and certain manufacturing sectors where union structures are absent.

## The Demographics: Who Gets In

The registered apprenticeship system in California is approximately 90 to 92 percent male. Women represent less than 10 percent of all registered apprentices, a figure that has improved from roughly 5 percent a decade ago but remains dramatically below women's share of the overall California workforce at 48 percent.

Racial and ethnic composition is more complex. Hispanic workers represent approximately 35 to 40 percent of registered apprentices, closely tracking their share of the construction workforce and of the state's general workforce. White workers represent approximately 30 to 35 percent. Black workers represent approximately 5 to 7 percent, below their 6 percent share of the state population but above their share in many individual trades. Asian and Pacific Islander workers represent approximately 5 to 8 percent.

The underrepresentation of women is not primarily a structural exclusion enforced by formal rules. Formal discrimination in registered apprenticeship was prohibited by federal regulations dating to the 1970s. The underrepresentation reflects more diffuse barriers: recruitment practices that rely on word-of-mouth through existing networks that skew male, physical workplace cultures in many trades that have been hostile to women, the geographic concentration of union hiring halls in areas not easily accessible by transit to many women with caregiving responsibilities, and a cultural signal to young women that construction trades are not for them that begins long before apprenticeship applications are submitted.

The Division of Apprenticeship Standards has set targets for female participation and required programs to develop outreach plans. Progress has been slow. At the current rate of improvement, female participation would reach 15 percent sometime in the mid-2030s.

## The Sectors Missing from the Picture

The building and construction trades account for roughly 70 to 75 percent of California's registered apprentices. The remaining 25 to 30 percent are distributed across other sectors, but that distribution is thin and uneven.

Healthcare apprenticeships, primarily medical assistant, pharmacy technician, and home health aide programs, have grown but remain under 3,000 statewide. Given that healthcare is the largest employment sector in California and faces documented workforce shortages in clinical support roles, the gap between the sector's workforce needs and its use of the apprenticeship model is significant.

Information technology apprenticeships, covering cybersecurity analysts, software quality assurance testers, network administrators, and related roles, have been the fastest-growing non-construction sector. Several employer-run programs, including arrangements with major Bay Area technology employers, have added several thousand slots since 2018. Starting wages for IT apprentices average $22 to $35 per hour, and the programs typically last one to two years rather than the four to five of construction trades, making them faster pathways to middle-class wages.

California's Cal Competes Tax Credit includes apprenticeship hiring as a qualifying criterion, providing employers some financial incentive to create registered apprenticeship slots. The incentive has not produced a surge in non-construction apprenticeship registrations. The barrier for most employers in non-construction sectors is not financial but structural: the joint apprenticeship committee model requires a level of employer-labor coordination that is natural in a unionized construction context and unfamiliar outside it.

The number of new apprenticeship registrations per year runs approximately 20,000 to 25,000 across all sectors in California. The number of people who want living-wage careers in healthcare, technology, manufacturing, and public service is orders of magnitude larger.

## The Outcome: What Completing an Apprenticeship Means

The return on completing a registered apprenticeship in the building trades is, by workforce development standards, extraordinary. A journeyman electrician in California earns approximately $90,000 to $110,000 per year in base wages, plus benefits including defined-benefit pension contributions, health insurance, and annuity payments that can add $30,000 to $50,000 in total compensation value on top of wages. The total compensation package for a journeyman IBEW electrician in Los Angeles is approximately $140,000 to $160,000 per year. The apprenticeship itself costs the apprentice nothing beyond their time; wages during the training period, while lower than journeyman wages, are sufficient to support a household without taking on student debt.

Compare this to the cost of a four-year college degree in California: average total cost at a California State University exceeds $100,000 for four years of tuition, fees, housing, and books for a student without financial aid. The median starting salary for a CSU graduate is approximately $50,000. The financial arithmetic of apprenticeship versus college is not uniformly favorable to either, and depends heavily on the individual's field, location, and preferences. But for the construction trades, the arithmetic is not close: zero debt, earn-while-you-learn, and a journeyman wage that exceeds the median California household income within five years of starting.

The apprenticeship route does not work for everyone. It requires five years of physical labor in often demanding conditions. It requires passing aptitude and drug tests for entry. In many trades it requires working a dispatch hall system that can mean geographic mobility and variable employment in slower economic periods. The union card that results is less transferable across state lines than a college diploma, though California's scale means that mobility within the state is rarely an issue.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's registered apprenticeship system is a high-quality workforce development program that is available to a small fraction of the people who could benefit from it, concentrated in industries that are already unionized, disproportionately excludes women and certain communities of color through network-dependent recruitment, and has expanded to new industries only at the margins despite fifteen years of stated policy goals to broaden its reach. The 80,000 registered apprentices is an impressive number in absolute terms and an embarrassingly small number relative to a workforce of 19 million. Every year California produces tens of thousands of JATC-trained journeyman electricians and plumbers with living-wage careers ahead of them, and simultaneously allows hundreds of thousands of workers in healthcare, technology, and services to cycle through training programs that cost money, take time, and produce credentials that do not carry the same wage premium. The question of why the apprenticeship model has not scaled to those sectors is partly organizational and partly political: the joint apprenticeship committee model depends on labor-management structures that do not exist in most growing industries, and no one with sufficient power has had sufficient incentive to build substitute structures.

The generous read is that California's building trades apprenticeship system produces exactly what it is designed to produce: skilled workers, at scale, with wages that have made the California construction trades a middle-class career path that did not require a four-year degree. The JATC model is effective precisely because it aligns the incentives of employers and workers around shared training quality standards, a structure that is difficult to replicate in industries without equivalent labor-management partnership. The non-construction apprenticeship growth, while smaller in absolute terms, represents genuine progress: IT apprenticeship programs did not exist at scale in 2015 and now serve thousands of workers a year. The demographic gaps in women's representation are real and documented, and the state and individual JATCs are investing in targeted outreach; the improvement from 5 percent to 10 percent female enrollment in a decade, though slow, is movement in the right direction. A workforce development model that reliably produces $100,000-a-year careers is not a system to dismantle in pursuit of a broader version that might not work as well.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Division of Apprenticeship Standards publishes registration data and completion data. It does not publish long-term wage trajectory data, meaning the claim that journeyman electricians earn $90,000 to $110,000 comes from union collective bargaining agreements and industry surveys, not from a matched panel dataset following apprentice completers over a career. The wage data is real but not systematically tracked by DAS.

The data does not capture the applicant-to-acceptance ratio for oversubscribed programs. In popular JATC programs in Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Sacramento, there are reported to be many more qualified applicants than available slots. The number of people who applied, passed aptitude tests, and were not selected is not published. That number would be the most direct measure of unmet demand for apprenticeship opportunity in California.

Non-completion rates are also underreported. Registered apprenticeship programs have historically had completion rates in the 40 to 60 percent range nationally, with completion rates varying substantially by trade, region, and demographic group. California-specific completion rates disaggregated by gender, race, and trade are not prominently published by DAS, which limits the ability to identify where the system is losing people and why.

---

Eighty thousand apprentices in the country's largest registered apprenticeship system. Ninety-two percent male. Seventy percent in construction. A pathway to $100,000-a-year wages, available to whoever can get in. The system California has built is, within its defined scope, genuinely excellent. The scope is the problem. A workforce development model that covers 80,000 of 19 million workers, concentrates in one industry, and has expanded to new sectors only at the margins is not a workforce development system. It is a guild. A well-functioning one. A guild that California has decided, so far, not to systematically break open or replicate.
