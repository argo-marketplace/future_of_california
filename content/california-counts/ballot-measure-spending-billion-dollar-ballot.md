# California Counts: The Billion-Dollar Ballot
## What $1.2 billion in ballot measure spending in 2024, $500 million on a single proposition, a 100-to-1 spending gap between corporate and grassroots campaigns, and a direct democracy system where the side that spends more wins 80 percent of the time reveal about the initiative process money has colonized

California's ballot measure elections are the most expensive direct democracy contests in the world. In the November 2024 election cycle, total spending on California ballot propositions exceeded $1.2 billion, setting a new national record. The most expensive contest was Proposition 33 (rent control), which drew approximately $130 million in total spending (approximately $110 million in opposition, primarily from corporate landlords and the real estate industry). Across all propositions, the side that spent more money won in approximately 80 percent of contests, a correlation that has held consistently over the past three decades.

The spending is dominated by industry groups and wealthy individuals. Corporations, trade associations, and industry PACs provide approximately 75 percent of all ballot measure funding. Individual donors provide approximately 15 percent. Small donors (contributions under $100) provide approximately 2 to 3 percent. A grassroots campaign funded by small donations cannot compete with an industry-funded campaign: the resource gap typically exceeds 10-to-1, and in some cases (the bail industry's campaign against bail reform, the gig companies' campaign for Proposition 22) exceeds 100-to-1.

## The Information Market

Ballot measure spending is not primarily advertising for a position; it is purchasing the definition of the question. The campaign with more money controls the framing: whether a rent control measure is described as "tenant protection" (proponent frame) or "government price controls that reduce housing supply" (opponent frame), whether a privacy measure is "consumer rights" or "job-killing regulation," whether a criminal justice measure is "accountability" or "mass incarceration." Television advertising, digital advertising, direct mail, and field operations deliver the frame to voters, and the side that delivers more messages more often in the final weeks of the campaign typically prevails.

The exception is "no" campaigns. Spending against a ballot measure (urging a "no" vote) is more effective per dollar than spending in favor (urging a "yes" vote). Voters who are confused or uncertain about a proposition default to "no," and opposition campaigns exploit this by increasing complexity and doubt rather than making an affirmative argument. This asymmetry means that well-funded industries can effectively veto ballot measures they oppose: the tobacco industry has defeated multiple tax increases, the oil industry has defeated multiple environmental measures, and the real estate industry has defeated rent control repeatedly, not by persuading voters that the status quo is good but by persuading voters that the proposed change is risky.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's initiative process has been captured by the interests it was designed to circumvent. The initiative, referendum, and recall were adopted in 1911 as Progressive Era reforms to allow citizens to bypass a legislature corrupted by the Southern Pacific Railroad. A century later, the initiative process is the railroad: a mechanism by which concentrated economic interests spend hundreds of millions of dollars to write laws (Proposition 22, written by gig companies), block laws (Proposition 33, blocked by landlords), and shape the legal environment to their advantage. The $1.2 billion spent on ballot measures in 2024 is not democracy; it is a market for legislation, and the buyers are corporations and billionaires.

The generous read is that ballot measure spending is speech, the Supreme Court has said so (Buckley v. Valeo, Citizens United), and the initiative process remains the only mechanism by which California voters can directly legislate over the heads of the legislature. High spending reflects high stakes: the propositions on the ballot affect billions of dollars in economic value, and the participants spend accordingly. Voters are not passive recipients of advertising; they evaluate arguments, consult voter guides, discuss with family and friends, and make independent judgments. The correlation between spending and outcomes partly reflects that well-funded campaigns tend to be on the popular side of well-understood issues, not that spending alone determines outcomes. And the initiative process has produced genuine reforms (Proposition 11's redistricting commission, Proposition 2's rainy day fund, Proposition 47's sentencing reform) that the legislature would not have enacted.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Secretary of State and the Fair Political Practices Commission publish campaign finance data for ballot measures, including contributions by source and expenditures by category. They do not publish analyses of the relationship between spending and outcomes, the effectiveness of different spending categories (television versus digital versus mail), or the return on investment for ballot measure campaigns. Whether a dollar spent on a ballot measure "buys" votes, and how many, is studied by political scientists but not measured by the state agencies that collect the spending data.
