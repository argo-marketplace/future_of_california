# California Counts: The Slow Emergency
## What 4 million diabetics, 27 percent adult obesity, $55 billion in annual healthcare costs, and a sugary drink tax that only four cities adopted reveal about the chronic disease epidemic California cannot regulate its way out of

Approximately 4 million California adults have been diagnosed with diabetes (Type 1 and Type 2 combined), and an additional estimated 2 million have undiagnosed diabetes or prediabetes. The prevalence rate for diagnosed diabetes is approximately 10 percent of the adult population, consistent with the national average. Approximately 27 percent of California adults are obese (BMI of 30 or higher), and an additional 33 percent are overweight (BMI 25 to 29.9). Combined, 60 percent of California adults exceed the healthy weight range. Among children ages 2 to 11, approximately 18 percent are obese, and the rate is higher for Latino children (approximately 25 percent) and children in low-income families.

The financial cost of chronic disease attributable to obesity and diabetes in California is estimated at approximately $55 billion per year: approximately $35 billion in direct medical costs (hospitalizations, physician visits, medications, dialysis) and approximately $20 billion in indirect costs (lost productivity, disability, premature mortality). Diabetes alone accounts for approximately $27 billion in direct medical costs, driven by the expense of managing complications (kidney disease, retinopathy, neuropathy, cardiovascular disease) that result from poorly controlled blood sugar over decades. The cost is borne by Medi-Cal (which covers approximately 40 percent of diabetics in the state), Medicare, employer-sponsored insurance, and patients directly.

## The Geography of Chronic Disease

Chronic disease prevalence in California follows the familiar geography of disadvantage. Diabetes prevalence exceeds 14 percent in the Imperial Valley, the southern San Joaquin Valley, and low-income neighborhoods in Los Angeles, compared to 6 to 7 percent in affluent coastal communities. Obesity rates are highest in the Central Valley, the Inland Empire, and the rural north, and lowest in the Bay Area and coastal Southern California. The correlation with income, education, food access, and race is consistent and strong: communities with the lowest incomes, the fewest grocery stores, the most fast-food outlets, and the least recreational infrastructure have the highest chronic disease rates.

The food environment is a measurable determinant. Approximately 2.7 million Californians live in "food deserts," census tracts where the nearest grocery store is more than a mile away in urban areas or more than 10 miles in rural areas. In these tracts, the available food is disproportionately from convenience stores, gas stations, and fast-food restaurants that offer calorie-dense, nutrient-poor options at low cost. CalFresh (food stamps) serves approximately 4.5 million Californians, providing a monthly average benefit of approximately $230 per person, an amount that nutrition research suggests is insufficient to purchase a healthy diet at California grocery prices.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California, the state that regulates automobile emissions to parts per billion and monitors pesticide drift with satellite precision, has proven unable or unwilling to address a chronic disease epidemic that costs $55 billion per year and kills more Californians than any other cause. Four cities (Berkeley, San Francisco, Oakland, and Albany) have adopted sugary drink taxes; the state legislature has repeatedly failed to pass a statewide tax. AB 2316 (2022), which would have prohibited the sale of sugary drinks in schools, was vetoed. The state's public health approach to chronic disease is educational (campaigns promoting healthy eating and physical activity) rather than structural (taxing harmful products, requiring food environment standards, or restricting marketing). Education without structural change produces awareness without behavior change, a pattern that the tobacco control experience demonstrated: tobacco use declined when California combined education with taxation, advertising restrictions, and workplace smoking bans. Chronic disease prevention has received the education without the structural tools.

The generous read is that California's chronic disease rates, while high, are below the national average for most metrics, and the state's investment in public health infrastructure (county public health departments, community health centers, Medi-Cal managed care) provides a platform for chronic disease management that many states lack. The CalFresh Healthy Living program, the Medi-Cal Diabetes Prevention Program, and the community health worker models being deployed through CalAIM are evidence-based interventions that target the highest-risk populations. The state's lower chronic disease rates relative to the South and Midwest reflect the combined effect of higher incomes, better food access in most regions, a culture of physical activity, and public health investments that, while insufficient, exist.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Department of Public Health publishes chronic disease prevalence data through the California Health Interview Survey (CHIS) and the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS). These surveys measure self-reported diagnosis, not clinical data. The actual prevalence of diabetes and prediabetes, including undiagnosed cases, is estimated from clinical studies and may exceed the survey-based figures by 30 to 50 percent.
