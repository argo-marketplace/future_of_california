# California Counts: The Ride Without a Driver
## What 9 million test miles, 450,000 weekly trips, and one $10 billion failure reveal about California's autonomous vehicle experiment

California requires every company testing autonomous vehicles on public roads to file an annual disengagement report with the DMV. The report must state how many miles the vehicle drove in autonomous mode and how many times a human safety driver had to take control. No other state requires this. No other country requires this. California invented the concept of mandated machine driving transparency in 2014, and for a decade the disengagement report was the only public dataset in the world that measured how well a robot drives a car. Between December 2024 and November 2025, autonomous vehicle permit holders logged more than nine million test miles on California roads. One company, Waymo, drove the vast majority of them. Another company, Cruise, drove zero: its permits had been revoked, its fleet grounded, and its parent company had written off more than $10 billion in losses. The disengagement reports tell a story that reads like a parable about transparency, consolidation, and the distance between testing a technology and surviving contact with the public.

---

## The Permits: 50 Companies In, One Company Standing

California's DMV issues three tiers of autonomous vehicle permits. A testing permit with a safety driver allows a company to test autonomous vehicles with a trained human behind the wheel ready to take control. A driverless testing permit allows testing without any human in the vehicle. A deployment permit allows commercial operation. As of 2025, approximately 50 companies hold or have held testing permits with a safety driver. Seven hold driverless testing permits. Two hold deployment permits: Waymo and Nuro.

The list of testing permit holders reads like a graveyard of ambition. Apple held a permit for Project Titan before canceling its autonomous vehicle program in 2024 after spending an estimated $10 billion over a decade. Uber held a permit before selling its Advanced Technologies Group to Aurora in 2020 after its own fatality in Arizona. Cruise held a permit before the DMV suspended it in October 2023. Argo AI, backed by Ford and Volkswagen, shut down in 2022. Dozens of smaller companies filed reports showing a few hundred or a few thousand miles driven, then stopped filing.

The consolidation is visible in the mileage data. In the 2024 reporting period, Waymo accounted for the vast majority of autonomous miles driven in California. Total testing miles across all companies dropped approximately 50 percent from the prior year, not because the technology retreated but because Cruise, the second-largest tester, ceased operations entirely. The remaining companies collectively logged a fraction of Waymo's total. The permit system that was designed to foster an industry of competitors has documented the emergence of a near-monopoly.

## The Disengagement Reports: The World's Only AI Driving Exam

Every year by January 1, each testing permit holder must file with the DMV a report listing every disengagement: every instance where the autonomous system either failed or the safety driver chose to take manual control. The report must include the date, location, cause, and whether the vehicle was in autonomous mode at the time. California Vehicle Code Section 38750 and the DMV's implementing regulations created this requirement in 2014.

The early reports were brutal. Companies reported disengagements every few miles. The technology was crude, the public roads unforgiving, and the data confirmed what skeptics suspected: the cars could not drive. But the trajectory was steep. Waymo's disengagement rate fell from once every 5,000 miles to once every tens of thousands of miles, then to rates so low that the metric became almost meaningless for the best-performing vehicles. A car that drives 100,000 miles between disengagements is not being measured by a disengagement report. It is being measured by what happens in the 100,000 miles.

The reports have a structural limitation that has grown more significant as the technology has improved. A disengagement in heavy San Francisco traffic is not the same as a disengagement on a straight highway in the Central Valley, but the reports do not weight by difficulty. A company testing only on easy routes can report a lower disengagement rate than a company testing in complex urban environments. The DMV publishes the data. It does not normalize it.

By 2025, the disengagement report had become a victim of its own success. The companies with the best technology had disengagement rates so low that year-over-year comparisons were statistically noisy. The companies with the worst technology had stopped testing or gone bankrupt. The report remained the world's only mandated disclosure of autonomous driving performance, but the variation it captured was increasingly about reporting methodology rather than driving capability.

## The Cruise Implosion: $10 Billion and a Cover-Up

On the night of October 2, 2023, a pedestrian in San Francisco was struck by a hit-and-run human driver, thrown into the adjacent lane, and then hit by a Cruise robotaxi. The Cruise vehicle's autonomous system detected the pedestrian, braked, but could not stop in time. It then executed a "pullover maneuver," dragging the pedestrian approximately 20 feet at up to seven miles per hour before coming to a stop. The pedestrian suffered severe injuries.

What followed was not primarily a story about autonomous vehicle safety. It was a story about institutional dishonesty. When Cruise briefed the DMV and the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration on the incident, it showed video of the initial impact and braking but cut the footage before the dragging sequence. The DMV learned about the dragging from other sources. On October 24, 2023, the DMV suspended all Cruise driverless testing and deployment permits in California, effective immediately, citing an "unreasonable risk to public safety." The CPUC followed by suspending Cruise's commercial passenger service permits.

Cruise admitted to filing a false report. The DMV fined the company $500,000. Cruise reached an $8 million settlement with the injured pedestrian. Nine senior executives, including the CEO, departed. The company recalled its entire fleet of approximately 950 vehicles.

General Motors, which had acquired Cruise in 2016 for approximately $1 billion and invested billions more, attempted a restart. Cruise resumed limited testing in Phoenix, Houston, and Dallas with safety drivers in 2024. In December 2024, GM announced it would cease funding Cruise's robotaxi operations entirely, redirecting autonomous driving development toward personal vehicle features. GM disclosed cumulative losses on Cruise exceeding $10 billion. The most expensive autonomous vehicle program outside of Waymo ended not because the technology failed, but because the company lied about what happened when it did.

## Waymo: 450,000 Rides a Week and Counting

While Cruise imploded, Waymo scaled. Waymo, a subsidiary of Alphabet, began as Google's Self-Driving Car Project in 2009 and has received more than $11.5 billion in investment. In February 2026, Waymo raised $16 billion at a valuation near $100 billion, the largest private funding round in the autonomous vehicle industry.

The operational numbers are no longer experimental. By December 2025, Waymo was providing more than 450,000 paid rides per week across its operating cities: San Francisco, Los Angeles, Phoenix, and Austin. Total paid rides in 2025 exceeded 14 million. Waymo's fleet had grown to approximately 2,500 robotaxis in the United States. By May 2026, Waymo's coverage area spanned more than 1,400 square miles across 11 cities, an area larger than Rhode Island. The company has logged more than 170 million fully autonomous miles.

The CPUC granted Waymo its initial driverless deployment permit for San Francisco in 2023. In March 2024, the CPUC approved expansion to the San Francisco Peninsula and Los Angeles. The expansion occurred over the objection of the San Francisco Board of Supervisors, which had passed a nonbinding resolution opposing driverless vehicle expansion in the city.

Waymo's safety data, published in peer-reviewed studies, forms the most comprehensive comparison between autonomous and human driving performance available. A 2024 study in Traffic Injury Prevention compared Waymo's rider-only crash data to human benchmarks over 7.1 million miles. An expanded study covering 56.7 million miles, also peer-reviewed, confirmed the initial findings. Across both studies, Waymo's autonomous vehicles showed statistically significant reductions in crash rates compared to human drivers, with particularly large reductions in crashes involving injuries.

The safety data has a structural caveat. Waymo's vehicles operate in specific, geofenced areas that the company has mapped in granular detail. They do not drive in snow. They do not drive on unmapped rural roads. They do not drive in construction zones they have not previously encountered. The comparison to human drivers, who drive everywhere in all conditions, is not apples to apples. Waymo's vehicles are safer than human drivers within the domain where Waymo's vehicles operate. Whether that domain is representative of driving generally is a question the data does not answer.

## The Regulatory Split: Two Agencies, One Technology

California's autonomous vehicle regulation is divided between two agencies that do not report to each other. The DMV, under the Business, Transportation and Housing Agency, handles testing permits, safety requirements, vehicle standards, disengagement reports, and collision reports. The CPUC, an independent regulatory commission, handles commercial passenger service permits: the authorization to pick up riders and charge fares.

The division made sense when it was created. The DMV regulates vehicles. The CPUC regulates transportation companies. But autonomous vehicles are both a vehicle and a transportation company in one, and the jurisdictional split has produced coordination failures. When the Cruise pedestrian incident occurred, the DMV and the CPUC took separate enforcement actions on separate timelines based on separate legal authorities. The DMV suspended Cruise's testing permits on October 24, 2023. The CPUC suspended Cruise's commercial permits separately. Neither agency had the authority to do what the other did.

SB 1298, signed in 2012 by Governor Brown, was the foundational statute authorizing autonomous vehicle testing in California. It directed the DMV to adopt regulations, which the DMV did in 2014 and updated multiple times since. The CPUC created its autonomous vehicle programs through its own regulatory proceedings starting in 2018. In April 2025, California announced it was overhauling its autonomous vehicle regulations, an acknowledgment that the framework built incrementally over 13 years needed structural revision.

Arizona, by contrast, has governed autonomous vehicles primarily through executive order. Governor Ducey's 2015 and 2018 executive orders created a permissive framework with minimal reporting requirements. Texas has no autonomous vehicle testing permit requirement at all. The contrast is stark: California requires more data from autonomous vehicle companies than any jurisdiction on earth, and the data has documented both the technology's improvement and the industry's consolidation. Arizona and Texas require less data and have attracted companies that prefer to operate without public scrutiny. Whether transparency produces better outcomes or merely better documentation is a question the two regulatory models are answering in real time.

## The Other Companies: Zoox, Nuro, and the Long Tail

Waymo is not alone, though it is dominant. Amazon's Zoox, acquired for $1.2 billion in 2020, holds a driverless testing permit in California and has been testing in San Francisco, with expansion to Los Angeles in April 2025 and to Phoenix and Dallas in 2026. Zoox's vehicle is purpose-built: a bidirectional pod with no steering wheel, designed from the ground up for autonomous operation rather than retrofitted from a conventional car. In August 2025, Zoox received federal clearance from NHTSA. In May 2025, Zoox issued a software recall after a crash in Las Vegas, a reminder that the testing phase is not finished.

Nuro received the first-ever autonomous vehicle deployment permit from the California DMV for its driverless delivery vehicles, small pods that carry packages rather than people. By 2026, Nuro had pivoted toward robotaxi service, partnering with Uber and receiving approval to test driverless Lucid Gravity vehicles as robotaxis on California roads.

Pony.ai, a Chinese-American company, went public in late 2024 and announced mass production of its seventh-generation robotaxi platform. Aurora Innovation, which acquired Uber's self-driving unit, has focused on autonomous trucking rather than passenger vehicles, operating primarily in Texas.

The long tail of testing permit holders includes automakers (Mercedes-Benz, BMW, Nissan), technology companies (Nvidia, Qualcomm), and startups at various stages of viability. Most test a handful of vehicles for a few thousand miles per year. The permit list is long. The list of companies with a viable path to commercial deployment is very short.

## The Community Friction: Cones, Protests, and a City Divided

San Francisco became the primary battleground for public attitudes toward autonomous vehicles, and the conflict was visceral. In the summer of 2023, protesters affiliated with a group called Safe Street Rebel placed traffic cones on the hoods of Waymo and Cruise vehicles, disabling them by exploiting the vehicles' cautious response to obstacles. The tactic went viral. The message was clear: the robots could be stopped by anyone with a $3 cone.

Chinatown residents and businesses organized against AV expansion, citing vehicles blocking narrow streets, confusing elderly pedestrians, and creating traffic in a neighborhood designed for foot traffic. The San Francisco Fire Department and Police Department documented instances of autonomous vehicles blocking emergency response, stopping in active fire zones, and failing to yield to emergency vehicles. The San Francisco Municipal Transportation Agency tracked AV-related service disruptions. In late 2025, when a San Francisco power outage disrupted cellular service, Waymo vehicles froze in place across the city, blocking intersections until power was restored.

Labor unions, particularly SEIU 1021 and the San Francisco Taxi Workers Alliance, organized against robotaxis on employment grounds. San Francisco had approximately 2,600 licensed taxi drivers before the pandemic. Uber and Lyft had already reduced their earnings. Robotaxis threatened to eliminate them entirely. The labor argument was straightforward: every autonomous ride is a ride that does not employ a human driver.

The San Francisco Board of Supervisors voted to oppose CPUC expansion of AV permits in the city. The vote was nonbinding. The CPUC approved the expansion anyway, over the city's objection, on a 3-1 vote in August 2023. The episode illustrated a governance tension that the regulatory framework had not resolved: local governments have no formal authority over autonomous vehicle deployment in their own cities. The DMV issues permits statewide. The CPUC issues permits statewide. A city that does not want robotaxis on its streets has no regulatory mechanism to prevent them.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has built the most expensive, most regulated, and most transparent autonomous vehicle testing ground in the world, and the result is a single company with a near-monopoly on driverless rides, operating over the objection of the city where it is most visible, employing zero drivers in a state with a transportation labor force of hundreds of thousands, valued at $100 billion on the basis of a technology that works only in fair weather on pre-mapped roads. The disengagement reports documented an industry that consolidated from 50 permit holders to one viable commercial operator. Cruise proved that the reporting requirements could be gamed, that a company could lie to regulators about a pedestrian being dragged 20 feet, and that the penalty for doing so was a $500,000 fine and an $8 million settlement, amounts that round to zero against $10 billion in cumulative investment. The transparency infrastructure is real. The accountability infrastructure is not.

The generous read is that California's regulatory framework did exactly what it was designed to do. The disengagement reports created a public record that allowed researchers, journalists, and regulators to track the technology's development. The DMV's collision reporting requirement produced the dataset that enabled peer-reviewed safety comparisons between autonomous and human drivers. When Cruise lied, the DMV caught it, suspended the permits, and the company collapsed. The system worked. The consolidation to a single dominant operator is not a regulatory failure; it is the predictable outcome of a technology that requires billions of dollars in investment and millions of miles of testing before it can operate commercially. Waymo's 450,000 weekly rides, its 170 million autonomous miles, and its peer-reviewed safety data represent the most significant transportation innovation since the smartphone enabled ride-hailing. California's contribution was not the technology. It was the data infrastructure that proved the technology works.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The DMV publishes disengagement reports and collision reports. The CPUC publishes quarterly ridership data. Waymo publishes safety comparisons. The data that none of these sources capture is the counterfactual: what happens to the taxi drivers, the Uber drivers, the Lyft drivers, and the delivery drivers who are displaced by automation. San Francisco's 2,600 taxi medallion holders represent a workforce that has already been diminished by ride-hailing and faces elimination by robotaxis. The economic impact of that displacement, the lost wages, the devalued medallions, the career transitions that may or may not occur, is not measured by any California state agency.

The data also does not capture the geographic equity question. Waymo operates in San Francisco, Los Angeles, and select other cities. It does not operate in Fresno, Bakersfield, Stockton, or the Central Valley. It does not operate in rural California. The technology's benefits, reduced crash rates, increased mobility for those who cannot drive, 24-hour availability, accrue to residents of wealthy urban areas. The technology's costs, job displacement, infrastructure adaptation, regulatory overhead, are distributed more broadly. Whether autonomous vehicles will ever serve the parts of California that need transportation most is a question the current deployment data cannot answer, because no company has an economic incentive to find out.

And the disengagement reports themselves are becoming obsolete. Waymo has advocated for their elimination, arguing that the metric is no longer meaningful for vehicles that drive tens of thousands of miles between disengagements. The DMV's 2025 regulatory overhaul may restructure reporting requirements entirely. The dataset that documented the autonomous vehicle industry's development from infancy to commercial viability may not survive the technology's maturation. The transparency tool that California invented to hold autonomous vehicles accountable may be retired precisely when the vehicles are operating at a scale where accountability matters most.

---

California did not invent the autonomous vehicle. It invented the autonomous vehicle permit, the disengagement report, the collision disclosure, and the commercial deployment framework that every other jurisdiction now references. The state built a regulatory infrastructure for a technology that did not yet work and then watched as 50 companies tested the technology, 49 failed or retreated, and one scaled to 450,000 rides per week at a $100 billion valuation. The data that California mandated has been cited in peer-reviewed journals, congressional hearings, and investor prospectuses. It has also documented a $10 billion corporate failure, a pedestrian dragged 20 feet under a robotaxi, a company that lied about it, and a city that did not want the technology it got. The ride without a driver is the ride without precedent. California is writing the regulatory playbook in real time, with real data, on real streets, and the only certainty the data provides is that the experiment is not over.
