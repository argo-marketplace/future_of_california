# California Counts: The Ride Without a Driver
## What 9 million test miles, 500,000 weekly trips, and one $10 billion failure reveal about California's autonomous vehicle experiment

California requires every company testing autonomous vehicles on public roads to file an annual disengagement report with the DMV. The report must state how many miles the vehicle drove in autonomous mode and how many times a human safety driver had to take control. No other state requires this. No other country requires this. California invented the concept of mandated machine driving transparency in 2014, and for a decade the disengagement report was the only public dataset in the world that measured how well a robot drives a car. Between December 2024 and November 2025, autonomous vehicle permit holders logged more than nine million test miles on California roads. One company, Waymo, drove the vast majority of them. Another company, Cruise, drove zero: its permits had been revoked, its fleet grounded, and its parent company had written off more than $10 billion in losses. The disengagement reports tell a story that reads like a parable about transparency, consolidation, and the distance between testing a technology and surviving contact with the public.

---

## The Permits: 50 Companies In, One Company Standing

California's DMV issues three tiers of autonomous vehicle permits. A testing permit with a safety driver allows a company to test autonomous vehicles with a trained human behind the wheel ready to take control. A driverless testing permit allows testing without any human in the vehicle. A deployment permit allows commercial operation. As of May 2026, 27 companies hold active testing permits with a safety driver, down from a peak of 50. Six hold driverless testing permits. Three hold deployment permits. The number of permit holders has declined steadily: 50 in 2022, 43 in 2023, 38 in 2024, 31 in January 2025. The exits have outnumbered the entrants in every year since 2022.

The list of former testing permit holders reads like a graveyard of ambition. Apple held a permit for Project Titan before canceling its autonomous vehicle program in 2024 after spending an estimated $10 billion over a decade. Uber held a permit before selling its Advanced Technologies Group to Aurora in 2020 after its own fatality in Arizona. Cruise held a permit before the DMV suspended it in October 2023. Argo AI, backed by Ford and Volkswagen, shut down in 2022. Dozens of smaller companies filed reports showing a few hundred or a few thousand miles driven, then stopped filing.

The consolidation is visible in the mileage data. In the 2024 reporting period (December 2023 through November 2024), total testing miles across all companies dropped to approximately 4.5 million, a 50 percent decline from the prior year's 9 million, not because the technology retreated but because Cruise, the second-largest tester, ceased operations entirely. Waymo drove 2.4 million of those miles, operating 1,035 vehicles in California, a 136 percent increase from 438 vehicles the year prior. In the 2025 reporting period, mileage rebounded to more than 9 million miles, with Waymo accounting for over 68 percent of all vehicle miles traveled and Zoox contributing nearly 25 percent. Cumulative autonomous test miles in California since 2014 now exceed 41 million. The permit system that was designed to foster an industry of competitors has documented the emergence of a near-monopoly.

## The Disengagement Reports: The World's Only AI Driving Exam

Every year by January 1, each testing permit holder must file with the DMV a report listing every disengagement: every instance where the autonomous system either failed or the safety driver chose to take manual control. The report must include the date, location, cause, and whether the vehicle was in autonomous mode at the time. California Vehicle Code Section 38750 and the DMV's implementing regulations created this requirement in 2014.

The early reports were brutal. Companies reported disengagements every few miles. The technology was crude, the public roads unforgiving, and the data confirmed what skeptics suspected: the cars could not drive. But the trajectory was steep. Waymo's disengagement rate fell from once every 5,000 miles to once every tens of thousands of miles, then to rates so low that the metric became almost meaningless for the best-performing vehicles. A car that drives 100,000 miles between disengagements is not being measured by a disengagement report. It is being measured by what happens in the 100,000 miles.

The reports have a structural limitation that has grown more significant as the technology has improved. A disengagement in heavy San Francisco traffic is not the same as a disengagement on a straight highway in the Central Valley, but the reports do not weight by difficulty. A company testing only on easy routes can report a lower disengagement rate than a company testing in complex urban environments. The DMV publishes the data. It does not normalize it.

By the 2025 reporting period, the disengagement report had become a victim of its own success. Total disengagements across all companies fell to fewer than 9,300, down from 28,800 the prior year. Waymo averaged 19,234 miles per disengagement. Zoox averaged 60,682 miles per disengagement, the best rate of any company testing. Nuro, by contrast, averaged just 646 miles per disengagement, a sharp deterioration from 2,044 miles the year before, recording 244 disengagements in 157,561 miles. Consumer Watchdog called the Nuro data "frightening" given the company's plans to deploy robotaxis for Uber. The spread between the best and worst performers is now two orders of magnitude, but the companies with the best technology had disengagement rates so low that year-over-year comparisons were statistically noisy. The report remained the world's only mandated disclosure of autonomous driving performance, but the variation it captured was increasingly about reporting methodology rather than driving capability.

## The Cruise Implosion: $10 Billion and a Cover-Up

On the night of October 2, 2023, a pedestrian in San Francisco was struck by a hit-and-run human driver, thrown into the adjacent lane, and then hit by a Cruise robotaxi. The Cruise vehicle's autonomous system detected the pedestrian, braked, but could not stop in time. It then executed a "pullover maneuver," dragging the pedestrian approximately 20 feet at up to seven miles per hour before coming to a stop. The pedestrian suffered severe injuries.

What followed was not primarily a story about autonomous vehicle safety. It was a story about institutional dishonesty. When Cruise briefed the DMV and the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration on the incident, it showed video of the initial impact and braking but cut the footage before the dragging sequence. The DMV learned about the dragging from another government agency ten days later, only after requesting the full video. On October 24, 2023, the DMV suspended all Cruise driverless testing and deployment permits in California, effective immediately, citing an "unreasonable risk to public safety," grounding a fleet of approximately 150 robotaxis in San Francisco. The CPUC followed by suspending Cruise's commercial passenger service permits.

The penalties accumulated across multiple agencies. NHTSA imposed a $1.5 million fine for failure to fully report the crash and required Cruise to submit a corrective action plan. The U.S. Department of Justice fined Cruise $500,000 for filing a false report to influence the federal investigation, with a three-year consent agreement requiring ongoing compliance. Cruise reached an $8 million settlement with the injured pedestrian. CEO Kyle Vogt resigned in November 2023. Nine senior executives departed. The company recalled its entire fleet of approximately 950 vehicles.

General Motors, which had acquired Cruise in 2016 for approximately $581 million and invested billions more, attempted a restart. Cruise resumed limited testing in Phoenix, Houston, and Dallas with safety drivers in May 2024. But in July 2024, GM indefinitely delayed production of the Origin, Cruise's purpose-built autonomous vehicle. In December 2024, GM announced it would cease funding Cruise's robotaxi operations entirely, citing the "considerable time and resources" needed to scale and "an increasingly competitive robotaxi market." The restructuring was projected to reduce spending by more than $1 billion annually. GM disclosed cumulative losses on Cruise exceeding $10 billion, against less than $500 million in total revenue. The most expensive autonomous vehicle program outside of Waymo ended not because the technology failed, but because the company lied about what happened when it did.

## Waymo: 450,000 Rides a Week and Counting

While Cruise imploded, Waymo scaled. Waymo, a subsidiary of Alphabet, began as Google's Self-Driving Car Project in 2009. Alphabet has invested approximately $30 billion in Waymo since inception, according to Wolfe Research. The company has raised $27.1 billion across five external funding rounds. In February 2026, Waymo raised $16 billion at a $126 billion post-money valuation, led by Dragoneer Investment Group, DST Global, and Sequoia Capital, with Alphabet funding the significant majority. It was the largest private funding round in autonomous vehicle history.

The operational numbers are no longer experimental. By December 2025, Waymo was providing more than 450,000 paid rides per week across its operating cities. By March 2026, that figure reached 500,000 weekly rides, a tenfold increase from 50,000 weekly rides in May 2024. Total paid rides in 2025 exceeded 14 million, tripling the approximately 4.5 million rides provided in 2024. Lifetime rides surpassed 20 million. Waymo's fleet had grown to approximately 3,000 robotaxis, all fully electric, built on the sixth-generation Waymo Driver platform using a Geely Zeekr RT body manufactured at a facility in Mesa, Arizona. By May 2026, Waymo's coverage area spanned more than 1,400 square miles across 11 U.S. cities, an area larger than Rhode Island. The company has logged more than 200 million fully autonomous miles. Waymo's annualized revenue reached $355 million as of February 2026, up from approximately $125 million at the end of 2024. Alphabet expects Waymo to contribute "meaningfully" to its financials by 2027. The company is targeting one million weekly trips by the end of 2026.

The expansion timeline tells the scaling story. San Francisco launched first. Los Angeles opened to the public on November 12, 2024, initially covering 80 square miles from Santa Monica to downtown, later expanding to over 120 square miles including Inglewood and Silverlake. Phoenix and Austin followed. In early 2026, Waymo launched driverless rides in Miami, Orlando, Houston, Dallas, and San Antonio, with all five targeting commercial service by year's end. San Diego, Seattle, Denver, and Nashville were announced for 2026 commercial launch. London became Waymo's first international market, with testing beginning in December 2025 and Tokyo announced for service.

The CPUC granted Waymo its initial driverless deployment permit for San Francisco in 2023. On August 10, 2023, the CPUC voted to allow both Cruise and Waymo to operate like taxis and charge fares at any hour in San Francisco. In March 2025, the DMV approved an expanded operational design domain for Waymo covering additional Bay Area Peninsula cities, including San Jose. The expansion occurred over the objection of the San Francisco Board of Supervisors, which had passed a nonbinding resolution opposing driverless vehicle expansion in the city.

Waymo's safety data, published in peer-reviewed studies, forms the most comprehensive comparison between autonomous and human driving performance available. A 2024 study in Traffic Injury Prevention compared Waymo's rider-only crash data to human benchmarks over 7.1 million miles. An expanded study covering 56.7 million miles, also peer-reviewed, confirmed the initial findings. A separate study led by Swiss Re, using insurance claims data across 25 million miles, found an 88 percent reduction in property damage claims and a 92 percent reduction in bodily injury claims compared to human drivers. Even when benchmarked against newer vehicles (model years 2018-2021), the reductions held: 86 percent for property damage, 90 percent for bodily injury. Across 127 million autonomous miles through September 2025, Waymo reported 90 percent fewer serious-injury-or-worse crashes, 82 percent fewer airbag-deployment crashes, and 92 percent fewer pedestrian injury crashes relative to human benchmarks. At its current scale of over 4 million miles per week, Waymo estimates it is preventing approximately one serious-injury crash every eight days.

The safety data has a structural caveat. Waymo's vehicles operate in specific, geofenced areas that the company has mapped in granular detail. They do not drive in snow. They do not drive on unmapped rural roads. They do not drive in construction zones they have not previously encountered. The comparison to human drivers, who drive everywhere in all conditions, is not apples to apples. Waymo's vehicles are safer than human drivers within the domain where Waymo's vehicles operate. Whether that domain is representative of driving generally is a question the data does not answer.

## The Regulatory Split: Two Agencies, One Technology

California's autonomous vehicle regulation is divided between two agencies that do not report to each other. The DMV, under the Business, Transportation and Housing Agency, handles testing permits, safety requirements, vehicle standards, disengagement reports, and collision reports. The CPUC, an independent regulatory commission, handles commercial passenger service permits: the authorization to pick up riders and charge fares.

The division made sense when it was created. The DMV regulates vehicles. The CPUC regulates transportation companies. But autonomous vehicles are both a vehicle and a transportation company in one, and the jurisdictional split has produced coordination failures. When the Cruise pedestrian incident occurred, the DMV and the CPUC took separate enforcement actions on separate timelines based on separate legal authorities. The DMV suspended Cruise's testing permits on October 24, 2023. The CPUC suspended Cruise's commercial permits separately. Neither agency had the authority to do what the other did.

SB 1298, signed in 2012 by Governor Brown, was the foundational statute authorizing autonomous vehicle testing in California. It directed the DMV to adopt regulations, which the DMV did in 2014 and updated multiple times since. The CPUC created its autonomous vehicle programs through its own regulatory proceedings starting in 2018. In April 2025, California announced it was overhauling its autonomous vehicle regulations, an acknowledgment that the framework built incrementally over 13 years needed structural revision. The new regulations, finalized in early 2026, comprehensively updated both light-duty and heavy-duty vehicle rules: they lifted the ban on testing and deploying autonomous vehicles weighing over 10,001 pounds, opening California to autonomous freight and transit operations for the first time, while requiring manufacturers to demonstrate a minimum of 500,000 autonomous miles (at least 100,000 in California) before applying for deployment. New safety reporting requirements, effective July 31, 2025, mandate detailed reports for accidents, near-misses, disengagements, and sensor or software failures.

Arizona, by contrast, has governed autonomous vehicles primarily through executive order. Governor Ducey's 2015 and 2018 executive orders created a permissive framework with minimal reporting requirements: companies must submit a law enforcement interaction plan to the Department of Transportation and the Department of Public Safety, but there is no mandated disengagement reporting and no public mileage data. Texas passed SB 2807 in 2025, creating its first formal authorization requirement for commercial autonomous vehicle operations, administered by the Texas Department of Motor Vehicles, a regulatory framework that did not exist until Waymo was already operating commercially in Austin, Houston, Dallas, and San Antonio. The contrast is stark: California requires more data from autonomous vehicle companies than any jurisdiction on earth, and the data has documented both the technology's improvement and the industry's consolidation. Arizona and Texas require less data and have attracted companies that prefer to operate without public scrutiny. Waymo's five newest U.S. cities are all in states with lighter regulatory regimes. Whether transparency produces better outcomes or merely better documentation is a question the competing regulatory models are answering in real time.

## The Other Companies: Zoox, Nuro, and the Long Tail

Waymo is not alone, though it is dominant. Amazon's Zoox, acquired for $1.2 billion in 2020, holds a driverless testing permit in California and has been testing in San Francisco and Foster City, with expansion to Los Angeles in April 2025 and to Phoenix and Dallas in March 2026. Zoox's vehicle is purpose-built: a bidirectional pod with no steering wheel, designed from the ground up for autonomous operation rather than retrofitted from a conventional car. In August 2025, NHTSA issued a federal exemption for Zoox's driverless vehicles. In November 2025, Zoox launched its "Zoox Explorers" program in San Francisco, offering free rides to select users in neighborhoods including SoMa and the Mission District. But Zoox also issued two software recalls in 2025: one in April covering 258 vehicles for unexpected hard braking, and another in May covering 270 vehicles after a Las Vegas crash revealed the system could inaccurately predict other vehicles' movements. The testing phase is not finished.

Nuro received the first-ever autonomous vehicle deployment permit from the California DMV for its driverless delivery vehicles, small pods that carry packages rather than people. By 2026, Nuro had pivoted entirely toward robotaxi service, abandoning its low-speed delivery program and partnering with Uber to deploy up to 20,000 Lucid Gravity vehicles equipped with Nuro's self-driving system over six years. In May 2026, Nuro received a California driverless testing permit for the Lucid Gravity vehicles, running on Nuro's autonomous stack built on NVIDIA's Drive AGX Thor computing platform. Two additional permits remain before commercial operations can begin: a deployment permit from the DMV and a driverless ride-hailing permit from the CPUC. Nuro's disengagement data, however, raises questions about readiness: 646 miles per disengagement versus Waymo's 19,234 and Zoox's 60,682.

Uber itself is pursuing a California ballot measure for 2026 that Consumer Watchdog has labeled a "License to Kill," alleging it would strictly limit victims' medical recovery in accident cases and prevent the seriously injured from finding contingency-fee attorneys, effectively shielding robotaxi operators and manufacturers from liability. Uber has also announced plans to deploy 50,000 Rivian robotaxis, including in San Francisco.

Pony.ai, a Chinese-American company, went public in late 2024 and announced mass production of its seventh-generation robotaxi platform, focused primarily on deployments in Guangzhou and Shenzhen rather than California. Aurora Innovation, which acquired Uber's self-driving unit, has focused on autonomous trucking rather than passenger vehicles, operating two driverless trucks daily as of May 2025 with plans to scale to "tens of trucks" by year's end, primarily in Texas.

The long tail of testing permit holders includes automakers (Mercedes-Benz, BMW, Nissan), technology companies (Nvidia, Qualcomm), and startups at various stages of viability. Only Waymo and Mercedes-Benz have tested continuously for all 11 years since the program began. Most test a handful of vehicles for a few thousand miles per year. The permit list is long. The list of companies with a viable path to commercial deployment is very short.

## The Community Friction: Cones, Protests, and a City Divided

San Francisco became the primary battleground for public attitudes toward autonomous vehicles, and the conflict was visceral. In the summer of 2023, protesters affiliated with a group called Safe Street Rebel placed traffic cones on the hoods of Waymo and Cruise vehicles, disabling them by exploiting the vehicles' cautious response to obstacles. The tactic went viral. The message was clear: the robots could be stopped by anyone with a $3 cone.

Chinatown residents and businesses organized against AV expansion, citing vehicles blocking narrow streets, confusing elderly pedestrians, and creating traffic in a neighborhood designed for foot traffic. The San Francisco Fire Department and Police Department documented 55 incidents in a six-month period where self-driving cars interfered with rescue operations, including running through yellow emergency tape, blocking firehouse driveways, and refusing to move for first responders. After Waymo phased out in-car safety monitors in June 2024, the San Francisco Municipal Transportation Agency faced an influx of complaints, creating a new dispatch category: "Driverless Car Incidents."

The December 2025 blackout crystallized the problem. A PG&E substation fire cut power to 130,000 homes and businesses, nearly one-third of PG&E's San Francisco customers. Over 1,500 Waymo robotaxis stalled at darkened intersections or in traffic lanes, their systems requesting "confirmation checks" from Waymo's fleet response team faster than the team could process them. At least three Waymo vehicles directly hindered emergency services, delaying an ambulance for 40 minutes and blocking firefighters responding to two fires. In at least 63 instances, vehicles had to be manually retrieved. Mayor Daniel Lurie personally called Waymo leadership to demand they clear the cars. Waymo eventually suspended service and later shipped a software update for navigating disabled traffic lights. Experts described the failure not as a software flaw but as an "operational management failure," a fleet too large for the company's human intervention capacity. The San Francisco Board of Supervisors held hearings.

Labor unions, particularly SEIU 1021 and the San Francisco Taxi Workers Alliance, organized against robotaxis on employment grounds. San Francisco had approximately 2,600 licensed taxi drivers before the pandemic. Uber and Lyft had already reduced their earnings. Robotaxis threatened to eliminate them entirely. The labor argument was straightforward: every autonomous ride is a ride that does not employ a human driver. In November 2024, San Francisco voters passed Proposition L, a tax on ride-hailing and autonomous vehicle companies projected to generate $25 million annually for Muni transit. But the measure was nullified by the competing Proposition M, which voters also approved and which contained a provision overriding the ride-share tax.

The San Francisco Board of Supervisors voted to oppose CPUC expansion of AV permits in the city. The vote was nonbinding. The CPUC approved the expansion anyway, over the city's objection, on a 3-1 vote in August 2023. The episode illustrated a governance tension that the regulatory framework had not resolved: local governments have no formal authority over autonomous vehicle deployment in their own cities. The DMV issues permits statewide. The CPUC issues permits statewide. A city that does not want robotaxis on its streets has no regulatory mechanism to prevent them.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has built the most expensive, most regulated, and most transparent autonomous vehicle testing ground in the world, and the result is a single company with a near-monopoly on driverless rides, operating over the objection of the city where it is most visible, employing zero drivers in a state with a transportation labor force of hundreds of thousands, valued at $126 billion on the basis of a technology that works only in fair weather on pre-mapped roads. The disengagement reports documented an industry that consolidated from 50 permit holders to one viable commercial operator. The DMV has received 978 autonomous vehicle collision reports since 2014, but it stopped tracking at-fault data in 2020 and does not require reporting for crashes involving vehicles carrying paying customers, only those being tested. Cruise proved that the reporting requirements could be gamed, that a company could lie to regulators about a pedestrian being dragged 20 feet, and that the combined penalties from the DMV, NHTSA, and the DOJ totaled $2.5 million, amounts that round to zero against $10 billion in cumulative investment. The transparency infrastructure is real. The accountability infrastructure is not.

The generous read is that California's regulatory framework did exactly what it was designed to do. The disengagement reports created a public record that allowed researchers, journalists, and regulators to track the technology's development. The DMV's collision reporting requirement produced the dataset that enabled peer-reviewed safety comparisons between autonomous and human drivers. When Cruise lied, the DMV caught it, suspended the permits, and the company collapsed under the weight of its own dishonesty and $10 billion in losses. The system worked. The consolidation to a single dominant operator is not a regulatory failure; it is the predictable outcome of a technology that requires tens of billions of dollars in investment and hundreds of millions of miles of testing before it can operate commercially. Waymo's 500,000 weekly rides, its 200 million autonomous miles, and its peer-reviewed safety data, including Swiss Re's finding of 88 percent fewer property damage claims and 92 percent fewer bodily injury claims, represent the most significant transportation innovation since the smartphone enabled ride-hailing. Autonomous vehicle startups raised a record $21.4 billion through April 2026, with Waymo's $16 billion round accounting for the majority. California's contribution was not the technology. It was the data infrastructure that proved the technology works.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The DMV publishes disengagement reports and collision reports. The CPUC publishes quarterly ridership data. Waymo publishes safety comparisons. The data that none of these sources capture is the counterfactual: what happens to the taxi drivers, the Uber drivers, the Lyft drivers, and the delivery drivers who are displaced by automation. San Francisco's 2,600 taxi medallion holders represent a workforce that has already been diminished by ride-hailing and faces elimination by robotaxis. The economic impact of that displacement, the lost wages, the devalued medallions, the career transitions that may or may not occur, is not measured by any California state agency.

The data also does not capture the geographic equity question. Waymo operates in San Francisco, Los Angeles, and select other cities. It does not operate in Fresno, Bakersfield, Stockton, or the Central Valley. It does not operate in rural California. The technology's benefits, reduced crash rates, increased mobility for those who cannot drive, 24-hour availability, accrue to residents of wealthy urban areas. The technology's costs, job displacement, infrastructure adaptation, regulatory overhead, are distributed more broadly. Whether autonomous vehicles will ever serve the parts of California that need transportation most is a question the current deployment data cannot answer, because no company has an economic incentive to find out.

Nationally, NHTSA's Standing General Order on crash reporting, first issued in 2021 and amended three times since, has recorded over 5,200 autonomous vehicle incidents through November 2025, including 451 injuries and fatalities. Of those, 1,429 involved Waymo vehicles, reflecting the company's dominance in miles driven rather than a disproportionate crash rate. Tesla's "robotaxi" service, which launched in mid-2025 with human safety drivers using Level 2 FSD Supervised rather than true autonomous driving, reported 14 crashes in its first months, including five in January 2026 alone. The CPUC has confirmed that Tesla does not operate an autonomous vehicle service in California; it holds a standard TCP permit, the same kind of permit any limousine company gets, with zero AV oversight.

And the disengagement reports themselves are becoming obsolete. Waymo has advocated for their elimination, arguing that the metric is no longer meaningful for vehicles that drive tens of thousands of miles between disengagements. The DMV's 2025-2026 regulatory overhaul restructured reporting requirements, mandating detailed safety event reports for accidents, near-misses, and sensor or software failures starting July 31, 2025. The dataset that documented the autonomous vehicle industry's development from infancy to commercial viability may not survive the technology's maturation. The transparency tool that California invented to hold autonomous vehicles accountable may be retired precisely when the vehicles are operating at a scale where accountability matters most.

---

California did not invent the autonomous vehicle. It invented the autonomous vehicle permit, the disengagement report, the collision disclosure, and the commercial deployment framework that every other jurisdiction now references. The state built a regulatory infrastructure for a technology that did not yet work and then watched as 50 companies tested the technology, 49 failed or retreated, and one scaled to 500,000 rides per week at a $126 billion valuation. The data that California mandated has been cited in peer-reviewed journals, congressional hearings, and investor prospectuses. It has also documented a $10 billion corporate failure, a pedestrian dragged 20 feet under a robotaxi, a company that lied about it, 1,500 stalled robotaxis during a blackout, and a city that did not want the technology it got. The ride without a driver is the ride without precedent. California is writing the regulatory playbook in real time, with real data, on real streets, and the only certainty the data provides is that the experiment is not over.
