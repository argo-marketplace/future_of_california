# California Counts: The Crumbling Classroom
## What 10,500 schools, a $100 billion facilities backlog, a 50-year-old average building age, and a bond financing model that spends $2 for every $1 built reveal about the school buildings California expects children to learn in

California operates approximately 10,500 public schools in approximately 1,000 school districts, serving 5.9 million students. The school buildings, which collectively represent one of the largest real estate portfolios in the state, have an average age of approximately 50 years. More than 30 percent were built before 1970. Many have never received a comprehensive modernization: their HVAC systems predate modern energy codes, their electrical systems cannot support classroom technology, their roofs leak, their plumbing fails, and their playgrounds are surfaced with materials that do not meet current safety standards.

The cumulative deferred maintenance and modernization backlog for California's K-12 school facilities is estimated at $100 billion to $150 billion, depending on the methodology and scope of the estimate. The range reflects the difficulty of assessing 10,500 buildings across 1,000 districts with no statewide facility condition assessment system. The Office of Public School Construction (OPSC), which administers state facility funding, does not maintain a comprehensive database of facility conditions. Individual districts assess their own facilities on their own schedules using their own methodologies. The $100 billion figure is the most commonly cited estimate, derived from school district bond measures, local assessments, and the Coalition for Adequate School Housing.

## The Bond Model

California funds school construction and modernization primarily through general obligation bonds, approved by voters at the state and local levels. State bonds (Proposition 1A of 1998, Proposition 47 of 2002, Proposition 55 of 2004, Proposition 51 of 2016, and Proposition 2 of 2024) have provided approximately $45 billion in cumulative school facility funding. Local bonds, approved by 55 percent of voters in a school district election (a threshold reduced from two-thirds by Proposition 39 of 2000), have provided tens of billions more.

The bond model has a structural deficiency: it is debt financing. For every dollar of facility construction funded by a general obligation bond, the issuing entity (state or district) pays approximately $1 to $2 in interest over the 25-to-30-year life of the bond. Capital appreciation bonds, a particularly expensive variant used by some districts, can produce repayment ratios of $5 to $10 for every $1 borrowed. A school district that issues $50 million in bonds to build a new school may repay $100 million to $150 million over the bond term. The interest payments are funded by property tax overrides, adding to the tax burden on property owners within the district.

The bond model also produces geographic inequity. Wealthy districts with high property values can pass larger local bonds, generating more per-pupil facility funding than low-income districts with lower property values. The state bond program is intended to offset this disparity by prioritizing funding for districts with the greatest need and least local capacity, but the state bond funds are episodic (available only when voters approve a new bond) and oversubscribed (the waitlist for Proposition 51 funds was exhausted within a few years of approval). The result is a facility quality disparity that mirrors the income disparity of the communities the schools serve: wealthy districts have modern, well-maintained facilities, and low-income districts have aging buildings with deferred maintenance.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California educates 5.9 million children in buildings that a commercial landlord would be required to remediate. The average school building is 50 years old. The backlog exceeds $100 billion. The financing model charges $2 in debt service for every $1 of construction. The state has no comprehensive inventory of school facility conditions, no statewide facility standards, and no ongoing capital funding stream. The bond model produces new money episodically, when voters approve a ballot measure, and distributes it through a bureaucratic process at OPSC that takes years from application to construction. The children who attend school in buildings with inadequate ventilation, outdated wiring, and deteriorating structures are disproportionately low-income and children of color, because the facility quality tracks district wealth, and district wealth tracks residential segregation. The physical environment in which children learn affects their learning, and California's school buildings are a physical expression of unequal investment.

The generous read is that the bond model, for all its cost, has produced real improvements. Proposition 51 and Proposition 2 have funded thousands of school construction and modernization projects. Local bonds have funded new schools, HVAC replacements, seismic retrofits, and technology upgrades across the state. The 55 percent threshold for local bonds (reduced from 67 percent by Proposition 39) has made facility investment accessible to more districts. And the school facility stock, while old, is not uniformly decrepit: many districts have used local bond proceeds to maintain and modernize their facilities effectively. The backlog is real, but the trajectory of investment is positive, and the alternative to bond financing, pay-as-you-go capital funding from the general fund, has never been politically viable in a state where the operating budget faces annual competition from healthcare, social services, and public safety.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

California does not have a statewide school facility condition assessment database. The OPSC tracks applications for state bond funding but does not maintain an independent assessment of every school building in the state. The $100 billion backlog estimate is an aggregation of local data and advocacy estimates, not a state-produced figure based on systematic assessment. Without a statewide inventory, the state cannot prioritize its limited facility funding based on objective condition data, cannot track whether investments are reducing the backlog, and cannot answer the basic question: which school buildings in California are in the worst condition?
