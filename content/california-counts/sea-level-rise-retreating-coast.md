# California Counts: The Retreating Coast
## What 3.5 feet of projected sea-level rise by 2100, $150 billion in exposed coastal property, 600,000 residents in inundation zones, and a managed retreat policy that no one will fund or enforce reveal about the climate adaptation California plans on paper and defers in practice

California's Ocean Protection Council projects 3.5 feet of sea-level rise along the California coast by 2100 under a high-emissions scenario, with approximately 1 foot by 2050. Under extreme scenarios (rapid Antarctic ice sheet collapse), rise could exceed 6 feet by 2100. The state's 2024 Sea-Level Rise Guidance recommends that state agencies plan for the high-emissions scenario, meaning that infrastructure, land-use decisions, and development permits should account for at least 3.5 feet of rise over the next 75 years.

Approximately 600,000 Californians live in areas that would be inundated by 3.5 feet of sea-level rise (combined with 100-year storm surge). The exposed assets include approximately $150 billion in residential and commercial property, 280 miles of coastal highways (including sections of Highway 1 and Highway 101), 30 wastewater treatment plants, 2 international airports (SFO and OAK, both built on fill in San Francisco Bay), and critical energy infrastructure (power plants, transmission lines, fuel storage). The San Francisco Bay shoreline is particularly vulnerable: much of the Bay's perimeter is built on fill or reclaimed marshland at elevations of 5 to 10 feet above current high tide, meaning that even modest sea-level rise combined with storms can produce flooding.

## The Adaptation Gap

California has produced extensive sea-level rise planning documents: the Ocean Protection Council guidance, the Coastal Commission's sea-level rise policy guidance, local coastal programs incorporating sea-level rise, and adaptation plans from individual cities and counties. The planning framework is among the most developed in the nation. Implementation is minimal. The state has no dedicated funding source for sea-level rise adaptation. The Coastal Conservancy's climate adaptation grants total approximately $50 million per year, a fraction of the need. No California community has implemented a managed retreat program (relocating structures and residents away from the coast), though several (Pacifica, Imperial Beach, Del Mar) have studied it.

The political economy of managed retreat is toxic. Coastal property owners whose homes and businesses would be relocated resist ferociously. Local governments that depend on property tax revenue from coastal parcels cannot afford to lose their tax base. The cost of buying out coastal property at fair market value would run to tens of billions of dollars. And the timeline is long enough (2050, 2100) that current officeholders can defer the decision without facing immediate consequences. The result is a planning paradigm that acknowledges the inevitability of retreat while funding and permitting continued development in the retreat zone.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's sea-level rise policy is a masterpiece of acknowledged but unaddressed risk. The state has produced the science, adopted the projections, published the guidance, and mandated the planning. It has not funded adaptation, prohibited development in inundation zones, or implemented retreat in a single community. The Coastal Commission continues to approve coastal development permits with conditions (sea-level rise acknowledgment, waiver of future armoring rights) that defer risk rather than prevent it. The 600,000 people in inundation zones are accumulating assets in locations the state's own science says will be underwater. Each year of deferred action increases the eventual cost of adaptation and the number of people who will need to be relocated.

The generous read is that sea-level rise adaptation is a multi-generational challenge and California is further along the preparation curve than any other state. The science is established, the projections are adopted, the planning framework exists, and the legal tools (Coastal Act authority, public trust doctrine, local land-use power) are available. The state is building natural infrastructure (wetland restoration, living shorelines, dune replenishment) that provides near-term resilience while buying time for longer-term decisions. The political difficulty of managed retreat is not unique to California; no jurisdiction anywhere in the world has implemented large-scale managed retreat from sea-level rise. California's task is to be first, and being first at something difficult takes time.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Ocean Protection Council and Coastal Commission publish sea-level rise projections and mapping. They do not publish a statewide inventory of adaptation projects (built, funded, planned), their costs, or their expected lifespan. The fundamental question, how much California has spent on sea-level rise adaptation versus how much it needs to spend, cannot be answered from publicly available state data because no agency tracks total adaptation expenditure.
