# California Counts: The Debt Owed
## What 1.6 million veterans, an $800 million CalVet budget, 8 state veterans homes, and a claims processing backlog that leaves benefits uncollected reveal about the services California provides to the population it repeatedly thanks and inconsistently serves

California is home to approximately 1.6 million veterans, the largest veteran population of any state. The population is aging: the median age of California veterans is approximately 65, and the Vietnam-era cohort (ages 70 to 85) constitutes the largest single group. The post-9/11 cohort (approximately 350,000) is younger and faces a different profile of service-connected needs: traumatic brain injury, post-traumatic stress disorder, military sexual trauma, and the transition challenges of reintegrating into a civilian economy where military skills are inconsistently recognized.

The California Department of Veterans Affairs (CalVet) operates on an annual budget of approximately $800 million and administers eight veterans homes (residential care facilities for aged and disabled veterans) with approximately 2,700 beds across the state. The homes provide skilled nursing, assisted living, memory care, and domiciliary care at costs subsidized by the state and partially reimbursed by the federal VA. CalVet also operates the County Veterans Service Officer (CVSO) support program, which funds county-level staff who assist veterans in filing claims for federal VA benefits: disability compensation, pension, healthcare, education (GI Bill), and home loan guarantees.

---

## The Benefits Gap

The CVSO program is CalVet's highest-return investment. County Veterans Service Officers helped California veterans recover approximately $800 million per year in federal VA benefits, primarily disability compensation. The state's investment in the CVSO program is approximately $15 million per year, a 50-to-1 return. The benefits recovery represents federal money flowing into California communities: disability compensation payments are spent locally on housing, food, healthcare, and other needs.

Despite the CVSO program's effectiveness, a significant share of California veterans with service-connected conditions do not file claims. The VA estimates that approximately 30 to 40 percent of eligible veterans nationwide do not receive the disability compensation they have earned, due to lack of awareness, difficulty navigating the claims process, stigma associated with claiming disability, or distrust of the VA bureaucracy. In California, the uncollected benefits are estimated at $1 billion to $2 billion per year, money that veterans have earned through military service and that would flow to California communities if claimed.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has the largest veteran population in the nation and provides services through a department whose $800 million budget is dominated by the cost of operating eight residential homes for an aging population, leaving minimal resources for the broader 1.6 million veterans who need claims assistance, mental health services, employment support, and housing. The 30 to 40 percent of eligible veterans who do not claim their benefits represent a failure of outreach and access that leaves billions of federal dollars uncollected. The eight veterans homes, while essential for the residents they serve, are facilities that serve 2,700 veterans out of 1.6 million, a coverage rate of 0.17 percent. CalVet's operational focus on residential care for the oldest veterans leaves the post-9/11 generation, which faces the highest rates of PTSD, TBI, and suicide, largely dependent on the federal VA and community-based organizations for the services the state does not provide at scale.

The generous read is that the CVSO program's 50-to-1 return on investment represents one of the most cost-effective government programs in the state. CalVet's partnership with county CVSOs has produced $800 million per year in federal benefits for California veterans and their families. The veterans homes provide essential long-term care for veterans who cannot afford private residential care and whose VA benefits do not cover the full cost. And the state's investment in veterans' services extends beyond CalVet: the Employment Development Department provides priority services to veterans, the California Housing Finance Agency administers the CalHFA Veterans Housing Loan program, and state hiring preferences for veterans facilitate public-sector employment. The system is fragmented. Its total investment in veterans is larger than CalVet's budget alone suggests.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CalVet publishes claims assistance data and veterans home census data. It does not publish a comprehensive needs assessment of California's 1.6 million veterans: how many have unmet healthcare needs, how many experience homelessness, how many have unclaimed benefits, how many are employed below their skill level, and how veterans' needs differ by era of service, geography, and demographics. The absence of a statewide veterans needs assessment means that CalVet allocates resources based on historical patterns and legislative direction rather than on data-driven identification of where the greatest needs exist.
