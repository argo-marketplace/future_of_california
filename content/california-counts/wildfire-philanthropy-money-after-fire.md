# California Counts: The Money After the Fire
## What $100 million through the California Community Foundation, $100 million through FireAid, 48,000 donors, 260 nonprofit grantees, a Latham and Watkins review, and a House Judiciary Committee investigation reveal about the philanthropic response to the January 2025 Los Angeles wildfires and the tension between speed and accountability when private money flows into public disaster

The January 2025 Los Angeles wildfires (the Palisades and Eaton fires primarily, along with several smaller blazes) destroyed more than 12,000 structures, killed at least 29 people, and caused an estimated $250 billion to $275 billion in total economic damage, making them the costliest natural disaster in American history. The philanthropic response was immediate and massive. Two vehicles dominated the private fundraising landscape: the California Community Foundation's Wildfire Recovery Fund, which raised more than $100 million from over 48,000 donors, and FireAid, a benefit concert organized by music industry executive Irving Azoff that also raised approximately $100 million. Together, these two efforts channeled approximately $200 million in private charitable contributions toward wildfire recovery in Los Angeles County.

The California Community Foundation (CCF), established in 1915, is one of the largest community foundations in the United States, with approximately $2.5 billion in assets under management. CCF launched its Wildfire Recovery Fund within days of the fires' ignition, leveraging its existing grant-making infrastructure, nonprofit relationships, and fiduciary systems to begin disbursing funds rapidly. CCF raised $20 million in the first five days, $30 million in the first two weeks, $50 million by early February, and surpassed $100 million by spring 2025. The foundation absorbs all administrative costs of the fund, meaning every dollar donated goes to grant-making rather than overhead.

---

## How CCF Deployed the Money

CCF's disbursement strategy prioritized speed in the first phase and targeted recovery in subsequent phases. Within two weeks of the fires, CCF had awarded over $30 million in grants to more than 200 nonprofit organizations delivering frontline services: emergency shelter, food distribution, mental health crisis counseling, legal aid for insurance claims, and direct cash assistance. A second round of approximately $15 million went to 84 additional organizations, expanding the reach to include small business support, workforce displacement assistance, immigration services for undocumented workers who lost employment, and environmental health monitoring.

The direct cash assistance component illustrates the model. CCF funded CORE (Community Organized Relief Effort) to deliver unrestricted cash grants to wildfire-affected households, with families in West Altadena receiving an average of $7,500 over three months, totaling $637,000 in that program alone. Cash transfers are the evidence-based gold standard in disaster relief: research from the Center for Disaster Philanthropy consistently finds that direct cash assistance allows survivors to address their most urgent needs (which vary by household) more efficiently than in-kind donations or voucher programs.

By January 2026, CCF published "Rising Together: A Year of Impact," an interactive report documenting where funds were deployed, who was served, and what obstacles remain. The report shows grants distributed across categories including housing assistance, case management, mental health and medical care, legal aid, childcare, animal welfare, food assistance, and community infrastructure rebuilding. CCF awarded grants to more than 260 nonprofit organizations over the year, reaching renters, seniors, children, people with disabilities, displaced workers, and small business owners across the fire-affected regions of Altadena, Pasadena, Pacific Palisades, and other LA County communities.

## FireAid and the Latham and Watkins Review

FireAid took a different path. The benefit concert (held at the Intuit Dome and Kia Forum in Inglewood on January 30, 2025, featuring performances by Billie Eilish, Lady Gaga, Stevie Wonder, the Red Hot Chili Peppers, and others) raised approximately $100 million. Actor Miles Teller told the concert audience that "all the money raised will go directly to people who need it now and long-term efforts to build it back." The phase one distribution of $50 million went to community-based organizations including CORE, Brotherhood Crusade, the Pasadena Community Foundation, Latino Community Foundation, Team Rubicon, Heal the Bay, and others, with grants of $100,000 or more to each.

In July 2025, Representative Kevin Kiley (R-Rocklin) sent a letter to FireAid requesting a detailed breakdown of all nonprofit recipients, amounts, dates, and purposes of expenditures. Kiley subsequently urged U.S. Attorney General Pam Bondi to open a Department of Justice investigation, alleging that funds had been misallocated. FireAid responded by retaining Latham and Watkins, with partner Makan Delrahim (former chief of the DOJ Antitrust Division during President Trump's first term) leading the review. The Latham and Watkins review examined the disbursement of funds and, according to FireAid, cleared the organization of wrongdoing.

The House Judiciary Committee, however, reached a different conclusion. On January 6, 2026, the committee released an interim staff report titled "FireAid Apparently Misused Charitable Donations by Funding Illegal Aliens, Podcasters, and Non-Profits' Administrative Costs." The report, produced under Chairman Jim Jordan, alleged that FireAid donations had been used for purposes "wholly unrelated to fire relief," including Native American voter outreach, political advocacy organizations, podcast production, ecological restoration projects (described in the report as "fungus planting"), and more than $500,000 in nonprofit staff salaries and bonuses. The committee report argued that these expenditures contradicted FireAid's representations to donors that funds would be used "solely for direct community support for relief, recovery and rebuilding, not for overhead or salaries."

## The Administrative Overhead Question

The contrast between CCF and FireAid illuminates the structural tension in disaster philanthropy. CCF, as an established community foundation with existing infrastructure, could absorb administrative costs into its endowment-funded operations and deploy grants through a network of vetted nonprofit partners built over decades. FireAid, as an ad hoc entity created for a single event, had no existing grant-making infrastructure, no pre-established grantee relationships, and no fiduciary systems. It relied on intermediary organizations to identify recipients and distribute funds, and those intermediaries had their own operational costs, programmatic priorities, and definitions of "wildfire relief" that did not always align with donor expectations of "direct relief to fire victims."

The question of what counts as wildfire relief is at the center of the dispute. The House Judiciary Committee treated "direct relief" narrowly: cash payments to fire victims, rebuilding assistance, and emergency services. FireAid and its grantees defined "recovery" more broadly: immigration services for undocumented workers who lost homes and jobs in the fires, legal aid for tenants facing displacement, community organizing to ensure equitable rebuilding, and ecological restoration of fire-damaged landscapes. The broader definition is consistent with disaster recovery best practices as articulated by the Center for Disaster Philanthropy, FEMA, and academic disaster researchers. It is also less politically intuitive to a donor who wrote a check expecting it to rebuild a house.

The administrative overhead question applies to all disaster philanthropy, including CCF's. When CCF grants $100,000 to a nonprofit that provides mental health counseling to fire survivors, a portion of that grant covers the nonprofit's rent, utilities, insurance, therapist salaries, and administrative staff. That is not waste; it is the cost of delivering services. But the line between "programmatic expense" and "overhead" is genuinely blurry, and the donor who sees "100 percent of donations go to grants" may not realize that the grants themselves fund organizations with their own overhead structures. CCF's transparency (absorbing its own administrative costs, publishing detailed impact reports, naming grantees) mitigates this concern. FireAid's lack of comparable transparency infrastructure amplified it.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that the philanthropic response to the January 2025 fires exposed the limitations of private charity as disaster infrastructure. Two hundred million dollars sounds enormous, but it is less than 0.1 percent of the estimated $250 billion in total damage. The money arrived quickly but was distributed through a patchwork of organizations with varying levels of accountability, transparency, and alignment with donor intent. FireAid's experience demonstrates that celebrity-driven fundraising can raise vast sums but lacks the institutional infrastructure to deploy them responsibly, inviting political exploitation when expenditures do not match public expectations. The House Judiciary Committee's investigation, whatever its merits on the facts, was transparently partisan: Republican members used wildfire philanthropy to attack organizations serving immigrant communities and progressive constituencies. The fire victims became collateral in a political conflict that had nothing to do with their recovery.

The generous read is that CCF's response represents disaster philanthropy at its best: an established institution with deep community relationships, fiduciary expertise, and the capacity to deploy $100 million in grants to 260 organizations within a year, with full transparency and zero administrative cost to donors. CCF's "Rising Together" report provides an unprecedented level of accountability, documenting where funds went, who was served, and what challenges remain. The foundation's model, channeling philanthropy through a permanent community institution rather than an ad hoc vehicle, is the evidence-based approach that disaster recovery experts recommend. The fact that CCF raised $100 million from 48,000 donors (an average of approximately $2,000 per donor, though the distribution is heavily skewed by large gifts) reflects genuine community generosity. The money was raised, the money was spent, and the survivors were served.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CCF's "Rising Together" report documents grants awarded and populations served. It does not yet document long-term outcomes: how many fire survivors have returned to stable housing, how many small businesses have reopened, how many displaced workers have found employment, and how the fire-affected communities compare to their pre-fire baselines on measures of economic well-being, mental health, and community cohesion. The fundamental question of disaster philanthropy, whether the money made a lasting difference, requires longitudinal data that will not be available for years.
