# California Counts: The Digital Disaster
## What $31 billion in EDD fraud, an 18-month procurement cycle, a COBOL mainframe from 1986, and 130 failed IT projects reveal about the state that invented Silicon Valley but cannot build a working website

In 2020, the California Employment Development Department paid an estimated $31 billion in fraudulent unemployment insurance claims, approximately 18 percent of all claims processed during the pandemic. Inmates, identity theft rings, and international fraud operations exploited a system running on a COBOL mainframe originally deployed in 1986, using an identity verification process that required in-person appointments at offices that were closed due to COVID. The fraud was not a sophisticated attack on a hardened system. It was an opportunistic raid on a system so antiquated that it could not distinguish between a legitimate claimant and a bot submitting thousands of applications with stolen Social Security numbers. The state that hosts Google, Apple, and Meta could not operate a benefits website.

EDD is the most visible failure. It is not the only one. The California Department of Technology's project tracking shows that since 2000, approximately 130 state IT projects have been classified as challenged, failed, or significantly over budget and behind schedule. The Financial Information System for California (FI$Cal), intended to replace the state's legacy accounting system, was budgeted at $617 million in 2005 and has cost more than $1.2 billion, with deployment issues persisting a decade after go-live. The Department of Motor Vehicles' IT modernization, initiated in 2019, has consumed hundreds of millions and produced incremental rather than transformative improvements. The child welfare case management system (CWS-CARES), intended to replace the 1990s-era system used by 58 counties, was cancelled in 2015 after spending $243 million with nothing deployable.

## The Procurement Trap

California's IT procurement process, governed by the State Administrative Manual and overseen by the Department of Technology, requires an average of 12 to 18 months from project approval to vendor contract execution. The process involves a feasibility study report, a project approval lifecycle gate review, a competitive solicitation (typically a Request for Proposal), an evaluation and scoring process, a protest period, and contract negotiation. Each step involves review by the Department of Technology, the Department of Finance, and in some cases the Legislature's Joint Legislative Budget Committee.

The process was designed to prevent waste and corruption. It achieves neither. The timeline ensures that by the time a vendor is selected, the technology landscape has shifted and the original requirements are partially obsolete. The RFP format favors large systems integrators (Accenture, Deloitte, IBM, Infosys) who can navigate the procurement process over innovative smaller firms who cannot absorb the cost of a year-long sales cycle. The fixed-price, waterfall delivery model that procurement rules incentivize is incompatible with modern agile software development, which requires iterative delivery, changing requirements, and continuous user feedback.

The Department of Technology's annual budget is approximately $900 million. The state's total IT spending, including department-level technology costs, is approximately $10 billion per year. The spending produces operational technology, maintaining existing systems, far more than transformative technology (building new capabilities). An estimated 60 to 70 percent of the state's IT budget is spent maintaining legacy systems that would cost less to replace than to maintain, but replacement requires capital investment that the annual budget cycle does not accommodate.

## The Talent Gap (Again)

The state employs approximately 7,000 IT workers across all departments. The vacancy rate for IT classifications is approximately 25 percent, the highest of any occupational group in state service. The salary range for a state IT Specialist I (the entry-level classification) is approximately $75,000 to $100,000. The equivalent position at a Bay Area technology company pays $130,000 to $180,000 in total compensation. Senior IT managers in state service earn $140,000 to $170,000. The equivalent role at a technology company pays $250,000 to $400,000.

The gap is not just compensation. State IT workers operate within a procurement and governance framework that would be unrecognizable to their private sector counterparts. A state developer who identifies a bug in a production system cannot deploy a fix without a change management review. A state architect who recommends a cloud migration must navigate a procurement process that takes longer than the migration itself. The institutional environment selects for compliance rather than innovation, patience rather than speed, and process adherence rather than outcome delivery. The talented IT professionals who enter state service often leave within two to three years, citing the pace and constraints.

The California Office of Digital Innovation (ODI), created in 2020, was intended to bring modern technology practices (human-centered design, agile development, cloud-native architecture) to state government. ODI has demonstrated results on specific projects (redesigning Alpha.ca.gov, improving the COVID vaccination portal), but its staff of approximately 50 cannot transform a state technology enterprise of 7,000 workers and $10 billion in annual spending. ODI is a lifeboat. The ship needs a new engine.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has spent more than $10 billion per year on information technology for decades and has produced systems that are slower, less reliable, and less secure than what a competent startup could build in months for a fraction of the cost. The $31 billion EDD fraud was the consequence of a system that every audit had identified as a failure and no governor had prioritized replacing. The 130 failed IT projects represent billions in wasted public funds and years of delayed services. The procurement system is designed to prevent risk and instead guarantees failure by selecting for vendors who can survive an 18-month sales cycle rather than vendors who can build working software. The state cannot recruit or retain IT talent because it pays half what the private sector offers and imposes working conditions that drive away anyone capable of producing results elsewhere. California's state technology infrastructure is a monument to institutional inertia: the systems from the 1980s still run because no one has built replacements, the procurement system prevents building replacements quickly, and the talent that could build them is across the Bay earning three times as much.

The generous read is that government IT is hard everywhere, and California's failures, while spectacular, are not unique. The federal Healthcare.gov launch in 2013 was as disastrous as anything California has produced. The IRS runs systems as old as EDD's. The VA's health records modernization has cost billions and is years behind schedule. What distinguishes California is scale: the state serves 40 million people through systems that must handle volumes comparable to national governments. ODI, USDS-inspired modernization efforts, and the incremental cloud migration underway at multiple departments represent genuine progress. The EDD has invested $2 billion in system modernization since the fraud crisis, including a new identity verification system and fraud detection capabilities. The CDT is implementing a cloud-first policy. The trajectory is toward modern systems. The pace is inadequate to the urgency.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CDT publishes a project portfolio dashboard that tracks major IT projects by status. It does not publish a comprehensive assessment of the state's legacy system inventory: how many systems are running on unsupported platforms, how many are maintained by a single knowledgeable employee, and how many are one failure away from a service outage affecting millions. The technical debt across California's state government, the accumulated cost of deferred modernization, has been estimated informally at $30 to $50 billion but has never been formally assessed.
