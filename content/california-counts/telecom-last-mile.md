# California Counts: The Last Mile
## What $6 billion in broadband investment, 2 million unserved households, the California Public Utilities Commission's $2 billion middle-mile network, and a federal broadband map that overcounts coverage reveal about the telecommunications infrastructure California is building to connect the residents the market left behind

California has committed approximately $6 billion to broadband infrastructure since 2021, combining state funds (approximately $4 billion from the general fund and bond measures) and federal funds (approximately $2 billion from the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act's Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment program). The investment targets the approximately 2 million California households (approximately 15 percent) that lack access to broadband internet at speeds of 100 megabits per second download and 20 megabits per second upload, the FCC's current broadband standard. The unserved households are concentrated in rural areas (the Sierra Nevada, the North Coast, the desert communities of San Bernardino and Imperial counties), tribal lands, and low-income urban neighborhoods where the commercial return on infrastructure investment is insufficient to attract private capital.

The centerpiece of California's broadband strategy is the Middle-Mile Broadband Initiative, a $3.9 billion project to build approximately 10,000 miles of open-access fiber-optic backbone connecting communities across the state. The middle-mile network (the long-haul fiber that connects local networks to internet exchange points) is being built by the California Department of Technology in partnership with third-party operators. The network will not deliver service directly to homes; it will provide the backbone to which local "last mile" providers (ISPs, municipal networks, tribal networks) can connect to offer retail service. The middle-mile network is designed to be operational by 2028.

---

## The Last-Mile Problem

The middle-mile network solves the backbone problem but does not solve the last-mile problem: the connection from the local network node to the individual household. Last-mile connections in rural areas cost $3,000 to $10,000 per household (for buried fiber or fixed wireless equipment), compared to $500 to $1,500 in urban areas. The cost is driven by distance (rural homes are far apart), terrain (mountains, forests, desert), and permitting (environmental review, right-of-way access, Caltrans encroachment permits for lines crossing state highways).

The CPUC administers the California Advanced Services Fund (CASF), which provides grants and loans to ISPs and local governments for last-mile construction. CASF has approximately $500 million in available funding, sufficient to connect approximately 50,000 to 150,000 households, a fraction of the 2 million unserved. The remaining gap will require additional state and federal funding, private investment (which flows to areas where subscriber density supports a commercial return), and alternative technologies (fixed wireless, low-earth-orbit satellite) for the most remote and costly locations.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has spent decades allowing telecommunications companies to cherry-pick profitable markets (urban and suburban areas) while leaving rural and low-income communities unserved, and is now spending $6 billion in public funds to build the infrastructure that the private sector declined to build. The middle-mile network is a government-built backbone that will benefit private ISPs (who will use it to offer retail service) without guaranteeing affordable retail pricing, service quality, or universal coverage. The $6 billion investment addresses the infrastructure gap but not the affordability gap: broadband service costs $50 to $100 per month, an expense that low-income households may not be able to sustain even if physical infrastructure reaches their home. Connecting the last mile is a necessary condition for digital equity but not a sufficient one.

The generous read is that the $6 billion investment represents the largest state broadband commitment in the nation and will transform connectivity in communities that the private market abandoned. The middle-mile network creates open-access infrastructure that enables competition: multiple ISPs can use the same backbone, reducing the monopoly pricing that prevails in many rural areas. The BEAD program includes affordability requirements (recipients must offer a low-cost option for qualifying households). And the pandemic demonstrated that broadband is not a luxury but a utility: without it, children cannot attend school, workers cannot access jobs, patients cannot access telehealth, and communities cannot participate in the digital economy. The $6 billion makes broadband a public infrastructure investment, like roads and water, rather than a private product dependent on market returns.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The FCC publishes broadband coverage maps based on ISP self-reporting, which systematically overstates coverage (an ISP that serves one household in a census block can report the entire block as served). California's CPUC maintains its own broadband map, which is more granular but still depends on provider-reported data. The true number of unserved and underserved households (those with nominal access but speeds below the standard or prices above affordability) is not known with precision. The gap between the map and the reality is the gap between reported coverage and experienced access.
