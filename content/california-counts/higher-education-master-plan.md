# California Counts: The Diploma and the Debt
## What $0 to $15,891 in tuition, 900,000 students, a 1960 Master Plan, and a $47 billion endowment reveal about the public university system that was supposed to be free

The University of California charged no tuition for 108 years. From its founding in 1868 through the governorship of Ronald Reagan, who in 1970 imposed the first student fees on a system that had been free to every eligible Californian, the UC operated on the principle that public higher education was a public investment, not a private purchase. The California Master Plan for Higher Education, adopted in 1960 under Governor Pat Brown, codified the architecture: the University of California would accept the top 12.5 percent of high school graduates and conduct the state's research, the California State University would accept the top 33 percent and provide professional and technical education, and the community colleges would accept everyone. Tuition at all three tiers would be free or nominal. The state would pay because the state would benefit. Sixty-six years later, UC tuition and fees for a California resident are $15,891 per year. CSU tuition is $7,488. Community college fees are $1,380 per year, the lowest in the nation but no longer free. The Master Plan's promise of universal access through free public higher education has been replaced by a system that charges students what the state will not appropriate, and finances the gap with debt that students carry into a labor market where a bachelor's degree is simultaneously more necessary and less sufficient than at any point in the plan's history.

## The Funding Inversion

The Master Plan assumed that the state would fund public higher education as a core obligation, comparable to K-12 education or public safety. For the first three decades, it did. In fiscal year 1980-81, state general fund appropriations to the UC and CSU systems combined represented approximately 14 percent of the state budget. By fiscal year 2024-25, the combined share had fallen to approximately 7 percent. The absolute dollars have increased, from roughly $4 billion in 1980 to approximately $22 billion in 2025, but not proportionally to enrollment growth, inflation, or the expanding scope of university operations.

The decline is not a mystery. Proposition 13 (1978) constrained property tax revenue. Proposition 98 (1988) guaranteed K-14 education a minimum share of the general fund, effectively ring-fencing approximately 40 percent of discretionary spending for K-12 and community colleges and leaving higher education to compete with prisons, healthcare, and social services for the remainder. The prison boom of the 1980s and 1990s, during which California built 23 new prisons, diverted billions from the general fund. Medi-Cal enrollment doubled and then doubled again. Each new obligation compressed the share available for higher education, which unlike K-12 had no constitutional guarantee and unlike prisons had no court orders mandating expenditure.

The universities responded by raising tuition. UC tuition was $0 until 1970, approximately $1,820 in 1990, $3,964 in 2000, $10,302 in 2010, and $15,891 in 2025. The increases are sharpest during recessions, when state appropriations are cut and the universities raise tuition to backfill the loss. The pattern is visible in the data: tuition jumps of 30 to 40 percent during the 2001-03 downturn, the 2008-11 Great Recession, and the initial pandemic period. Between downturns, tuition rises moderately or holds flat. The ratchet never reverses. No governor has reduced UC tuition. The highest historical tuition becomes the new floor.

The funding inversion is now complete. In 1990, the state provided approximately 78 percent of UC's core operating revenue and students provided 22 percent. By 2025, the proportions are roughly reversed: students and their families (through tuition, fees, and room and board) provide approximately 52 percent of core revenue, and the state provides approximately 48 percent. The University of California is no longer a state-funded university. It is a state-affiliated university funded primarily by its customers.

## The Systems: UC, CSU, and Community Colleges by the Numbers

The University of California enrolls approximately 295,000 students across 10 campuses, including five medical schools and three law schools. It employs approximately 230,000 people, making it one of the largest employers in the state. Its annual budget exceeds $47 billion including medical centers, research grants, and auxiliary operations. Its research expenditures of approximately $8 billion per year rank it first among all university systems globally. UC faculty have won 71 Nobel Prizes. Its medical centers generate approximately $18 billion in annual revenue. The UC system is, by most measures, the greatest public research university system ever created. It is also the most expensive for students of any public system of its caliber: Berkeley, UCLA, UC San Diego, and UC Davis all charge total costs of attendance (tuition, fees, room, board, books, transportation) exceeding $40,000 per year for in-state students and $75,000 for out-of-state students.

The California State University enrolls approximately 460,000 students across 23 campuses, making it the largest four-year public university system in the nation. CSU's mission is professional and applied education: teaching, nursing, engineering, business, social work, and criminal justice. It produces more bachelor's degrees than any university system in the country. It is also chronically underfunded relative to its enrollment. Per-student state funding at CSU is approximately $12,500, compared to roughly $24,000 at UC (excluding medical center and research revenues). CSU faculty earn 20 to 30 percent less than UC faculty in comparable disciplines. The CSU system has deferred approximately $6.4 billion in maintenance and capital needs across its 23 campuses, a backlog that manifests in aging laboratories, overcrowded classrooms, and deteriorating student housing.

The California Community Colleges, with 116 campuses and approximately 1.8 million students, constitute the largest system of higher education in the nation. Community colleges serve the broadest population: recent high school graduates, working adults, career changers, immigrants, and students remediating academic deficiencies before transferring to four-year institutions. Approximately 40 percent of UC graduates and 60 percent of CSU graduates begin at a community college. The system's annual budget of approximately $19 billion is funded primarily through Proposition 98 general fund allocations and local property taxes. Per-student funding is approximately $10,500, though this figure understates the resource challenge because a majority of community college students attend part-time, and funding formulas have historically rewarded enrollment counts rather than completion.

## The Debt Burden

The average UC graduate carries approximately $19,800 in student loan debt at graduation, below the national average of approximately $29,000. This figure is lower than might be expected because UC's financial aid system, anchored by the Blue and Gold Opportunity Plan and expanded Cal Grant programs, covers tuition and fees for students with family incomes below $80,000 and provides partial grants for families earning up to $200,000. Approximately 55 percent of UC undergraduates graduate with no student loan debt.

The average obscures the distribution. Students from middle-income families, those earning $80,000 to $200,000, receive less grant aid and borrow more. Students who take longer than four years to graduate (the six-year graduation rate at UC is approximately 87 percent, meaning 13 percent take longer or do not finish) accumulate more debt. Students who attend UC but do not complete a degree carry debt without the earnings premium that a degree provides. The worst outcome in the higher education system is debt without a diploma, and approximately 9 percent of UC students and 38 percent of CSU students who begin a bachelor's degree do not complete one within six years.

At CSU, the average debt at graduation is approximately $17,600, lower than UC because tuition is lower. But CSU graduates earn less on average than UC graduates, meaning the debt-to-income ratio can be comparable or higher. CSU's six-year graduation rate of approximately 62 percent means that more than one in three students who start at CSU do not finish within six years, many of them accumulating debt along the way. The students most likely to leave without a degree are first-generation college students, low-income students, and students of color, the populations the Master Plan was designed to serve.

Community college students borrow less, but their completion rates are the lowest in the system. Approximately 48 percent of community college students who state a goal of earning a degree or transferring succeed within six years. The AB 705 reforms, which reduced remedial coursework requirements, and the Associate Degree for Transfer pathway, which guarantees CSU admission for students who complete a prescribed curriculum, have improved transfer rates. But the fundamental challenge remains: community college students are disproportionately working adults with family responsibilities, attending part-time, at institutions with per-student resources one-third of what UC provides.

## The Out-of-State Subsidy

Facing state funding declines, the University of California turned to a lucrative alternative revenue source: out-of-state and international students who pay tuition and fees of approximately $48,000 to $52,000 per year, roughly three times the in-state rate. Out-of-state enrollment at UC grew from approximately 5 percent in 2000 to approximately 23 percent by 2018. At UC Berkeley and UCLA, the most selective campuses, out-of-state students represented nearly 25 percent of undergraduates.

The revenue was significant: each out-of-state student generated approximately $30,000 to $35,000 more in tuition revenue than an in-state student, net of financial aid. Across the system, out-of-state tuition revenue contributed approximately $1.2 billion per year to UC's operating budget, money that subsidized in-state student financial aid, faculty salaries, and campus operations. The UC administration argued that out-of-state students were not displacing California residents because enrollment was growing overall. Critics, including the state legislature and the State Auditor, argued that admission was zero-sum at selective campuses: every spot given to an out-of-state applicant was a spot denied to a California resident.

In 2017, following a State Auditor report that criticized the growth in nonresident enrollment, UC adopted a systemwide cap on nonresident undergraduate enrollment at each campus. The cap was set at the 2017-18 level for most campuses. Subsequent policy adjustments reduced the target to 18 percent of new enrollment. The effect has been to stabilize nonresident enrollment but not reverse it. The revenue dependency remains: the approximately $1 billion in additional tuition from nonresident students cannot easily be replaced by state appropriations, and no governor has offered to do so.

## The Endowment Paradox

The University of California's combined endowment and investment portfolio totals approximately $47 billion, making it one of the largest institutional investment pools in the world. The UC Investments office manages the portfolio, which includes the endowment, the pension fund, and working capital. The endowment portion, approximately $24 billion, ranks among the top ten university endowments in the nation.

The endowment is distributed unevenly. UC San Francisco's endowment exceeds $6 billion. UC Berkeley's is approximately $3.5 billion. Some newer campuses, including UC Merced, have endowments below $200 million. The endowment generates approximately $1.5 billion per year in spendable income under the standard 5 percent payout policy, but much of that income is restricted to specific purposes: named professorships, scholarship funds, research centers, and capital projects designated by donors. The unrestricted portion available for general operations is a fraction of the total.

The paradox is that the University of California is simultaneously one of the wealthiest and most underfunded public institutions in the country. Its investment portfolio rivals those of Harvard and Stanford, but its per-student state appropriation is below the national average for public research universities. It raises $5 billion per year in philanthropy but cannot fund competitive graduate student stipends without external grants. It operates five medical centers that generate $18 billion in revenue but cannot maintain the roofs of its classroom buildings. The wealth exists in silos: medical center revenues stay in medical centers, endowment income stays with donors' designated purposes, research grants fund research. The general operating budget that pays for undergraduate instruction, the core mission of a public university, is the part that depends on state appropriations and tuition, and it is the part that has been squeezed for 40 years.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California broke the most important promise in American public education. The Master Plan guaranteed that any qualified Californian could attend a world-class public university for free. That promise lasted one generation. Today, the system charges $15,891 at UC and $7,488 at CSU, funds the gap with student debt, supplements revenue with full-fare out-of-state students, and produces graduation rates at CSU and community colleges that mean more students leave without degrees than with them. The funding inversion, from 78 percent state-funded to 52 percent student-funded at UC, represents a generational transfer of cost from taxpayers to students, from the old to the young, from the voting public that benefits from an educated workforce to the individuals who must finance their own training. The state spends more per prisoner ($132,000 per year) than per UC student ($24,000 in state funds), a comparison that overstates the disparity (prisons include housing and healthcare) but captures the revealed priorities: California found the money for 23 prisons in 20 years but could not maintain free tuition at 10 universities.

The generous read is that the California public university system, despite its funding challenges, remains the most accessible and highest-quality public higher education system in the world. UC's financial aid program ensures that the majority of students pay less than the sticker price and that low-income students attend tuition-free. UC's research output, Nobel laureates, patent portfolio, and economic impact justify its claim as the greatest public university system ever built. CSU produces more graduates than any system in the nation, feeding the state's workforce with teachers, nurses, engineers, and social workers. The community colleges serve 1.8 million students with open enrollment, low fees, and a transfer pathway that produces 40 percent of UC's graduates. Total state funding for higher education has increased in absolute terms every decade. The Master Plan's structural architecture, three differentiated tiers with guaranteed pathways between them, remains intact and has been replicated globally. The promise of free tuition was aspirational and perhaps unsustainable for a system that now serves 2.5 million students in a state whose budget faces competing demands that did not exist in 1960. What replaced it is not nothing. It is a financial aid system that approximates the Master Plan's intent, if not its letter, by ensuring that ability to pay is not the primary barrier to access.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The UC, CSU, and community college systems each publish enrollment, graduation, demographic, and financial data. What none of them publishes comprehensively is outcomes data that connects degrees to earnings, employment, and economic mobility. The Employment Development Department's wage matching data, linked to student records, can show what graduates earn five and ten years after enrollment. These data, produced through the Cal-PASS Plus system and the Cradle-to-Career Data System, show wide variation: a UC Berkeley computer science graduate earns a median of $150,000 within five years, while a CSU social work graduate earns $48,000. Whether the education caused the earnings differential, or whether it reflects pre-existing differences in talent, family background, and field selection, is the question the data cannot answer.

The data does not capture the students who never enroll. The Master Plan guarantees a place for the top 12.5 percent at UC and the top 33 percent at CSU. For the remaining two-thirds of high school graduates, the pathway runs through community colleges, where completion rates hover near 48 percent. The students who could have enrolled but did not, deterred by cost, complexity, family obligation, or the belief that college is not for them, are invisible in the system's statistics. They are also the population whose lifetime earnings are most affected by the decision, and whose absence from the data makes it impossible to calculate the true social return on public higher education investment.

The data also does not capture what the Master Plan's architects would recognize. Clark Kerr, the UC president who designed the 1960 plan, envisioned a system where the tiers were complementary and the boundaries permeable: a student who started at a community college could transfer to UC without financial penalty. Today, the financial penalty is real. A student who spends two years at a community college ($2,760 in fees) and two years at UC ($31,782 in tuition) pays $34,542. A student who attends UC for four years pays $63,564. The transfer student saves money but navigates a transfer process that, despite improvements, loses approximately 20 percent of earned credits in the transition, extending time to degree and increasing total cost. The Master Plan is an architectural drawing. The building that was actually constructed has the same number of floors but different dimensions, different materials, and a bill that Clark Kerr would not recognize.

---

The most audacious promise in American public education was that California would educate anyone who was qualified, at any level, for free. That promise built the workforce that built Silicon Valley, the aerospace industry, the agricultural research system, and the biomedical complex that made California the world's fifth-largest economy. The state made the investment and reaped the return. Then it stopped making the investment and continued reaping the return, for a while, on the accumulated human capital of previous generations. The question now is whether a system that charges $15,891 per year, graduates 62 percent of its CSU students, and depends on out-of-state tuition to balance its books can produce the next generation's return, or whether the Master Plan's broken promise will compound, like the pension obligations it resembles, until the deficit is too large to close. Clark Kerr's plan assumed that California would always choose to invest in its future. The data suggests that for 40 years, California has chosen to consume it instead.
