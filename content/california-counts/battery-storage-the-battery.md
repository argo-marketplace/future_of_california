# California Counts: The Battery
## What 10 GW of installed battery storage, a 7,000 percent increase since 2019, the duck curve that drives deployment, and a 4-hour discharge duration that covers the evening peak but not the multi-day gaps reveal about the storage technology California is scaling faster than any jurisdiction on Earth

California has approximately 10 gigawatts of grid-scale battery storage installed and operating as of early 2026, more than any other state and approximately 40 percent of the U.S. total. The growth has been extraordinary: from approximately 150 megawatts in 2019 to 10,000 megawatts in 2026, a roughly 7,000 percent increase in six years. The vast majority is lithium-ion battery systems with a discharge duration of four hours, meaning a 100 MW system can discharge at full capacity for four hours before depleting.

The deployment is driven by the "duck curve," CAISO's term for the daily electricity demand shape that solar power creates. During midday, solar generation floods the grid with cheap power (sometimes driving wholesale prices to zero or below). In the evening, as solar output drops and demand peaks (air conditioning, cooking, lighting), the grid must rapidly ramp other resources to fill the gap. Batteries charge during the midday solar surplus and discharge during the evening peak, smoothing the curve and providing the fast-ramping capacity that natural gas peaker plants previously supplied. In 2025, battery storage displaced approximately 5,000 MW of natural gas peaker capacity during peak evening hours, reducing both carbon emissions and ratepayer costs for the most expensive electricity hours of the day.

---

## The Duration Gap

Four-hour batteries solve the daily duck curve problem. They do not solve the multi-day or seasonal storage challenge. California's grid faces extended periods (winter storms, wildfire smoke events, multi-day heat waves) when solar output is reduced for days or weeks and demand is elevated. A four-hour battery system fully discharges by 10 PM and cannot provide power through the night or into the next day if the following day's solar generation is impaired.

Long-duration energy storage (LDES), systems that can discharge for 8 to 100 hours or more, is the missing technology. Candidates include iron-air batteries, compressed air, liquid air, gravity-based systems, green hydrogen, and pumped hydro storage. California has approximately 4,000 MW of existing pumped hydro (primarily the Helms and Castaic facilities), but new pumped hydro faces siting, permitting, and environmental constraints that make expansion slow and expensive. The CEC and CPUC have set targets for LDES procurement, but commercial-scale deployment of non-pumped-hydro LDES technologies is two to five years away at best.

---

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has deployed 10 GW of battery storage to solve a problem (the evening peak) that is a subset of the reliability problem the grid faces. Four-hour batteries are a lucrative investment for developers (the price arbitrage between midday solar surplus and evening peak pays for the battery within 5 to 10 years) but they do not provide the multi-day, seasonal, or emergency backup that a grid retiring natural gas requires. California's storage portfolio is overwhelmingly a single technology (lithium-ion), a single duration (four hours), and a single use case (daily peak shifting). The grid needs storage diversity: short-duration for daily cycling, medium-duration for multi-day events, and long-duration for seasonal gaps. The current deployment is impressive in scale and speed but insufficient in scope.

The generous read is that California built 10 GW of battery storage in six years, an achievement that energy planners a decade ago considered impossible. The storage deployment has materially reduced California's dependence on natural gas peaker plants, lowered electricity costs during peak hours, and demonstrated that renewables-plus-storage can provide reliable electricity at scale. Battery costs have declined approximately 90 percent since 2010, and four-hour systems now compete with natural gas on cost. The duration gap is real but closing: iron-air batteries (Form Energy) and other LDES technologies are beginning commercial deployment. California's aggressive storage procurement, combined with its renewable portfolio standard, is creating the market that drives cost reductions and technology maturation. The state is not waiting for perfect storage to build storage. It is building what exists and improving as technology advances.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CAISO publishes real-time battery storage charge and discharge data, and the CEC tracks installed capacity. Neither publishes a comprehensive analysis of storage adequacy: how much storage, at what durations, is required to maintain grid reliability as natural gas retires. The CPUC's Integrated Resource Plan includes storage procurement targets, but the modeling assumptions (solar output variability, demand growth, technology costs, retirement schedules) are contested and produce widely varying estimates.
