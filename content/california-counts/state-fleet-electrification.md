# California Counts: The State Fleet
## What 70,000 state-owned vehicles, a 2025 zero-emission purchase mandate, $300 million in estimated charging infrastructure needs, and a Department of General Services fleet management office that has been buying EVs faster than it can plug them in reveal about California's promise to lead on electrification

California owns and operates approximately 70,000 vehicles, making the state's fleet one of the largest employer-owned fleets in the United States. The Department of General Services manages this inventory through its Office of Fleet and Asset Management, overseeing everything from sedans driven by regulators to trucks operated by Caltrans maintenance crews. In 2020, Governor Newsom signed Executive Order N-79-20, directing state agencies to purchase only zero-emission light-duty vehicles by 2025. The state that mandated EV adoption for 40 million residents was also mandating it for itself. The data on how that commitment has translated into actual fleet transformation is more complicated than the executive order.

---

## The Fleet Inventory: 70,000 Vehicles Across 150 Agencies

The state fleet is not a single coherent inventory. It is distributed across approximately 150 departments and agencies, each of which manages its own day-to-day operations but procures vehicles through DGS contracts. The breakdown by class matters: the zero-emission purchase mandate covers light-duty vehicles, which account for roughly 50,000 of the 70,000 total. The remaining 20,000 vehicles are medium- and heavy-duty, including the trucks, buses, and specialized equipment that are harder and more expensive to electrify and are governed by a separate regulatory timeline.

The state spends approximately $200 million to $250 million per year on vehicle acquisition, fuel, and maintenance combined. Fuel costs alone run approximately $60 million to $80 million per year, depending on the price of gasoline and the number of miles driven. Fleet mileage across all departments is estimated at approximately 550 million miles per year. At the state's typical fleet fuel economy of roughly 25 miles per gallon, that implies annual fuel consumption of approximately 22 million gallons.

The cost per mile comparison between gasoline and electric vehicles is where the long-term economics favor electrification clearly. A gasoline vehicle costs approximately $0.14 to $0.18 per mile in fuel at $4.00 per gallon. An EV charged at commercial electricity rates in California costs approximately $0.03 to $0.05 per mile. Maintenance costs favor EVs further: no oil changes, fewer brake replacements due to regenerative braking, fewer moving parts. The California Department of Finance has estimated that over a 10-year ownership period, an EV costs approximately $9,000 to $15,000 less per vehicle in fuel and maintenance than a comparable gasoline vehicle. Multiplied across 50,000 light-duty vehicles, the total long-term savings from full electrification would approach $450 million to $750 million over a decade.

## The Progress: EVs Purchased, Chargers Not Built

The California Air Resources Board, which enforces fleet standards, reported that state agencies were purchasing EVs at an accelerating rate through 2023 and 2024. By the end of fiscal year 2023-24, approximately 15,000 of the state's light-duty vehicles were zero-emission, representing roughly 30 percent of the light-duty fleet. The pace of EV acquisition has averaged approximately 2,500 vehicles per year since 2020, but achieving 100 percent of new purchases requires replacing roughly 5,000 to 7,000 vehicles per year as older vehicles age out, and the state's vehicle replacement cycle averages seven to nine years.

The charging infrastructure deficit is where the program has lagged most visibly. DGS estimated in 2023 that full electrification of the light-duty fleet would require approximately 10,000 to 12,000 charging ports at state-owned facilities, including offices, garages, and field stations. As of 2024, approximately 3,500 charging ports had been installed. The gap of roughly 7,000 ports represents an estimated $280 million to $350 million in capital investment, based on average installed costs of $40,000 to $50,000 per Level 2 commercial charging station and $80,000 to $120,000 per DC fast charger.

The infrastructure lag creates a practical problem: EV purchases have outpaced charging availability at some state facilities, meaning vehicles that cannot be charged overnight at their home base must be charged elsewhere, reducing their operational utility and negating part of the cost savings. Several field offices, particularly in rural districts of Caltrans and the Department of Fish and Wildlife, have taken delivery of EVs before charging infrastructure was in place. The state has appropriated approximately $80 million for charging infrastructure installation since 2020, a figure that covers roughly 23 percent of the estimated need.

---

## The Upfront Capital Problem

The economics of fleet electrification favor EVs in the long run but require capital upfront that the state's annual budget cycle does not accommodate easily. A comparable mid-size EV sedan costs approximately $10,000 to $15,000 more upfront than its gasoline equivalent, before federal tax credits. The state is not eligible for consumer EV tax credits, which apply to individuals, though some fleet procurement arrangements can access commercial credits under the Inflation Reduction Act.

Across 50,000 light-duty vehicles replaced over a full replacement cycle, the upfront cost premium for EVs would total approximately $500 million to $750 million above what gasoline vehicles would cost. The state recovers that premium and then some over the vehicle's service life, but the capital flows in different directions: higher acquisition cost today, lower fuel and maintenance cost over seven to nine years. State budget accounting does not naturally capture multi-year total cost of ownership comparisons; annual appropriations look at acquisition cost, and the operational savings that justify the higher capital cost appear in future years' fuel line items across dozens of departments.

DGS has explored lease-financing arrangements and lifecycle cost procurement models to capture the long-term economics in the acquisition decision. The Legislative Analyst's Office noted in a 2023 report that the state's vehicle procurement process was not optimized for total cost of ownership analysis and recommended updating the methodology. Whether that recommendation has been acted upon is not fully visible in public budget documents.

---

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has committed to a fleet electrification mandate that it lacks the charging infrastructure to support and the procurement flexibility to finance efficiently. The 2025 target for 100 percent zero-emission light-duty purchases was written as an executive order in 2020, when the state's charging infrastructure was at roughly 10 percent of the required capacity. Five years later, the infrastructure is at roughly 30 percent. Departments are acquiring EVs they cannot fully charge, in some cases defeating the operational purpose of the vehicles. The upfront capital premium is being absorbed in annual budgets that did not plan for it, creating pressure on departments with tight operational budgets. And the mandate covers only new purchases, meaning the fleet will not be fully electric until every pre-2025 vehicle ages out, which at average turnover rates means the early 2030s at the earliest. California has declared itself a leader on fleet electrification. The data suggests it has been a faster leader on mandating than on infrastructure.

The generous read is that the direction is correct and the implementation, while imperfect, is accelerating. Thirty percent electrification of the light-duty fleet in four years is a faster transition than most large employer fleets anywhere in the country have achieved. The charging infrastructure deficit is real but bounded: 3,500 ports installed, 7,000 to go, with identified capital needs and ongoing appropriations. The long-term savings are not theoretical; they are calculable and substantial, and the state's multi-billion-dollar vehicle operating budget will benefit from them at scale once the transition is further along. Fleet electrification is also one of the few state decarbonization commitments that saves money rather than spending it, which makes it durable in a way that subsidy-dependent programs are not. The state is doing what it said it would do. The pace is constrained by capital availability and infrastructure construction timelines that are not unique to California.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

DGS publishes fleet inventory data, but the public-facing reports do not consistently track which vehicles are routinely charged at state facilities versus charged off-site or operated in ways that reduce EV operational benefits. The per-department breakdown of EV adoption rates is not consolidated in a single public dashboard, making it difficult to identify which agencies are leading and which are lagging. The infrastructure installation count is periodically reported but not updated in real time. And the total cost of ownership analysis that would justify the capital investment most clearly is not a standard component of the state's vehicle procurement reporting.
