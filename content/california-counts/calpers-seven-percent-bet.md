# California Counts: The Seven Percent Bet
## What $502 billion in assets, a 7 percent assumed return, $170 billion in unfunded liability, and 2 million beneficiaries reveal about the investment machine that underwrites California's retirement promise

The California Public Employees' Retirement System manages approximately $502 billion in assets for 2 million members, retirees, and their beneficiaries. It is the largest public pension fund in the United States and the sixth-largest in the world. Its investment returns determine whether California's state and local governments can honor the retirement benefits promised to teachers, firefighters, highway patrol officers, nurses, engineers, and the other 1.5 million active and inactive public employees whose pensions depend on CalPERS' ability to earn 7 percent annually on half a trillion dollars, compounded, forever.

The 7 percent assumed rate of return, reduced from 7.5 percent in 2016, is the number on which everything depends. If CalPERS earns 7 percent over time, the fund is fully funded: assets equal liabilities, contributions remain stable, and benefits are paid as promised. If it earns less, the unfunded liability grows, employer contributions increase, and public agencies must divert money from services to pension costs. CalPERS' actual 20-year annualized return as of fiscal year 2024-25 is approximately 7.1 percent, barely above the target and flattered by the post-pandemic stock market recovery. Its 10-year return is approximately 6.6 percent. Its 30-year return is approximately 7.7 percent, pulled up by the 1990s bull market. The assumed return is achievable over long horizons. It is not guaranteed over any horizon. And every year it falls short compounds the gap between what is owed and what exists to pay it.

## The Funded Ratio: 72 Percent

CalPERS' funded ratio, the percentage of future benefit obligations covered by current assets, is approximately 72 percent as of the most recent actuarial valuation. This means that for every dollar of benefits promised, the fund has approximately 72 cents in assets. The remaining 28 cents, approximately $170 billion, is the unfunded accrued liability: the gap between what CalPERS owes and what it has.

The funded ratio has fluctuated dramatically with investment returns. In 2000, CalPERS was approximately 100 percent funded, with assets of $172 billion against liabilities of $172 billion. The dot-com crash reduced the ratio to approximately 82 percent by 2003. Recovery brought it back above 90 percent by 2007. The Great Recession crashed it to approximately 61 percent in 2009. Recovery brought it to approximately 72 percent by 2019. The pandemic crash briefly reduced it, followed by the market recovery that brought it to approximately 80 percent in 2022. The subsequent market correction and rising interest rates produced the current 72 percent.

The 72 percent funded ratio places CalPERS in the middle of the pack among large public pension funds nationally. Some systems (Wisconsin, South Dakota) are funded above 95 percent. Others (Illinois, New Jersey, Kentucky) are funded below 50 percent. CalPERS' funded position is not a crisis by national standards. It is a chronic condition: a $170 billion hole that grows when markets fall, shrinks when markets rise, and never quite closes because the assumed return is optimistic enough to suppress contributions during good years and insufficient to eliminate the gap during bad ones.

## The Portfolio: Where $502 Billion Is Invested

CalPERS' investment portfolio is allocated across approximately five major asset classes. As of early 2026:

Public equity (stocks): approximately 42 percent of the portfolio, or roughly $211 billion, invested in domestic and international public equities through a combination of index funds and active management.

Fixed income (bonds): approximately 25 percent, or roughly $126 billion, invested in government, corporate, and securitized debt.

Private equity: approximately 13 percent, or roughly $65 billion, invested in leveraged buyout, venture capital, and growth equity funds managed by external partners.

Real assets: approximately 12 percent, or roughly $60 billion, split between real estate (direct ownership and fund investments) and infrastructure.

Other: approximately 8 percent in private debt, cash, and overlay strategies.

The portfolio's expected return, calculated by CalPERS' investment staff using forward-looking capital market assumptions, is approximately 6.5 to 7.0 percent annualized over a 10-year horizon. The range barely brackets the 7 percent assumption, meaning CalPERS must earn at or above its expected return simply to avoid falling further behind. There is no margin for underperformance.

Private equity has been CalPERS' highest-returning asset class, delivering approximately 11 to 13 percent annualized returns over the past 20 years. It has also been the most controversial: CalPERS pays approximately $3.5 to $4 billion per year in management fees and carried interest to external private equity managers, more than it spends on internal investment staff. The fee structure has been the subject of repeated board debates, media scrutiny, and legislative inquiries. CalPERS' 2024 decision to increase its private equity allocation from 13 to 17 percent of the portfolio reflects a strategic bet that the higher returns of private equity justify the higher fees and illiquidity, a bet that will take a decade to validate.

## The Employer Contribution Squeeze

When CalPERS' investment returns fall short, the gap must be closed by employer contributions. These contributions, paid by state and local government agencies as a percentage of covered payroll, have risen dramatically over the past 15 years. The state's contribution rate to CalPERS for miscellaneous employees has risen from approximately 16 percent of payroll in 2010 to approximately 27 percent in 2025. For public safety employees (police, fire), the rate has risen from approximately 25 percent to over 50 percent in some agencies.

The contribution increases are felt directly in local government budgets. A city that spends $10 million per year on police salaries and pays a CalPERS contribution rate of 50 percent is spending $5 million on pension contributions alone, money that is not available for hiring additional officers, maintaining infrastructure, or providing services. A school district whose CalSTRS contribution rate has risen from 8 percent to 19 percent is diverting an additional 11 percent of its salary budget to pension costs, the equivalent of eliminating approximately one in ten teaching positions.

The Public Employees' Pension Reform Act (PEPRA), enacted in 2013, created a lower-benefit, higher-employee-contribution tier for public employees hired after January 1, 2013. PEPRA capped pensionable salaries, raised retirement ages, and eliminated pension spiking practices. The reform will reduce long-term pension costs as the pre-PEPRA workforce retires and is replaced by PEPRA-tier employees. But the transition is slow: pre-PEPRA employees and retirees will constitute the majority of pension obligations for 15 to 20 more years. The unfunded liability is overwhelmingly a pre-PEPRA problem. PEPRA solved the future. It did not solve the past.

## The Governance Question

CalPERS is governed by a 13-member Board of Administration, including the State Controller, the State Treasurer, a designee of the Governor, members of the legislature, and six elected representatives of plan members. The board sets investment policy, selects the CEO and Chief Investment Officer, and makes benefit decisions. Board members serve four-year terms.

The governance structure creates potential conflicts between the board's fiduciary duty to maximize risk-adjusted returns for beneficiaries and the political interests of elected and appointed board members. Board elections have been contested by competing slates representing different philosophies: some candidates campaign on aggressive return targets and higher private equity allocation, others on ESG (environmental, social, and governance) investing and climate risk, and others on fee reduction and index-based strategies. The CIO position has experienced significant turnover: CalPERS has had six CIOs in the past 15 years, a rate of executive churn that is incompatible with long-term investment strategy.

The ESG debate has been particularly contentious. CalPERS divested from tobacco in 2000 and from companies with operations in Sudan in 2006. Calls to divest from fossil fuels have been debated for more than a decade. The board adopted a Net Zero by 2050 climate action plan in 2021, which commits to reducing the portfolio's carbon intensity but does not require divestiture from fossil fuel companies. The tension between maximizing financial returns (CalPERS' fiduciary obligation) and advancing social or environmental goals (which board members and beneficiaries may value) is unresolved and may be unresolvable within the current governance structure.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that CalPERS has made a multi-generational bet on 7 percent returns that history suggests is achievable on average but not reliably, and that every year of underperformance compounds into contribution increases that squeeze public services. The $170 billion unfunded liability is the accumulated cost of decades of optimistic assumptions, retroactive benefit enhancements granted during bull markets, and contribution holidays taken when the fund appeared overfunded. The employer contribution rates, now consuming 25 to 50 percent of payroll depending on the employer and plan, represent a transfer from current public services to past pension promises. Every dollar a city pays to CalPERS is a dollar it does not spend on parks, roads, libraries, or the current workforce. The $3.5 to $4 billion in annual private equity fees exceed the operating budgets of most state agencies. And the governance structure, which subjects half a trillion dollars in investment decisions to the electoral politics of board elections, is not how any private institution would manage an asset of this magnitude.

The generous read is that CalPERS has delivered on its fundamental promise: 2 million beneficiaries receive pension checks every month. The average CalPERS pension is approximately $38,000 per year, modest by any standard and well below private sector executive retirement packages. The fund's 30-year return of 7.7 percent exceeds the assumed rate. PEPRA has structurally reduced the cost of future pension accruals. The funded ratio, while below 100 percent, is stable and improving. And the scale of CalPERS' asset management, $502 billion managed at a total cost of approximately 50 basis points, is among the most cost-efficient in institutional investing. The unfunded liability is a problem of the past that is being addressed through higher contributions and benefit reform. It is not a crisis that threatens the fund's ability to pay benefits. CalPERS has never missed a pension payment. The actuarial projections show the funded ratio improving over the next 20 years as PEPRA savings accumulate and the unfunded liability is amortized. The system works. It costs more than it should. But it works.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CalPERS publishes annual financial statements, actuarial valuations, investment performance reports, and board meeting materials. The transparency is extensive by institutional standards. What the public reporting does not provide is a clear accounting of the total cost of the pension system to California's economy: the sum of employer contributions, employee contributions, and the opportunity cost of funds diverted from services to pension obligations. A comprehensive analysis by the Stanford Institute for Economic Policy Research estimated that the present value of California's total public pension liability (CalPERS, CalSTRS, UC Retirement, and local plans) exceeds $1 trillion when valued at a risk-free discount rate rather than the expected return on assets. The gap between the two methodologies, expected return versus risk-free rate, is the most contentious accounting question in public pension finance. CalPERS uses the expected return method, which is standard practice for public pensions. Economists argue that the risk-free rate is the appropriate measure because pension benefits are guaranteed obligations, not risky investments. The difference is approximately $400 billion: the true cost of California's pension promise may be $400 billion higher than the official books show, depending on which accounting framework you believe.
