# California Counts: The Closed Doors
## What the closure of the Division of Juvenile Justice, a 90 percent decline in youth incarceration, $300,000 per youth per year in county facilities, and a 40 percent recidivism rate reveal about the system California dismantled without building a replacement

In June 2023, California closed the Division of Juvenile Justice (DJJ), the state-run system for incarcerating the most serious juvenile offenders. At its peak in the 1990s, DJJ (then called the California Youth Authority) held approximately 10,000 youth in 11 facilities. At closure, it held fewer than 700. The shutdown completed a decades-long trajectory: California's youth incarceration rate has declined approximately 90 percent since 1996, one of the steepest declines of any state and a reduction that has occurred alongside a corresponding decline in youth crime rates.

SB 823 (2020) mandated the DJJ closure and transferred responsibility for all juvenile offenders, including those convicted of the most serious offenses (murder, rape, arson, kidnapping), to county probation departments. The transfer was accompanied by approximately $200 million per year in state funding to counties through the Juvenile Justice Realignment Block Grant, intended to support the development of county-level secure youth treatment facilities and evidence-based rehabilitation programs. The per-youth cost at DJJ in its final years was approximately $300,000 to $360,000 per year, one of the highest per-capita incarceration costs in the nation, reflecting both the system's shrinking population (fixed facility costs spread over fewer youth) and the intensive programming (education, mental health treatment, vocational training) required by legal settlements.

## The County Patchwork

The 58 county probation departments that inherited DJJ's population vary enormously in capacity, programming, and approach. Los Angeles County, which has the largest juvenile justice population in the state, operates several secure youth facilities and has invested in evidence-based programs (cognitive behavioral therapy, multisystemic therapy, functional family therapy) that peer-reviewed research has shown to reduce recidivism. Smaller counties, particularly rural counties with a handful of juvenile cases per year, lack the population base to operate specialized facilities or programming and may place youth in neighboring counties' facilities or in group homes.

The variation produces disparate outcomes. A youth committed for a serious offense in LA County may receive individual and family therapy, educational programming leading to a high school diploma or GED, vocational training, and a structured reentry plan. A youth committed for a similar offense in a rural county may be placed in a facility with minimal programming, taught by a single teacher serving all grade levels, and released to a community with no reentry services. The quality of juvenile justice in California depends, as with so many California services, on the county of residence, a geographic lottery that no state policy has equalized.

The recidivism data captures the system's limited success. Approximately 40 percent of youth released from county juvenile facilities are rearrested within two years. For youth released from the old DJJ system, the recidivism rate was approximately 50 percent. The improvement is modest and may reflect changes in the population (the youth now in secure facilities are fewer and, on average, older and charged with more serious offenses than the broader DJJ population of the 1990s) rather than improvements in programming effectiveness.

## The Intersection with Foster Care and Mental Health

The juvenile justice system is not separate from the foster care and mental health systems. Approximately 25 to 30 percent of youth in the juvenile justice system have current or prior involvement with the child welfare system. An estimated 60 to 70 percent of justice-involved youth have a diagnosable mental health condition. Approximately 80 percent have experienced at least one adverse childhood experience (abuse, neglect, household dysfunction), and the average number of ACEs among incarcerated youth is four to five, well above the threshold associated with dramatically elevated risk of substance abuse, depression, and criminal behavior.

The systems that are supposed to intervene before a youth enters the justice system, child welfare, school-based mental health, community mental health, substance use treatment, are the same systems that this series has documented as fragmented, underfunded, and uncoordinated. The foster care system loses track of youth who age out at 18 or 21. The school system suspends and expels the students whose behavior reflects trauma. The mental health system has three-month wait lists in most counties. The juvenile justice system receives the youth who fell through the gaps in every other system, then spends $300,000 per year per youth attempting to repair damage that upstream investments of a fraction of that amount might have prevented.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California closed DJJ without ensuring that counties could replicate or improve upon the services it provided. The $200 million annual block grant, divided across 58 counties, provides approximately $3.4 million per county on average, insufficient to build or operate the specialized secure facilities with evidence-based programming that effective juvenile rehabilitation requires. Rural counties lack the scale. Urban counties face competing priorities. The realignment transferred responsibility without transferring capacity. And the 90 percent decline in youth incarceration, while laudable, masks a harder question: what happened to the youth who would have been incarcerated under the old system? Some are diverted to community-based programs that work. Some are diverted to nothing. Some are not diverted at all but prosecuted as adults under Proposition 21 provisions that remain in effect, entering the adult prison system where their youth is a vulnerability rather than a mitigating factor.

The generous read is that closing DJJ was the right decision. The facilities were antiquated, the outcomes were poor (50 percent recidivism), and the per-youth cost was indefensible. Research consistently shows that community-based alternatives to incarceration produce equal or better outcomes at lower cost for the vast majority of justice-involved youth. The 90 percent decline in youth incarceration has occurred alongside a decline in youth crime, demonstrating that locking up fewer young people does not produce more crime. The county-level variation in quality is a problem that state oversight and standards can address over time. The realignment is three years old. Building the county-level infrastructure to replace a 50-year-old state system takes longer than three years.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Board of State and Community Corrections (BSCC) collects data on county juvenile facility populations, capacity, and some program participation metrics. It does not publish standardized outcome data that would allow comparison of recidivism rates, educational attainment, employment outcomes, or housing stability across counties. The block grant requires counties to submit spending reports, but the spending data does not include outcome measures that would indicate whether the money is producing better results than DJJ did. The question that matters, whether youth fare better under county-level care than they did under DJJ, is not currently answerable from the publicly available data because the outcome measurement infrastructure has not been built.
