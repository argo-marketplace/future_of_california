# California Counts: The Invisible System
## What 400,000 clients, 21 regional centers, a $14 billion annual budget, and a 50-year entitlement that most Californians have never heard of reveal about the disability services system California built and quietly grew into one of its largest programs

The California Department of Developmental Services (DDS) administers a system of 21 nonprofit regional centers that collectively serve approximately 400,000 Californians with developmental disabilities: intellectual disability, cerebral palsy, epilepsy, autism, and conditions requiring services similar to those needed by individuals with intellectual disability. The system operates under the Lanterman Developmental Disabilities Services Act (1969), which established a statutory entitlement: every eligible Californian has a legal right to the services and supports necessary to live as independently as possible, regardless of cost. The entitlement is not capped, not means-tested, and not subject to waiting lists for core services (though specific services may have wait times in practice). It is one of the most expansive disability rights statutes in the nation.

The system's annual budget is approximately $14 billion, of which roughly 50 percent is state general fund and 50 percent is federal Medicaid matching funds. The budget has grown approximately 250 percent in the past 15 years, driven by a surge in autism diagnoses (autism now accounts for approximately 60 percent of new regional center intakes), the aging of the existing client population (adults with developmental disabilities are living longer, requiring services for more years), and rate increases for service providers that were frozen for more than a decade and have only recently been restored. The $14 billion budget makes DDS one of the five largest departments in the California state budget, larger than the Department of Corrections, larger than CalFire, and virtually unknown to the general public.

---

## The Autism Wave

The factor most responsible for the budget's growth is the increase in autism spectrum disorder diagnoses. In 2000, the regional center system served approximately 15,000 individuals with autism. By 2025, that number had grown to approximately 200,000, an increase of more than 1,200 percent. Autism has displaced intellectual disability as the largest diagnostic category in the regional center system and now accounts for the majority of new intakes.

The autism increase is driven by diagnostic expansion (broader diagnostic criteria in the DSM-5, increased screening in schools and pediatric settings), greater public awareness and reduced stigma, and the strong incentive to obtain a regional center diagnosis because it triggers an entitlement to services. Whether the increase reflects a genuine increase in the prevalence of autism or primarily reflects improved identification of a condition that was previously undiagnosed or misdiagnosed is debated in the clinical literature. The practical consequence for the regional center system is the same regardless: the client population is growing faster than the budget, and the per-client expenditure (approximately $35,000 per year on average) is under pressure.

## The Provider Crisis

Regional centers do not provide services directly. They function as case management and purchasing agencies: each client has an Individual Program Plan (IPP) developed by a service coordinator, and the regional center purchases the services specified in the plan from private providers (behavioral therapists, day programs, residential facilities, supported employment agencies, respite care workers). The system depends on a network of approximately 15,000 service providers, many of them small businesses and sole practitioners.

The provider network is in crisis. Service provider rates were frozen from 2003 to 2019, a 16-year period during which the minimum wage rose from $6.75 to $12.00 per hour. Providers who relied on regional center rates to cover the cost of direct service workers (who are paid near minimum wage) saw their rate revenue remain flat while their labor costs nearly doubled. The result was provider closures, reduced service availability, and a workforce shortage: direct support professionals in the developmental services system earn a median of approximately $17 to $19 per hour, below the living wage in every California metropolitan area and barely above the fast-food minimum of $20 per hour. The workers who provide the most intimate care to the most vulnerable Californians, bathing, dressing, feeding, transporting, supervising individuals with profound disabilities, are paid less than many entry-level retail positions.

SB 855 (2022) and subsequent legislation funded rate increases intended to bring provider rates to a level that covers the cost of service delivery at current wage levels. The rate reform, implemented over several years, is the most significant investment in the developmental services provider network in two decades. Whether it is sufficient to stabilize the provider network and attract workers into direct support roles is not yet clear.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has an entitlement-based disability services system that costs $14 billion per year and that most Californians, most legislators, and most journalists have never examined. The Lanterman Act guarantees services without a cost cap, but the guarantee is hollow when the providers who deliver the services cannot recruit workers because the rates are too low to pay competitive wages. The 400,000 clients are entitled to individualized services on paper. In practice, families spend months on wait lists for behavioral therapy, struggle to find qualified respite workers, and navigate a case management system where service coordinators carry caseloads of 60 to 80 clients, well above the 45 recommended by the Association of Regional Center Agencies. The system's invisibility is not accidental: the population it serves has limited political voice, the services it funds are delivered in private homes and small facilities rather than in visible public institutions, and the budget line items are buried in the DDS section of a $310 billion state budget. California built an extraordinary disability services entitlement. It has not funded it at the level the entitlement requires.

The generous read is that the Lanterman Act and the regional center system represent one of California's genuine policy achievements: a 50-year commitment to community-based services for people with developmental disabilities that has kept hundreds of thousands of Californians out of institutions and in their communities. The deinstitutionalization movement, which California led, closed the state's large developmental centers (Sonoma, Lanterman, Fairview) and replaced institutional care with community-based services that research consistently shows produce better outcomes and greater client satisfaction. The $14 billion budget, while under pressure, is a massive investment that no other state matches in scope or scale. The rate reforms are addressing the provider crisis. The system serves 400,000 people with a level of individualization (each client has a personalized IPP) that would be impossible in an institutional model. The system is imperfect, underfunded, and invisible. It is also indispensable.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

DDS publishes client count data, expenditure data, and purchase-of-service data by regional center, age group, and diagnostic category. It does not publish standardized outcome data: whether clients are achieving the goals in their IPPs, whether service quality varies by regional center or provider, or whether the rate increases are translating into improved service availability and workforce recruitment. The data infrastructure measures inputs (how much is spent) rather than outcomes (what the spending produces).
