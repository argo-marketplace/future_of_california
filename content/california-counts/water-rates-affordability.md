# California Counts: The Water Bill
## What 3,000 water systems, 300,000 households spending over 5 percent of income on water, and a human right declared in 2012 reveal about the gap between California's principles and its plumbing

California codified a human right to safe, clean, affordable drinking water in 2012. AB 685 made California the first state in the nation to recognize this right in law. Thirteen years later, approximately 1 million Californians cannot reliably afford water service, and the state's approximately 3,000 community water systems vary in their rates by a factor of 10 or more. The declaration was real. The infrastructure of affordability to back it up has been built incrementally, incompletely, and in most years, insufficiently.

---

## 3,000 Systems, One State

California has more community water systems than any other state in the country, approximately 3,000 systems serving populations that range from a few hundred people to several million. The number reflects California's geography, settlement history, and the fact that small rural communities across the Central Valley, the Sierra foothills, and the desert regions built their own water infrastructure in the twentieth century and have operated it ever since.

The Los Angeles Department of Water and Power is the country's largest municipal utility, serving approximately 4 million people. Its size confers an enormous cost advantage: the capital costs of treatment plants, pipelines, reservoirs, and distribution infrastructure are spread across millions of rate-payers, reducing the per-household cost of the fixed infrastructure. LADWP's rates, while rising in recent years, remain among the lower rates in the metropolitan area despite the agency's aging infrastructure challenges.

A small water system serving 500 people in Tulare County operates in an entirely different cost universe. The same basic requirements apply: a treatment plant, distribution lines, monitoring equipment, a qualified operator, laboratory testing, regulatory compliance. But the cost of all of that infrastructure is divided among 500 households instead of 4 million. The per-unit cost of water delivery from a small rural system is commonly 5 to 10 times the per-unit cost from a large urban system. This is not a management failure. It is arithmetic.

A 2023 UC Berkeley analysis found that approximately 300,000 California households spend more than 5 percent of their household income on combined water and wastewater service, the threshold commonly used to define unaffordable utility costs. These households are concentrated in three groups: low-income households served by large urban systems with rising rates, households served by small rural systems with structurally high costs, and households in communities of color in the Central Valley and other regions where water systems have long-deferred infrastructure maintenance and elevated contaminant levels.

## The Small System Problem

Small water systems in California face a compounding set of challenges. Many were built in the 1950s through 1970s and have infrastructure approaching or past its design life. Pipelines corrode, treatment equipment fails, wells become contaminated. The cost of replacing aging infrastructure is enormous relative to the small customer base that must pay for it.

Regulatory requirements add to the cost burden. Safe Drinking Water Act compliance requires water systems of all sizes to monitor for an expanding list of contaminants, submit regular reports, maintain qualified operators, and address violations. A violation at a system serving 500 people triggers the same regulatory response as a violation at a system serving 500,000, with roughly 1,000 times the per-household regulatory cost.

The State Water Resources Control Board's Safe and Affordable Funding for Equity and Resilience (SAFER) program was created by SB 200 in 2019 to provide financial assistance to struggling small water systems. SAFER receives approximately $130 million per year from a fund supported by a small charge on consolidated water system revenues. The program can provide grants, loans, technical assistance, and support for consolidation of struggling small systems with larger, more financially stable neighbors.

Consolidation is SWRCB's preferred long-term solution for chronically struggling small systems: connecting a small rural community's water infrastructure to a larger system that can provide professional management, economies of scale, and financial resilience. Consolidation is also, in many communities, deeply controversial. Water systems in small rural communities are often locally owned, locally governed, and locally valued as community institutions. The prospect of transferring control to a larger entity, often a county water district or a private utility, triggers resistance that is not irrational even if the financial logic of consolidation is sound.

The number of small water systems in chronic violation of drinking water standards in California runs into the hundreds. SWRCB's SAFER program dashboard tracks these systems and their progress toward compliance. Progress is uneven. Some systems have been in violation for a decade or more.

## LIRA: The Low Income Rate Assistance Program

SB 222, signed in 2021, established the Low Income Water Rate Assistance (LIRA) program, which created a framework for water bill assistance for low-income ratepayers, modeled on the Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP) for utility bills. The program was established in statute before funding was secured.

LIRA's design involves a small surcharge on water bills across participating water systems, with the collected funds used to discount bills for low-income customers. The program is opt-in for water systems, meaning its coverage depends on how many utilities choose to participate. Large utilities with established low-income rate assistance programs, including LADWP and East Bay MUD, have their own existing discount programs that predate LIRA. Small utilities, which serve the most cost-burdened customers, often lack the administrative capacity to run a LIRA discount program.

As of 2025, LIRA has not reached full statewide implementation. The program exists; the infrastructure to deliver it equitably across 3,000 water systems remains incomplete. Advocates estimate that the true gap between available assistance and need runs to hundreds of millions of dollars per year.

## The Arithmetic of Unaffordability

A household earning $35,000 per year that spends 5 percent of income on water and wastewater spends $1,750 per year, or approximately $146 per month. In Los Angeles, a typical residential water bill for a low-use household runs $50 to $80 per month for water alone before wastewater charges. In a small rural water system in the Central Valley, the same usage might cost $100 to $150 per month for water, and wastewater service where it exists adds to that. These are not precise averages but illustrative ranges drawn from published rate structures.

The 300,000 households spending more than 5 percent of income on water service are not primarily households with unusually high water use. They are households with low incomes or households served by high-cost small systems, often both simultaneously. Central Valley farmworker households, often with multiple people per unit, living in colonias served by small water systems with high rates and old infrastructure, represent the most acute overlap of low income and high water cost. These communities are also disproportionately Latino and often have limited political representation in water governance bodies.

The human right to water declared in AB 685 is not self-executing. It does not impose rate caps. It does not require the state to fund water service for people who cannot afford it. It does impose an obligation on state agencies to consider the human right to water when making decisions about water allocation and system management. That obligation has influenced some SWRCB decisions at the margin. It has not, in thirteen years, resolved the affordability gap for the 1 million Californians the data suggests cannot consistently afford water service.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California declared water a human right, built a legal framework for rate assistance, funded a program to help struggling small systems, and after thirteen years still has 1 million residents who cannot reliably afford water service, hundreds of small water systems in chronic violation of safe drinking water standards, and a rate assistance program that has not achieved full statewide coverage. The SAFER program's $130 million per year is meaningful for individual systems but small relative to the infrastructure investment backlog in California's small and disadvantaged water systems, which researchers have estimated in the billions. The consolidation strategy that SWRCB promotes as the long-term solution faces genuine community resistance and has proceeded slowly. The gap between the declaration of a human right and the funded reality of its implementation is measurable in the rate bills of farmworker families in the Central Valley.

The generous read is that California has done more than any other state to institutionalize water affordability as a policy goal. SB 200's SAFER funding mechanism is innovative and dedicated; the LIRA program, while not yet at full implementation, creates a statutory framework that other states do not have; and the SAFER program's consolidation assistance is genuinely helping some of the most distressed small systems in the country. The problem of small system unaffordability is not unique to California and is not the product of California policy failure so much as the arithmetic of small-scale infrastructure in a high-cost state. The human right declaration in AB 685, while not self-enforcing, has elevated water affordability as a criterion in regulatory decisions and provided advocates with a legal hook that has influenced individual SWRCB decisions. Thirteen years is not long enough to fix infrastructure that took a century to build and decades to neglect.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

Water rate data across California's 3,000 systems is not consolidated in a single publicly accessible database with uniform variables. Rate structures vary: some systems charge flat rates, some tiered rates, some inclining block rates, some seasonal rates. Comparing the cost of water service across systems requires applying different rate structures to a common usage assumption, which is the approach researchers use but which is not routinely published by any state agency.

The 300,000-household figure for households spending over 5 percent of income on water and wastewater is a modeled estimate from a 2023 academic analysis, not a direct administrative measure. The actual number could be higher if the household income data or usage patterns underlying the model are imprecise.

What the data definitively does not show is the health outcomes associated with water affordability. Households that reduce water use to avoid bills, defer payment until service is shut off, or rely on bottled water when they cannot afford service experience different health outcomes than households with consistent access. The connection between water unaffordability and health outcomes is documented in academic literature but is not tracked in routine California health surveillance data in a way that would allow quantification.

---

California's water system is three thousand systems, not one. That fact explains most of what the data shows about rate variation, unaffordability, and the distance between a declared human right and a monthly bill that some families cannot pay. Large systems have low rates. Small systems have high rates. Low-income households are overrepresented in both categories. The state has built the institutional architecture of a solution, in SAFER, in LIRA, in AB 685, in the SWRCB's disadvantaged community focus. What it has not done, in thirteen years, is fully fund that architecture. The right was easy to declare. The water bill is harder to fix.
