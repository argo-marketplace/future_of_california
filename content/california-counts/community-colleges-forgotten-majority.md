# California Counts: The Forgotten Majority
## What 116 colleges, 1.8 million students, a 33 percent completion rate, and $9,700 per student reveal about the largest higher education system in the world and the students it was built to serve

The California Community Colleges system is the largest system of higher education in the world. Its 116 colleges, organized into 73 districts, enroll approximately 1.8 million students each year. That is more students than the University of California and California State University systems combined, roughly three times over. The community colleges enroll approximately 70 percent of all California public higher education students. They serve the students the Master Plan assigned to them in 1960: those who need remediation, vocational training, transfer pathways to four-year institutions, or a second chance. They are, by design and by enrollment, the default institution of California higher education, the open door that anyone with a high school diploma or equivalent can walk through.

The open door leads to a labyrinth. The systemwide completion rate, defined as the percentage of first-time students who earn a certificate, associate degree, or transfer to a four-year institution within six years, is approximately 33 percent. Two out of three students who enter a California community college do not complete a credential or transfer within six years. For Black male students, the completion rate is approximately 22 percent. For Hispanic male students, approximately 27 percent. The rates improve for students who enroll full-time, but approximately 60 percent of community college students attend part-time, juggling coursework with jobs, families, and housing instability. The system was designed as an access institution. It delivers access. Whether it delivers outcomes is the question the completion data raises and does not answer.

## The Funding Gap

California community colleges receive approximately $9,700 per full-time equivalent student in state and local funding, compared to approximately $14,500 per student at CSU and approximately $38,000 per student at UC. The funding formula, revised in 2018 through the Student Centered Funding Formula, allocates revenue based on three components: a base allocation (70 percent, based on enrollment), a supplemental allocation (20 percent, based on the number of low-income students receiving financial aid), and a student success allocation (10 percent, based on completion and transfer outcomes). The formula was intended to reward colleges that serve disadvantaged students and produce results, but the base allocation dominates, and per-student funding remains well below what four-year institutions receive.

The funding gap has practical consequences. Community colleges pay faculty less than their CSU and UC counterparts: the average full-time community college faculty salary is approximately $95,000, compared to approximately $120,000 at CSU and $180,000 at UC. The gap is wider for adjunct faculty, who teach approximately 60 percent of all community college course sections at pay rates of $60 to $80 per lecture hour with no benefits, office space, or job security. A adjunct instructor teaching five courses per semester at two different colleges, the standard "freeway flyer" workload, earns approximately $40,000 to $50,000 per year, below the living wage in every California metropolitan area.

The capital infrastructure is equally strained. The community college system's deferred maintenance backlog is estimated at $20 billion to $25 billion. Many campuses include buildings constructed in the 1950s and 1960s that have not received major renovation. The state provides capital funding through general obligation bonds (Proposition 51 of 2016 allocated $2 billion for community colleges), but the bond funding is episodic and insufficient to address the accumulated backlog. Individual districts supplement state capital funds with local general obligation bonds, but the capacity to pass local bonds varies dramatically by district wealth and voter demographics.

## AB 1705 and the Remediation Revolution

For decades, community colleges placed the majority of incoming students into remedial courses in English and mathematics based on placement test scores. At some colleges, 80 to 90 percent of entering students were placed into at least one remedial course, and students placed into the lowest levels of remediation faced sequences of two, three, or four courses before reaching transfer-level work. The remediation sequences functioned as attrition machines: each additional course in the sequence produced dropout rates of 30 to 50 percent, so that a student placed three levels below transfer-level math had a completion probability in the single digits.

AB 705 (2017) and AB 1705 (2022) dismantled the remediation model. The laws require community colleges to place students directly into transfer-level English and math courses, with concurrent support (co-requisite courses or tutoring) rather than prerequisite remedial sequences. The results have been dramatic: throughput rates, the percentage of students completing transfer-level English and math within their first year, have approximately doubled systemwide. For English, throughput increased from approximately 25 percent under the old placement model to approximately 50 percent under AB 705 implementation. For math, throughput increased from approximately 15 percent to approximately 30 percent.

The improvement is real and significant, but the absolute numbers remain sobering. Even after the reform, 50 percent of community college students do not complete transfer-level English in their first year, and 70 percent do not complete transfer-level math. The barrier is not solely academic preparation. It is the compounding weight of part-time enrollment, work obligations, housing instability, food insecurity, and the absence of the wraparound support services that four-year residential institutions provide.

## The Transfer Pipeline

The community colleges are the primary feeder to the CSU system and a significant feeder to UC. Approximately 80,000 to 90,000 community college students transfer to a four-year institution each year, roughly half to CSU and 20,000 to 25,000 to UC. The Associate Degree for Transfer (ADT), created by SB 1440 (2010), guarantees community college students who complete a specified curriculum admission to CSU with junior standing. Over 40,000 ADTs are awarded annually, making the program the single most important transfer pathway in the system.

The transfer rate, however, represents a small fraction of total enrollment. Of the 1.8 million students enrolled, approximately 5 percent successfully transfer in any given year. The remaining students are pursuing vocational certificates (approximately 15 percent), taking courses for personal enrichment or workforce upgrading without credential intent (approximately 30 percent), or enrolled but making slow or no progress toward a credential (approximately 50 percent). The system's breadth, serving everyone from high school dual-enrollment students to retirees learning watercolor painting, makes aggregate statistics misleading: a completion rate calculated across all enrolled students includes large populations for whom "completion" was never the goal.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has built a two-tier public higher education system in which the institution that serves the most students, the most diverse students, and the lowest-income students receives the least funding per student and produces the worst outcomes. The 4-to-1 funding ratio between UC and community colleges is a resource allocation that values research prestige over workforce development and access. The 33 percent completion rate means the system fails the majority of the students it admits. The reliance on adjunct labor, 60 percent of instruction delivered by faculty earning poverty wages, is an exploitation of teaching labor that directly affects instructional quality. The community colleges were assigned the hardest job in the Master Plan, educating the students with the greatest needs, and were given the fewest resources to do it. The system is a monument to the gap between California's rhetorical commitment to educational equity and its fiscal commitment to educational hierarchy.

The generous read is that the community colleges serve 1.8 million students per year at a per-student cost of $9,700, a fraction of what four-year institutions charge, and produce real outcomes: 80,000 to 90,000 annual transfers to four-year institutions, hundreds of thousands of vocational certificates, workforce training that supports industries from healthcare to manufacturing, and a second-chance pathway for students who were not served well by K-12. The AB 705 reforms have doubled throughput in gateway courses, demonstrating that systemic improvement is possible. The Student Centered Funding Formula is directing resources toward the colleges that serve the neediest students. The completion rate, while low, reflects the reality that community colleges serve a population with challenges (poverty, work obligations, family responsibilities, inadequate K-12 preparation) that no institution could fully overcome through instruction alone. The community colleges are not failing. They are underfunded and overburdened, which is a different diagnosis with different prescriptions.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Chancellor's Office publishes extensive data through the Student Success Metrics dashboard and the DataMart system, including enrollment, completion, transfer, and demographic data at the college level. It does not publish data on the experience of attending a community college: the time students spend navigating bureaucratic enrollment processes, the frequency with which needed courses are unavailable due to section cuts, the percentage of students who lose financial aid due to pace-of-completion requirements, or the number of students who stop out temporarily and intend to return but never do. The data counts outcomes. It does not count the obstacles between enrollment and outcome that the institution itself imposes.
