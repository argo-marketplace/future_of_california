# California Counts: The Fire Budget
## What a $4.5 billion annual budget, 12,000 seasonal firefighters, 2,000 inmate fire crews, and a fire season that no longer ends reveal about the department California cannot stop feeding

The California Department of Forestry and Fire Protection (CalFire) operates on an annual budget of approximately $4.5 billion, making it one of the largest firefighting agencies in the world. The budget has roughly tripled since 2010, when it was approximately $1.5 billion. The increase reflects both the escalation of wildfire activity (the 10 largest fires in California history, measured by acres burned, have all occurred since 2017) and the expansion of CalFire's prevention and resilience mandate beyond its traditional suppression mission. CalFire employs approximately 12,000 permanent and seasonal personnel, operates 35 air tankers and helicopters, and maintains approximately 800 fire engines across 21 operational units covering 31 million acres of state responsibility areas.

The budget's growth trajectory is the clearest fiscal illustration of climate change in the California state budget. In the 1990s, CalFire's suppression costs averaged approximately $200 million per year. By the 2010s, the average had risen to approximately $800 million. In 2020 and 2021, suppression costs exceeded $2 billion in a single year. The Emergency Fund, a reserve account that CalFire draws from when suppression costs exceed the base budget, has been exhausted in most recent fire years, requiring supplemental appropriations from the legislature. The state budgets for fire suppression based on historical averages, but the historical averages are climbing so steeply that the base budget is structurally insufficient.

## Inmate Firefighters: The $1-an-Hour Workforce

California's inmate firefighting program is one of the oldest and most controversial elements of the state's fire response. Approximately 1,600 to 2,000 inmates are deployed on fire lines each fire season through the Conservation Camp program, jointly operated by CalFire and the California Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation. The inmates receive approximately $5.80 to $10.24 per day plus an additional $1 per hour when actively fighting fire. A full day on the fire line earns an inmate approximately $15 to $20.

The program provides approximately 30 percent of CalFire's hand crew capacity, the ground crews that cut fire lines, clear brush, and perform the manual labor of wildfire suppression. Without inmate crews, CalFire would need to hire approximately 3,000 to 4,000 additional firefighters at full compensation, costing an estimated $300 million to $500 million per year. The program saves the state approximately $100 million per year in reduced suppression costs, according to CDCR estimates.

AB 2147 (2020) created an expedited pathway for formerly incarcerated firefighters to obtain EMT certification and pursue careers in professional firefighting, addressing a paradox: inmates were trusted to fight fires alongside CalFire professionals but, upon release, were barred from the career by criminal background check requirements. The law expanded opportunities but did not eliminate all barriers. The number of formerly incarcerated firefighters who have entered professional firefighting through the expedited pathway remains small, in the hundreds rather than thousands, partly because the transition from $1-per-hour inmate labor to $25-to-$35-per-hour professional firefighting requires training, certification, and institutional support that the pathway provides in limited quantity.

## Prevention: The Budget That Grew

The most significant shift in CalFire's mission over the past five years has been the expansion from pure suppression (putting out fires) to prevention and resilience (reducing the conditions that make fires catastrophic). The state invested approximately $2.7 billion in wildfire resilience between 2020 and 2025, funding vegetation management (prescribed fire, mechanical thinning, fuel breaks), home hardening (fire-resistant building materials, defensible space enforcement), and community-level fire planning.

CalFire's vegetation management target is to treat approximately 500,000 acres per year through a combination of prescribed burning, mechanical treatment, and managed grazing. The actual acreage treated has averaged approximately 100,000 to 150,000 acres per year, roughly 20 to 30 percent of the target, constrained by air quality regulations (prescribed burns produce smoke that violates Clean Air Act standards), liability exposure (a prescribed burn that escapes control becomes the agency's legal and financial liability), staffing limitations, and the narrow weather windows during which burns can be conducted safely. The gap between the treatment target and actual treatment means the fuel load in California's forests and wildlands continues to accumulate faster than it can be reduced.

The Forest Management Task Force, created by executive order in 2020, set a statewide goal of treating 1 million acres per year by 2025 (500,000 acres on federal land, 500,000 on state and private land). Neither target has been met. The federal government has treated approximately 200,000 to 300,000 acres per year in California. The state has treated approximately 100,000 to 150,000 acres. The combined total is approximately one-third of the stated goal.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California is spending its way through a wildfire crisis it cannot suppress its way out of. The $4.5 billion annual budget is dominated by suppression costs, the most expensive and least effective form of fire management. Every dollar spent deploying air tankers and fire engines after a fire starts is a dollar that could have been spent reducing fuel loads before the fire started. The prevention budget, while growing, produces treatment of 100,000 to 150,000 acres per year in a state with 33 million acres of forest and wildland. At that pace, the fuel reduction cycle would take 200 years to complete a single pass through the landscape, by which time the first areas treated would have regrown. The inmate firefighting program subsidizes the suppression model with near-slave-wage labor, a cost savings that depends on mass incarceration. California has increased its fire budget by 200 percent in 15 years without meaningfully reducing fire risk, because the budget increase has chased suppression while prevention remains underfunded and operationally constrained.

The generous read is that CalFire has adapted to an unprecedented escalation in fire activity without a single season in which the agency failed to mount an effective response. The air tanker fleet has been modernized. The firefighter workforce has expanded. Mutual aid agreements with federal, local, and out-of-state agencies produce surge capacity during major fire events. The prevention investment, while below target, represents the largest sustained commitment to landscape-scale fuel reduction in state history. And the fundamental challenge, reducing fuel loads across 33 million acres while protecting air quality, managing liability, and maintaining ecological values, is genuinely difficult. Prescribed fire is not suppression in reverse; it requires different skills, different equipment, and different regulatory frameworks. CalFire is building the prevention capacity from near zero. The trajectory matters more than the current pace.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

CalFire publishes fire incident data (location, acreage, structures damaged and destroyed, suppression costs) through the Fire and Resource Assessment Program (FRAP). It does not publish a standardized cost-effectiveness analysis of suppression spending: how much was spent per acre contained, per structure saved, per life protected. The suppression cost data is aggregated by incident, not by tactical decision, making it impossible to evaluate whether specific expenditures (a particular air tanker deployment, a backfire operation, an evacuation order) were cost-effective. The prevention treatment data (acres treated by method and location) is published but not linked to fire outcome data in a way that would allow assessment of whether treated areas performed better during subsequent fires.
