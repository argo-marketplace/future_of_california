# California Counts: The Licensed State
## What 3.9 million licensees, 40 regulatory boards, 231,000 active contractor licenses, and a $1.5 billion licensing apparatus reveal about the permission California requires before you can earn a living

California licenses more occupations than any other state. The Department of Consumer Affairs oversees approximately 40 boards, bureaus, and commissions that collectively regulate more than 280 professions and issue licenses to approximately 3.9 million Californians. That figure represents roughly one in five working adults in the state. To cut hair, you need 1,600 hours of training and a license from the Board of Barbering and Cosmetology. To install a toilet, you need to pass an examination administered by the Contractors State License Board. To guide a fishing trip, you need a license. To sell real estate, to appraise a home, to practice acupuncture, to operate a pest control business, to work as a court reporter, to landscape a commercial property: each requires a specific credential issued by a specific board funded by specific fees paid by the people who do the work.

The CSLB alone, the largest single licensing entity in the state, maintains approximately 231,000 active contractor licenses (with another 54,000 inactive) across 44 classification types. A Class A (General Engineering) license permits bridge and highway work. A Class B (General Building) covers residential and commercial construction. The 42 C-specialty classifications range from C-2 (Insulation and Acoustical) to C-61 (Limited Specialty), each requiring a separate trade examination and proof of four years of journey-level experience. A contractor who installs both plumbing and electrical systems needs two separate C-licenses or a B-license with demonstrated competence in both trades. The licensing structure maps the entire built environment of California into a grid of regulated specialties, each with its own examination, its own experience requirements, and its own continuing education obligations.

## The Cost of the Gate

The Institute for Justice, a libertarian public interest law firm that has conducted the most comprehensive national studies of occupational licensing burden, ranked California among the three most broadly and onerously licensed states in its 2022 report. California licenses 76 of the 102 lower-income occupations studied, more than all but two other states. The average California licensed occupation requires approximately 827 days of education and experience, two examinations, and fees averaging $486. For some professions the burden is far higher: a cosmetologist must complete 1,600 hours of training at a cost of $10,000 to $20,000 in tuition before sitting for the licensing exam. An interior designer must accumulate 3,200 hours of supervised experience. A contractor must document four years of journey-level experience and pass both a trade examination and a law and business examination, with an application fee of $450, examination fees of approximately $100 per exam, and an initial license fee of $200 to $350.

The licensing fees fund the boards that administer them. The DCA's combined budget of approximately $1.5 billion is generated almost entirely by licensing and renewal fees, not general fund revenue. The boards are self-funding regulatory entities: the people they regulate pay for the privilege of being regulated. This structure creates an institutional incentive to maintain and expand licensing requirements rather than to question whether they are necessary. A board funded by licensing fees has no fiscal incentive to determine that a profession no longer requires licensing.

The economic research on occupational licensing is extensive and largely critical. Morris Kleiner, the University of Minnesota economist who has studied the topic for three decades, estimates that occupational licensing reduces employment in licensed occupations by 17 to 27 percent and raises consumer prices by 3 to 16 percent. The restrictions fall hardest on workers who are least able to absorb them: immigrants, workers with criminal records, military spouses who relocate across state lines, and low-income individuals who cannot afford the time and tuition required to obtain credentials. A 2016 White House report during the Obama administration estimated that occupational licensing costs the national economy $200 billion per year in higher prices and reduced labor mobility. In California specifically, the Institute for Justice estimates that licensing restrictions have eliminated approximately 196,000 jobs and cost the state economy as much as $22 billion.

## The Contractor Licensing System

The CSLB processes approximately 30,000 to 35,000 original license applications per year, administers more than 58,000 examinations annually through its testing vendor, and handles roughly 20,000 complaints. Women hold approximately 7 percent of California contractor licenses, compared to 11 percent nationally, with the lowest representation in electrical (5.1 percent) and plumbing (5.2 percent) and the highest in landscaping (12.1 percent). The board employs approximately 400 enforcement staff, including investigators who conduct field investigations of unlicensed activity and consumer complaints. The overall exam pass rate for first-time applicants is approximately 50 to 55 percent for the trade examinations and approximately 45 percent for the law and business examination. The combined first-attempt pass rate, requiring passage of both exams, is below 30 percent. The pass rate varies dramatically by classification: some specialty trades have pass rates above 70 percent while others fall below 40 percent.

The CSLB's data portal, one of the more accessible state licensing databases, publishes individual license records including license number, classification, status, bond information, workers' compensation insurance status, and complaint and legal action history. The portal allows consumers to verify that a contractor is licensed and in good standing before hiring them. Approximately 2.5 million license verification searches are conducted per year through the portal, a volume that suggests active consumer use of the system for its intended purpose.

The license itself requires a contractor to maintain a $25,000 surety bond (raised from $15,000 in 2023) and to carry workers' compensation insurance if they have employees. These requirements function as financial floors: a contractor who cannot obtain a bond or insurance cannot hold a license. The bond amount, while increased, covers only a fraction of the potential damage from a failed construction project. A homeowner who hires a licensed contractor whose $100,000 kitchen remodel goes wrong has recourse to the $25,000 bond and the CSLB's complaint process. A homeowner who hires an unlicensed contractor has no bond, no insurance requirement, and no regulatory body to which to complain.

## The 58-Board Archipelago

The DCA's 40-plus boards operate with significant independence. Each board has its own appointed members (typically a mix of licensees and public members), its own staff, its own examination contracts, its own enforcement priorities, and its own processing times. The Medical Board of California processes physician license applications in approximately 60 to 90 days. The Board of Registered Nursing, which regulates the largest single licensed profession in the state (registered nurses represent approximately 20 percent of all DCA licensees, followed by security guards at 10 percent and cosmetologists at 8 percent), has experienced processing backlogs that have extended initial license issuance to four months or more during periods of high demand.

The variation in processing times, examination difficulty, and renewal requirements across boards produces an inconsistent experience for workers and consumers. A profession regulated by a well-staffed board with efficient processes faces a different licensing reality than an identical profession regulated by an understaffed board with outdated examination content and a six-month processing backlog. The quality of licensing, not just its existence, varies dramatically across the DCA ecosystem.

The Little Hoover Commission, California's independent government oversight body, has issued multiple reports recommending consolidation of DCA boards, standardization of licensing processes, and elimination of licensing requirements for occupations where consumer harm is minimal. A 2016 report recommended reducing the number of boards and creating a more unified licensing infrastructure. AB 2138 (2018) barred licensing boards from denying licenses based on criminal convictions older than seven years or not substantially related to the occupation, and SB 878 (2020) required all DCA boards to publish average processing times. But the structural recommendations have been largely unimplemented. Each board has a constituency of licensees who view the board as their professional regulator and advocate, and who resist consolidation with other professions or elimination of requirements they themselves satisfied. The political economy of licensing mirrors the political economy of all regulation: the costs are diffuse (spread across all consumers and aspiring workers) while the benefits are concentrated (among existing licensees who benefit from restricted competition).

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has built a licensing apparatus that functions less as consumer protection and more as a labor cartel. The 3.9 million licensees are not all in professions where unlicensed practice poses genuine risk to health and safety. Licensing a cosmetologist for 1,600 hours protects incumbent salon owners from competition more than it protects consumers from bad haircuts. Licensing a landscape contractor protects established landscaping businesses from competition by workers who learned the trade informally. The 40 boards, funded by the fees of the workers they regulate, have an institutional interest in maintaining the licensing requirements that justify their existence. The result is a system that raises prices, restricts entry, reduces labor mobility, and falls hardest on the workers who can least afford the time and money to navigate it. California's licensing system is a regressive tax on work, collected by the state, that primarily benefits incumbents.

The generous read is that licensing exists because unregulated markets in construction, healthcare, and personal services produce real harm, and California's size and complexity require more regulatory infrastructure than smaller states. The CSLB's 231,000 active licensed contractors are building and maintaining the physical infrastructure of a state with 40 million people, $4.1 trillion in real property, and seismic, fire, and flood risks that make construction quality a life-safety issue. The 2.5 million annual license verification searches demonstrate that consumers value the system and use it. The complaint and enforcement process, while imperfect, provides recourse that does not exist in unlicensed markets. The cosmetology licensing requirements, however burdensome, exist because unsanitary practices in unlicensed salons cause real infections and injuries. The question is not whether licensing should exist but whether California has calibrated it correctly, and the evidence suggests that the state has over-licensed low-risk occupations while under-resourcing enforcement in high-risk ones.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The CSLB data portal publishes license status, complaint history, and legal actions for individual contractors. It does not publish aggregate data in a format that allows systematic analysis of licensing outcomes: what percentage of licensed contractors receive complaints, how complaint rates vary by classification, how complaint rates compare between newly licensed and long-tenured contractors, or whether license revocations correlate with the type of examination passed. The data is structured for individual consumer lookups, not for population-level analysis of whether licensing produces the consumer protection outcomes it is designed to achieve.

The DCA publishes board-level statistics in annual reports but does not maintain a unified data portal across all 40-plus boards that would allow cross-board comparison of processing times, complaint rates, enforcement outcomes, and the relationship between licensing requirements and consumer harm. The infrastructure for asking whether California's licensing system works, not at the individual license level but at the system level, does not exist in the public data.
