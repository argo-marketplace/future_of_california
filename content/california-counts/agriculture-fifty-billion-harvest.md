# California Counts: The $55 Billion Harvest
## What $55 billion in farm gate value, 69,000 farms, 400 commodities, 80 percent of the world's almonds, and 34 percent of the state's water reveal about the industry that feeds the nation from a desert it turned into a garden

California is the largest agricultural state in the nation by a wide margin. Its approximately 69,000 farms and ranches produced approximately $55 billion in farm gate value in the most recent reporting year, roughly 12 percent of the national total and more than twice the output of second-place Iowa. The state produces over 400 commodities, more than any other state. It is the sole or dominant U.S. producer of dozens of crops: almonds (approximately 80 percent of global production), walnuts, pistachios, artichokes, dates, figs, olives, pomegranates, raisins, and processing tomatoes. Approximately two-thirds of the nation's fruits and nuts and one-third of its vegetables are grown in California. The state's dairy industry, concentrated in the San Joaquin Valley, produces approximately 18 percent of the nation's milk.

The industry operates on 24.3 million acres, roughly one-quarter of the state's total land area. Of that, approximately 9 million acres are irrigated cropland, and the balance is rangeland, forest, and non-irrigated pasture. The irrigated acreage consumes approximately 34 percent of the state's developed water supply, roughly 24 million acre-feet in a normal water year, making agriculture by far the largest water user in California. The relationship between water and agriculture is the defining fact of California farming: the Central Valley's soils are among the most productive in the world, but annual rainfall in much of the valley is 8 to 12 inches, insufficient for most crops without irrigation. The entire agricultural edifice rests on an engineered water supply, federal and state reservoirs, canals, aqueducts, and groundwater wells, that transforms a semi-arid landscape into the most productive farming region on earth.

## The Economic Structure

California agriculture is bifurcated. At one end are the large-scale operations that produce the bulk of output: the top 10 percent of farms by revenue generate approximately 75 percent of the state's total farm gate value. These are major corporate and family operations with thousands of acres, sophisticated irrigation systems, mechanized harvesting, and direct relationships with processors and retailers. A large almond operation in the San Joaquin Valley may farm 5,000 to 10,000 acres and generate $20 million to $50 million in annual revenue.

At the other end are the approximately 40,000 farms that report annual revenues below $100,000, many of which are part-time operations, hobby farms, or small-acreage specialty producers. These farms are numerous but economically marginal: they collectively generate less than 5 percent of the state's total farm gate value. Many survive through off-farm income, farmers' market sales, and the economic imputation of owner labor that would not command a living wage if paid as employment.

The county-level data illustrates the geographic concentration. Kern County ($8.9 billion), Tulare County ($7.7 billion), Fresno County ($7.6 billion), and Monterey County ($4.6 billion) are the four largest agricultural counties. Combined, they produce approximately 50 percent of the state's total farm gate value. The Central Valley, running from Redding to Bakersfield, produces approximately 75 percent. Agriculture in coastal California (Monterey, Santa Barbara, Ventura, San Luis Obispo) is significant but oriented toward high-value specialty crops (strawberries, lettuce, wine grapes, avocados) rather than the commodity-scale production of the valley.

## Water and the Future

The intersection of agriculture, water, and climate change is the existential question for California farming. The Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA) will require the retirement of an estimated 500,000 to 1 million acres of irrigated Central Valley farmland by 2040 to bring groundwater extraction into balance with recharge. The acreage retirement will reduce agricultural output by an estimated $5 billion to $10 billion per year and eliminate tens of thousands of farm and farm-related jobs.

Simultaneously, surface water deliveries are becoming less reliable. The State Water Project and Central Valley Project, which deliver approximately 10 million acre-feet per year to farms and cities, have reduced allocations during drought years to as low as 5 percent of contract amounts. The long-term trend is toward less surface water availability for agriculture as urban demand grows, environmental flow requirements increase, and climate change reduces snowpack. California agriculture used approximately 34 percent of the state's developed water supply in 2020. By 2040, that share is projected to decline to 28 to 30 percent as water moves from farms to cities and ecosystems.

The adaptation strategies are already visible: conversion from flood irrigation to drip and micro-sprinkler systems (approximately 60 percent of irrigated acreage is now under efficient irrigation, up from approximately 30 percent in 2000), fallowing of low-value crops in favor of permanent crops with higher per-acre revenue (almonds, pistachios, citrus), and investment in managed aquifer recharge to bank wet-year surplus water underground. The transition is real but incomplete: the remaining 40 percent of acreage on less efficient irrigation systems represents the farms that cannot afford the capital investment ($2,000 to $5,000 per acre) to convert.

## The Labor Dependency

California agriculture employs approximately 800,000 workers during peak season, the largest agricultural labor force in the nation. The workforce is approximately 75 percent foreign-born, predominantly from Mexico, and an estimated 50 to 70 percent is undocumented. The median hourly wage for farmworkers is approximately $18 to $20, above the state minimum of $16 but below the living wage in every California agricultural region. The labor is physically demanding, seasonally variable, and increasingly difficult to recruit: farm labor shortages have been reported with growing frequency over the past decade as immigration enforcement has tightened, younger generations of Latino immigrants have moved into non-agricultural employment, and the physical demands of field work become less attractive relative to service-sector alternatives.

The mechanization response varies by commodity. Row crops (lettuce, broccoli, cauliflower) and tree fruits (apples, peaches, citrus) have proven difficult and expensive to mechanize. Nut crops (almonds, walnuts) are highly mechanized: a mechanical shaker harvests almonds with minimal hand labor. Wine grapes are increasingly machine-harvested in the Central Valley but still hand-harvested in premium coastal vineyards. Strawberries, California's most labor-intensive major crop, require hand picking because the fruit is too fragile for mechanical harvesting. The labor dependency creates both an economic vulnerability (to immigration enforcement and demographic change) and an ethical tension: California's agricultural economy depends on a labor force that the federal government classifies as unauthorized and that the state's own labor laws struggle to protect.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California agriculture is an extractive industry disguised as a pastoral one. It extracts water from overdrafted aquifers, labor from an undocumented workforce with limited legal protections, and soil fertility from land that was desert until engineers rerouted rivers to irrigate it. The $55 billion harvest is real, but the costs it externalizes, the depleted groundwater, the subsided land, the exploited workers, the diverted rivers, the pesticide exposure in farmworker communities, are not subtracted from the ledger. The industry survives on subsidized water (federal and state project water delivered at rates below the cost of delivery), subsidized labor (workers whose low wages are supplemented by Medi-Cal, CalFresh, and public housing), and subsidized environmental damage (aquifer depletion and subsidence whose costs will be borne by future generations). The $55 billion is a gross figure. The net, accounting for externalities, is unknown because no one has calculated it.

The generous read is that California agriculture feeds the nation and much of the world, employing 800,000 people, sustaining thousands of rural communities, and producing food that would be more expensive, less diverse, and less available if California's farms stopped producing. The industry has adapted to every constraint imposed on it: water scarcity (drip irrigation adoption), labor cost increases (mechanization where feasible), environmental regulation (integrated pest management, organic production), and market change (the shift from commodity crops to higher-value specialty crops). SGMA will force a painful transition, but the industry's history suggests it will adapt by intensifying production on the most productive land and retiring marginal acreage. California agriculture is not sustainable in its current form. No $55 billion industry operating in a semi-arid climate on overdrafted aquifers with an undocumented labor force can be. But the trajectory is toward greater efficiency, and the alternative, importing the food California produces from countries with weaker environmental and labor protections, is not obviously better for the planet or its people.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The California Department of Food and Agriculture publishes annual county agricultural commissioner reports with detailed commodity-level production and value data. The data is comprehensive at the commodity level but does not include production costs, farm-level profitability, or the cost of water used. A crop valued at $500 million may generate $50 million in farm profit or may operate at a loss subsidized by land appreciation and tax benefits. The economic health of the farms producing the $55 billion harvest is not visible in the production data, and the agricultural census, conducted every five years by USDA, provides farm financial data only at a high level of aggregation.
