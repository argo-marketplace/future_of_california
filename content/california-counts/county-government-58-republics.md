# California Counts: The 58 Republics
## What 58 counties, $115 billion in combined budgets, a 40-to-1 population disparity, and the largest county in the nation administering programs designed in Sacramento reveal about the government layer California cannot live without and cannot make work

California's 58 counties are the primary delivery mechanism for most state programs. Counties administer Medi-Cal, CalFresh, CalWORKs, foster care, behavioral health, public health, indigent healthcare, indigent defense, elections, property assessment, vital records, and land-use regulation in unincorporated areas. They operate the jail system, the juvenile justice system, the probation system, and the child welfare system. They employ approximately 450,000 workers, more than the state government itself. Their combined annual budgets total approximately $115 billion, funded through a mixture of state and federal pass-through funding (approximately 55 percent), property tax and other local revenue (approximately 30 percent), and fees and other sources (approximately 15 percent).

The disparities among the 58 counties are extreme. Los Angeles County has a population of approximately 10 million and an annual budget of approximately $44 billion. Alpine County has a population of approximately 1,100 and a budget of approximately $35 million. The 40,000-to-1 population ratio produces governmental entities that share a legal structure (the county charter or general law framework, a five-member board of supervisors, elected constitutional officers) but share almost nothing in capacity, resources, or administrative complexity. Los Angeles County's Department of Public Social Services alone has a budget larger than the total budget of 40 individual counties.

## The Realignment Dependency

California's counties have become increasingly dependent on state funding through a series of "realignment" transfers that shifted program responsibility from the state to counties while providing dedicated revenue sources. The 1991 Realignment transferred mental health, indigent healthcare, and social services programs to counties, funded by a share of sales tax and vehicle license fee revenue. The 2011 Realignment (AB 109) transferred responsibility for lower-level felons from state prison to county jail and supervision, funded by a share of sales tax revenue. Each realignment expanded county responsibility while providing revenue that, in the initial years, was approximately equal to costs.

Over time, the costs of realigned programs have grown faster than the dedicated revenue, producing structural deficits that counties must fill from general fund sources or absorb as service reductions. The 2011 Realignment's criminal justice transfer is a representative example: counties received approximately $1.2 billion per year in dedicated revenue to manage a population of approximately 30,000 felons who would previously have been in state prison. The cost of jailing, supervising, and providing services to this population has grown as the population's complexity (mental health needs, substance use, chronic medical conditions) has become apparent, while the dedicated revenue has grown more slowly.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California uses its 58 counties as the delivery arm for programs the state designs, mandates, and partially funds, then blames counties when the programs fail to produce results. The state sets Medi-Cal eligibility and benefit standards; counties operate the enrollment system. The state sets child welfare policy; counties provide the social workers. The state sets behavioral health mandates; counties run the clinics. The arrangement gives the state credit for policy ambition while shifting the administrative burden, the workforce challenges, and the fiscal risk to county governments that vary from a $44 billion bureaucracy to a $35 million operation. The 1,100 residents of Alpine County are governed by the same county charter framework as the 10 million residents of Los Angeles County, a one-size-fits-all legal structure that fits nothing.

The generous read is that the county structure provides a level of local responsiveness that a centralized state administration cannot. County boards of supervisors are elected by local constituents and make decisions that reflect local priorities. The variation across counties, while producing disparities, also produces innovation: counties like Santa Clara, San Mateo, and Alameda have developed programs (universal healthcare coverage, guaranteed income pilots, diversion programs) that have influenced state policy. The county system is California's federalism within federalism, a governance layer that allows the nation's most diverse state to administer programs in ways that account for the difference between a San Francisco census tract and a Modoc County ranch.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The State Controller publishes county financial data through the Counties Financial Data portal. The data reports revenues, expenditures, and fund balances at the department level. It does not report outcomes: how many Medi-Cal applications were processed, how quickly, with what error rate; how many child welfare investigations were completed, with what findings; how many behavioral health clients were served, with what clinical results. The fiscal data describes what counties spend. It does not describe what the spending produces. The gap between fiscal transparency and programmatic accountability is the fundamental limitation of California's county data infrastructure.
