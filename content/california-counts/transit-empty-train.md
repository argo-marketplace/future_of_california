# California Counts: The Empty Train
## What a 40 percent ridership decline, $29 per boarding on BART, $290 billion in transit capital needs, and a fiscal cliff that nearly shuttered the Bay Area's subway reveal about the public transit system California built for a commute that may not come back

California operates approximately 200 public transit agencies, the most of any state. They range from the Bay Area Rapid Transit system (BART), which operates 131 miles of heavy rail across five counties, to rural dial-a-ride services that transport a few dozen passengers per day. The state's transit agencies collectively provided approximately 1.1 billion unlinked passenger trips in 2019, the last pre-pandemic year. By 2023, that figure had recovered to approximately 660 million, roughly 60 percent of pre-pandemic ridership. As of early 2026, systemwide ridership has recovered to approximately 70 percent. The 30 percent that has not returned represents a structural shift in travel behavior, driven by remote work, that has upended the financial model on which California's transit systems were built.

The financial model assumed commuters. BART derived approximately 70 percent of its pre-pandemic operating revenue from fares, one of the highest farebox recovery ratios of any transit system in the nation. The San Francisco Municipal Transportation Agency (Muni) derived approximately 25 percent from fares. The Los Angeles Metro derived approximately 20 percent. When ridership collapsed in March 2020, fare revenue collapsed with it. Federal emergency funding, approximately $10 billion to California transit agencies through the CARES Act, CRRSAA, and the American Rescue Plan, filled the gap for three years. That funding is now exhausted. The agencies that depended most heavily on fares face the steepest fiscal cliffs.

## The BART Fiscal Crisis

BART's pre-pandemic operating budget was approximately $950 million, of which fare revenue contributed approximately $580 million. By fiscal year 2023-24, fare revenue had recovered to approximately $260 million, less than half the pre-pandemic level. BART spent approximately $840 million in federal relief funds over three years. When the federal money ran out in mid-2025, BART faced an annual operating deficit of approximately $300 million.

The cost per boarding illustrates the structural problem. In 2019, BART's operating cost per unlinked passenger trip was approximately $12. By 2023, with operating costs largely unchanged but ridership at 45 percent of pre-pandemic levels, the cost per boarding had risen to approximately $29. BART is spending roughly the same amount to operate the system while carrying half the passengers, a per-unit cost that no fare structure can recover without pricing out the riders who remain.

The state legislature passed a rescue package in 2024 that provided BART and other Bay Area transit agencies with bridge funding through a combination of regional measure revenues and state transit funding formula modifications. The package bought time. It did not solve the structural problem: BART's ridership may never return to pre-pandemic levels because the downtown San Francisco office workers who generated the majority of peak-hour trips are working from home two to three days per week. The system was built for five-day-a-week peak commuters. It now serves a ridership pattern that is flatter, more distributed across the day, and fundamentally smaller.

## Los Angeles Metro: Building Into Uncertainty

The Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority is in the middle of the largest transit capital program in the nation, spending approximately $20 billion on extensions and new lines for the 2028 Olympic Games and beyond. The Purple Line extension to Westwood, the Crenshaw/LAX Line, the Regional Connector (completed 2023), the East San Fernando Valley Light Rail, and the Sepulveda Transit Corridor collectively represent a generational investment in rail infrastructure for a region that has been defined by the automobile since the 1940s.

The capital spending is funded primarily by Measure R (2008) and Measure M (2016), two half-cent sales tax measures that generate approximately $3.5 billion per year combined and are projected to fund $120 billion in transportation improvements over 40 years. The voter-approved funding provides a stable capital base that most transit agencies lack.

The operating challenge is different. Metro's bus system, which carries approximately 70 percent of the agency's total ridership, has experienced a longer and deeper ridership decline than the rail system. Pre-pandemic bus ridership was approximately 280 million annual boardings. By 2023, it had declined to approximately 190 million. The decline predates the pandemic: Metro bus ridership peaked in 2006 at approximately 380 million and has declined steadily for nearly two decades, driven by fare increases, service cuts, competition from ride-hailing, and the suburbanization of the low-income populations that are transit's core ridership in Los Angeles. Metro is building a $20 billion rail network while the bus system that serves the majority of its riders quietly deteriorates.

## The State Role: SB 125 and Operating Funding

Historically, California has funded transit capital through bonds and formula programs but has not provided significant operating support. The Transit and Intercity Rail Capital Program (TIRCP) distributes cap-and-trade revenue for capital projects. The State Transit Assistance program distributes sales tax on diesel fuel for operations and capital. But the state's transit operating funding has been modest relative to the scale of the systems it supports.

SB 125 (2023) changed this by providing $5.1 billion in transit funding over five years, including $4 billion in operating support conditioned on transit agencies meeting performance benchmarks for ridership recovery, service levels, and fare integration. The law also required the Bay Area's 27 transit agencies to develop a regional network management structure, addressing the fragmentation that produces overlapping routes, incompatible fare systems, and confusing transfers across agency boundaries.

The $5.1 billion is significant but temporary. The underlying structural question is whether California's transit agencies can rebuild ridership to a level that supports their operating costs, or whether the state will need to provide permanent operating subsidies to maintain service levels. The 27 transit agencies in the Bay Area alone have combined annual operating budgets exceeding $4 billion. The $5.1 billion over five years covers approximately 25 percent of one region's operating costs for the funding period. A permanent operating subsidy at that scale would require a dedicated revenue source that the legislature has not identified.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California spent decades and tens of billions of dollars building transit infrastructure for a commuting pattern that the pandemic proved was optional for a large share of riders. BART was designed to carry 400,000 daily riders from suburban stations to downtown San Francisco. When those riders stopped commuting, the system's financial model collapsed. LA Metro is spending $20 billion on rail lines while its bus ridership, the backbone of its actual service, has declined 50 percent from its peak. The state's 200 transit agencies operate fragmented, uncoordinated networks that require a car to reach in most of the state, serving a transit-dependent population (low-income workers, elderly, disabled) while the choice riders who generate fare revenue have alternatives (cars, ride-hailing, remote work) that are faster and more convenient. California has a transit spending program. It does not have a transit system, because a system implies coordination, integration, and a service model designed around how people actually travel rather than how planners imagined they would.

The generous read is that transit infrastructure is a long-term investment, and evaluating it against the disruption of a once-in-a-century pandemic is like evaluating a bridge during an earthquake. Remote work is real, but hybrid work still produces three days of commuting per week, and employment growth will generate new riders over time. LA Metro's rail expansion will serve a metropolitan area of 10 million people for 50 to 100 years, a time horizon over which current ridership trends are a rounding error. BART's ridership is recovering, slowly. SB 125's performance-based funding model and regional coordination requirements are the first serious attempt to build a functional transit network from the fragments. And the climate imperative, reducing vehicle miles traveled to meet the state's greenhouse gas targets, requires transit investment regardless of current ridership. California cannot achieve its 2045 carbon neutrality goal without a functional transit system. The question is not whether to invest but whether the institutions can adapt their service models to a changed commuting landscape.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The National Transit Database, which all recipients of federal transit funding must report to, provides standardized data on ridership, operating costs, capital costs, service levels, and safety for every transit agency in the country. It does not capture the rider experience: the time spent waiting for a bus that doesn't come, the transfers between agencies that don't coordinate schedules, the stations that feel unsafe, or the first-and-last-mile gap between where transit stops and where people live. The data shows that 660 million trips were taken. It does not show the trips that were not taken because the service was too slow, too unreliable, or too inaccessible to compete with alternatives.
