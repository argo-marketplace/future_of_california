# California Counts: The Seconds That Matter
## What ShakeAlert's 1,100 sensors, a 5-to-20-second warning window, 50 million potential recipients, and a magnitude 7.0 event that could arrive before the alert reveal about the earthquake early warning system California built to give people seconds to survive

The ShakeAlert Earthquake Early Warning System, developed by the U.S. Geological Survey in partnership with Caltech, UC Berkeley, and the University of Washington, became publicly operational in California in October 2019. The system uses approximately 1,100 seismic sensors distributed across California to detect the initial P-wave (primary wave, the fast-traveling but less destructive wave that arrives before the damaging S-wave and surface waves) of an earthquake. When the sensors detect an event exceeding a threshold magnitude, the system calculates the estimated magnitude, location, and intensity, and sends alerts to wireless devices, the MyShake app, and connected infrastructure systems within approximately 2 to 5 seconds of detection.

The warning time depends on the recipient's distance from the epicenter. A recipient 20 miles from the epicenter might receive 5 to 10 seconds of warning. A recipient 50 miles away might receive 15 to 30 seconds. A recipient at or very near the epicenter receives effectively no warning, because the damaging waves arrive before or simultaneously with the alert. The warning window is short, measured in seconds, but research on earthquake casualty prevention demonstrates that even 5 seconds of warning is sufficient to take protective action (drop, cover, hold on), stop a car, step away from a hazardous position, or initiate automated safety protocols (shutting gas valves, opening fire station doors, slowing trains).

---

## The Gaps

ShakeAlert's effectiveness depends on sensor density, alert distribution, and recipient response. Sensor density is adequate in urban areas (where sensors are closely spaced) but thinner in rural and mountainous regions, which can produce slower detection and less accurate magnitude estimates for earthquakes in those areas. Alert distribution relies on the Wireless Emergency Alert (WEA) system (cell broadcast), the MyShake app (approximately 3 million downloads in California), and direct connections to infrastructure operators (BART, LADWP, fire departments). The WEA system has latency (2 to 5 seconds to compose and distribute the alert) that reduces the already-short warning window.

Recipient response is the most uncertain variable. Surveys indicate that approximately 60 percent of Californians have heard of earthquake early warning, but only approximately 15 to 20 percent have the MyShake app installed or have configured their phone settings to receive WEA earthquake alerts. The majority of Californians who would benefit from the system have not opted in. And the behavioral response to a seconds-long alert is not automatic: drills (the Great ShakeOut, an annual earthquake drill with approximately 10 million California participants) demonstrate that practiced responses are faster and more effective than unpracticed ones, but drill participation has plateaued.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that ShakeAlert is an engineering achievement with an implementation gap. The system can detect earthquakes in seconds and transmit alerts at the speed of light, but the last mile (getting the alert to the person who needs it, in time, with a response they have practiced) is where the system falls short. The 15 to 20 percent app adoption rate means 80 percent of Californians will not receive a ShakeAlert notification on their phone unless WEA delivers it, and WEA's latency and formatting limitations reduce its effectiveness. For the most dangerous scenario (a magnitude 7.0 or greater earthquake on a fault directly beneath a populated area, such as the Hayward Fault under the East Bay), the warning time at the epicenter is zero. ShakeAlert helps people far from the epicenter but cannot help the people closest to the earthquake who need help most.

The generous read is that ShakeAlert is the first operational earthquake early warning system in the United States, and it works. The system has successfully alerted millions of Californians during moderate earthquakes (including the 2023 magnitude 5.1 Ojai earthquake and the 2024 magnitude 4.4 Highland Park earthquake), providing seconds of warning that enabled protective action. The infrastructure connections are the highest-value application: automatic train braking (BART), automatic gas shutoff (utility connections), and automatic fire station door opening save lives and prevent secondary disasters (fires, derailments) even without individual human response. ShakeAlert's value increases with every connected system, and the buildout of automated responses is ongoing.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The USGS publishes ShakeAlert performance data (detection time, alert accuracy, distribution latency). It does not publish data on recipient behavior (what people do when they receive an alert) or the system's effect on casualty reduction (which would require a comparison with a no-alert counterfactual that is impossible to construct). Whether ShakeAlert has saved lives is theoretically knowable but practically unmeasurable.
