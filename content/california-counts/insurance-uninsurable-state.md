# California Counts: The Uninsurable State
## What 645,000 last-resort policies, $40 billion in fire losses, and a 37-year-old ballot measure reveal about the price of living in California

In May 2023, State Farm, the largest homeowners insurer in California, announced it would stop accepting new applications for property insurance in the state. The company cited "historic increases in construction costs outpacing inflation, rapidly growing catastrophe exposure, and a challenging reinsurance market." It was the largest single withdrawal from a state insurance market in American history. Within months, Allstate, which had already quietly stopped writing new California policies in November 2022, confirmed it had no plans to return. Farmers Insurance capped new policies at 7,000 per month in July 2023 and its subsidiary Farmers Direct exited the state entirely in November 2023, though by December 2024 Farmers had reversed course and expanded to 9,500 new policies per month. Chubb, AIG, Tokio Marine's Trans Pacific subsidiary, and multiple smaller carriers followed the exodus, with Trans Pacific ending more than 10,000 policies in a complete withdrawal. By the end of 2024, the California Department of Insurance had recorded 2.8 million non-renewals in the homeowners market over the preceding three years. The state's insurer of last resort, the California FAIR Plan, saw its policy count surge past 645,000, its total exposure climb to $650 billion, and its financial solvency become a subject of legislative hearings. Then, on January 7, 2025, the Palisades Fire ignited in Los Angeles. The insured losses from that fire and the concurrent Eaton Fire would reach an estimated $28 to $45 billion, with Swiss Re recording $40 billion in wildfire insured losses for all of 2025, nearly all from these two fires, making them the costliest wildfire event in world history. The California insurance crisis is not a crisis of fire. It is a crisis of price, regulation, and the collision between a 37-year-old ballot measure and a climate that no longer behaves the way it did when the voters approved it.

## The Exits: 2.8 Million Non-Renewals in Three Years

The insurer departures followed a pattern. First came the quiet reductions: companies stopped marketing in fire-prone ZIP codes, raised deductibles, and declined to renew policies at the margins. Then came the public announcements. Allstate stopped accepting new applications in California in November 2022. State Farm followed in May 2023. Farmers Insurance announced in July 2023 that it would not renew approximately 100,000 policies statewide, a 30 percent reduction. USAA limited new homeowners policies in California. Smaller carriers, including Pacific Specialty and Merced Property and Casualty, entered liquidation.

The California Department of Insurance tracks non-renewals through annual data calls. CDI data shows roughly 900,000 to one million total non-renewals per year in the California voluntary homeowners market from 2019 through 2022, with approximately 20 to 25 percent being insurer-initiated rather than policyholder-driven. Non-renewals increased 42 percent between 2020 and 2022 in the top 10 fire-distressed counties. Even in areas with low wildfire risk, non-renewals rose, driven by reinsurance costs that nearly doubled after 2017 and catastrophe modeling that priced California as a whole differently than its individual ZIP codes.

State Farm remained California's largest insurer even after halting new business, with approximately 1.2 million homeowners policies in force. In March 2024, State Farm announced it would non-renew 72,000 policies across California, including 30,000 in areas with no elevated wildfire risk. The company filed for a 22 percent rate increase. The Department of Insurance rejected the request as excessive, then provisionally accepted a revised filing in 2025. Allstate, meanwhile, secured approval for a 34.1 percent rate hike in 2024. In May 2026, Governor Newsom publicly warned insurance companies after the CDI took enforcement action against State Farm for 398 violations in claims handling following the LA fires, calling the industry's conduct "unacceptable."

The market contraction is not uniform. Insurers continue to write policies in low-risk urban areas where they can price profitably under the existing regulatory framework. They have withdrawn from the wildland-urban interface, the mountain communities, the canyon neighborhoods, and the rural foothill properties where wildfire risk is concentrated. The result is a sorting: wealthy coastal and urban homeowners retain coverage, while homeowners in fire-prone areas are funneled toward the insurer of last resort.

## The FAIR Plan: From Backstop to Frontline

The California FAIR Plan was created in 1968 after the Watts riots, when private insurers refused to write policies in neighborhoods damaged by civil unrest. Its name is an acronym: Fair Access to Insurance Requirements. It was designed as a temporary residual market, an insurer of last resort for homeowners who could not find coverage in the private market. It was never designed to be the primary insurer for nearly half a million California households.

As of December 2025, the FAIR Plan holds more than 645,000 policies, a 420 percent increase from 124,000 in 2019. By early 2026, the count had reached approximately 661,000. Total insured exposure has grown from $50 billion to $650 billion as of June 2025, a 289 percent increase since 2021. The average FAIR Plan premium is approximately $3,200 per year, more than double the statewide average. Growth slowed slightly in the first quarter of 2026, at 2.4 percent, but the trajectory remains steeply upward.

The FAIR Plan is not a government agency. It is a joint reinsurance association funded by assessments on all private insurers doing business in California. Every insurer pays into the FAIR Plan in proportion to its market share. If the FAIR Plan's claims exceed its reserves and reinsurance, it can assess its member companies, and those companies can pass the assessments through to all California policyholders in the form of surcharges. The mechanism means that a catastrophic loss to the FAIR Plan is ultimately paid by every insured homeowner in California, whether or not they live in a fire zone.

Coverage under the FAIR Plan is limited. Until 2025, the maximum dwelling coverage was $3 million, a cap that excluded many homes in the state's most expensive fire-prone neighborhoods, including Pacific Palisades, Malibu, and the Oakland-Berkeley hills. Commissioner Lara expanded coverage limits and authorized the FAIR Plan to write commercial, HOA, and agricultural policies. But FAIR Plan policies are bare-bones compared to standard market coverage: they cover fire and a limited set of perils, with no liability coverage, no theft coverage, and limited contents coverage. Policyholders must purchase a separate "difference in conditions" policy, called a DIC wrap, to approximate standard homeowners coverage. The combined cost of a FAIR Plan policy plus a DIC wrap is typically two to five times the cost of a standard market policy.

The FAIR Plan's financial position entered legislative scrutiny after the January 2025 fires. The plan had approximately $4.8 billion in combined exposure in the Palisades and Eaton fire zones. Total estimated losses from the fires reached $4.1 billion, against reserves of $377 to $458 million. The gap was enormous. In February 2025, the FAIR Plan levied a $1 billion assessment on all private insurers doing business in California, the mechanism by which catastrophic losses are socialized across the entire market. As of September 2025, nearly 40,000 claims had been processed and more than $20 billion paid across all insurers. The Assembly Insurance Committee held oversight hearings in January and May 2026.

## Proposition 103: The 37-Year-Old Constraint

California's insurance market operates under rules that no other major state shares. Proposition 103, approved by 51 percent of California voters in November 1988, was a consumer protection measure born from outrage over skyrocketing auto and homeowners insurance premiums. It established three principles that still govern the market.

First, prior approval: insurers cannot raise rates without the Department of Insurance's explicit authorization. In most states, insurers file rates and begin charging them immediately while regulators review. In California, the rate does not take effect until the commissioner approves it. The approval process routinely takes 12 to 18 months, and some filings take years. The lag means that insurers cannot adjust prices in real time as risk conditions change.

Second, public participation: any member of the public can intervene in a rate hearing, challenging the insurer's data, methodology, and proposed rates. Consumer advocacy organizations, particularly Consumer Watchdog, have intervened in hundreds of proceedings, often securing reductions or delays. The intervenor process adds time, cost, and uncertainty to every rate filing.

Third, and most consequentially for the wildfire crisis: Prop 103 required that rates be based primarily on historical loss data. The Department of Insurance interpreted this to mean that insurers could not use forward-looking catastrophe models to set rates. A catastrophe model uses climate science, weather patterns, fuel loads, and building characteristics to project future losses. Every other insurance market in the world uses catastrophe models. California, until 2024, prohibited them.

The practical effect was that California insurers were required to price wildfire risk based on what had already burned, not on what climate models predicted would burn. As wildfire seasons intensified, with 2020 alone burning 4.2 million acres, the most in recorded state history, and the 2017 and 2018 seasons destroying more than 30,000 structures combined, the gap between historical rates and projected risk widened. Insurers could see the future. They were required to price for the past. When the gap became large enough, they stopped writing policies rather than continue losing money on every policy they wrote.

## The Fires: $28 to $56 Billion in January

On January 7, 2025, driven by Santa Ana winds exceeding 100 miles per hour, the Palisades Fire ignited in the hills above Pacific Palisades, a neighborhood of multimillion-dollar homes in western Los Angeles. The concurrent Eaton Fire started near Altadena in the foothills of the San Gabriel Mountains. Together, the fires burned more than 37,000 acres, destroyed or damaged 16,251 structures, and forced the evacuation of more than 200,000 residents.

Verisk, the leading catastrophe loss modeling firm, estimated insured losses between $28 billion and $35 billion. CoreLogic issued a wider estimate of $35 to $45 billion. Swiss Re recorded $40 billion in wildfire insured losses for all of 2025, nearly all from these two fires. AccuWeather placed total economic damages, including uninsured losses, at $250 to $275 billion. Even at the low end, the January 2025 fires were the costliest wildfire disaster in world history, exceeding the combined insured losses of the 2017 and 2018 California fire seasons.

The FAIR Plan's exposure in the fire zones was $4.8 billion. Total losses to the plan reached an estimated $4.1 billion. In February 2025, the FAIR Plan levied a $1 billion assessment on its member insurers. Those insurers can pass the assessments through to all eight million homeowners insurance policies in California in the form of surcharges, meaning every California homeowner pays for the concentration of risk in the state's most fire-prone and expensive neighborhoods.

UCLA's Anderson Forecast estimated the total economic impact of the LA fires at $95 to $164 billion, making them the costliest natural disaster in California history and one of the costliest in American history, approaching Hurricane Katrina's inflation-adjusted total of $200 billion.

The fires did not start the insurance crisis. But they transformed it from a slow-motion market contraction into an acute financial emergency.

## The Reform: Catastrophe Models After 37 Years

Commissioner Ricardo Lara announced the Sustainable Insurance Strategy in September 2023. It was the most extensive overhaul of California insurance regulation in 30 years, and its central provision was the reversal of the catastrophe model ban. Under the new rules, insurers would be permitted to use forward-looking wildfire risk models in setting rates, subject to CDI review and validation.

The trade was explicit. In exchange for the right to price for future risk, insurers would be required to write policies in fire-prone areas. The specific mechanism: any insurer using a CDI-approved catastrophe model to justify a rate increase must commit to writing at least 85 percent of its statewide market share in FAIR Plan-distressed areas. An insurer that wants higher rates must accept higher-risk customers. The regulation flipped the market dynamic: under Prop 103 as previously interpreted, insurers could not price for risk and chose not to write. Under the Sustainable Insurance Strategy, insurers can price for risk but must write.

The Legislature codified elements of the strategy in SB 1060, signed in 2024. SB 1060 added a requirement that catastrophe models account for risk reduction: home hardening, defensible space, and community mitigation efforts must factor into the modeled rates, not just the raw fire risk. The CDI completed its evaluation of three approved forward-looking catastrophe models by 2025. In a first for California, Commissioner Lara announced enforcement actions against insurers that failed to meet the 85 percent writing requirement. By May 2026, multiple insurers had filed rate increases using catastrophe models, with requests ranging from 15 to 35 percent. California premiums had already risen 41 percent from 2023 to 2025, with a projected further 16 percent increase in 2026.

Consumer advocates split on the reform. Consumer Watchdog, the organization most responsible for enforcing Prop 103's constraints, argued that catastrophe models would be used to justify excessive rate increases and that the 85 percent writing requirement was unenforceable. United Policyholders, a nonprofit advocacy group, cautiously supported the reform as necessary to bring insurers back into the market. A 2025 ballot measure to fully repeal Proposition 103 was filed, though its prospects remained uncertain.

The question the reform cannot answer is whether any rate is high enough to make California's highest-risk properties insurable at a price homeowners can afford. A home in a canyon in Malibu or a ridgeline in the Oakland hills may be uninsurable at any actuarially sound rate that a middle-class household can pay. The reform assumes that a correct price exists for every risk. The fire data suggests that some risks may be correctly priced only at levels that make homeownership impossible.

## The Comparison: California and Florida's Mirror Image

California and Florida are running parallel insurance experiments with opposite structures and converging outcomes. Florida's Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, the state's insurer of last resort, reached 1.4 million policies at its peak in 2023. But the two states' trajectories have diverged. Florida's Citizens has been shrinking, falling from 1.4 million to 780,000 policies as private insurers returned to the market. Its exposure dropped from $620 billion to $295 billion. California's FAIR Plan has been exploding in the opposite direction, growing from 330,000 to more than 645,000 policies over the same period, with exposure rising to $650 billion. California's insurer of last resort now carries more than twice the exposure of Florida's.

The regulatory structures are inverted. Florida uses a competitive, file-and-use system where insurers can raise rates quickly. The result was that Florida homeowners saw average premiums reach $7,100 to $10,200 per year, the highest in the nation, and the market remained in crisis despite the rate flexibility. California uses a prior-approval system where rates are constrained. The result was that California homeowners paid below the national average for decades, but insurers withdrew when they could no longer sustain artificially low rates. Louisiana, facing comparable severity after recent hurricane seasons, saw a 58 percent single-year premium increase and average premiums of $6,000 to $6,300.

Florida's crisis is a price crisis: insurance is available but unaffordable. California's crisis is an availability crisis: insurance is regulated to be affordable but unavailable. Both states arrive at the same destination. Homeowners in the highest-risk areas cannot obtain adequate coverage at a price they can pay, and the insurer of last resort absorbs the risk that the private market refuses.

E&E News reported in 2025 that California had "leapfrogged Florida in US insurance risk," with the January 2025 fires pushing California's total catastrophe exposure above Florida's for the first time. The two states together account for more than a third of the national residual market.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that Proposition 103 worked exactly as designed for 35 years and then stopped working when the climate changed. The ballot measure held insurance premiums below the national average, saving California consumers an estimated $154 billion in premiums over three decades, according to Consumer Watchdog. It created a public participation process that gave consumers a voice in rate setting. And it locked the market into backward-looking pricing precisely when forward-looking pricing became essential. The ban on catastrophe models was not in the text of Prop 103. It was a regulatory interpretation by the Department of Insurance that calcified into de facto law. The interpretation made California the only major insurance market in the world that priced catastrophe risk by looking in the rearview mirror. When the wildfires arrived at a scale the rearview mirror had never shown, the insurers left. The FAIR Plan absorbed what the market expelled. The January 2025 fires produced $4.1 billion in losses against FAIR Plan reserves of less than $500 million, triggering a $1 billion industry assessment. The Sustainable Insurance Strategy is an attempt to reverse 37 years of regulatory path dependence. It may be too late. Insurers that have left California have no obligation to return, and many have reallocated capital to states where the regulatory environment is more predictable.

The generous read is that California's regulatory framework prevented the Florida outcome for decades. California homeowners paid less than the national average for homeowners insurance throughout the Prop 103 era, even as they lived in one of the most disaster-prone states in the country. The prior-approval process prevented the kind of annual premium spikes that Florida homeowners now experience. The public intervenor process held insurers accountable in a way that no other state replicates. The crisis is not caused by regulation. It is caused by wildfire. California's 2020 fire season burned 4.2 million acres, the most in recorded state history. The 2017 and 2018 fire seasons destroyed more than 30,000 structures combined. The January 2025 fires produced $28 to $45 billion in insured losses from a single event. No regulatory framework, whether prior-approval or file-and-use, can make those losses affordable. Florida's deregulated market has higher premiums and the same availability crisis. The Sustainable Insurance Strategy is the right response: allow realistic pricing in exchange for mandatory availability, and let the FAIR Plan shrink as the private market returns. The alternative, abandoning rate regulation entirely, would produce Florida-level premiums without solving the underlying problem of concentrated catastrophe risk.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The CDI publishes non-renewal data, rate filings, and market share reports. The FAIR Plan publishes policy counts and exposure totals. CAL FIRE publishes acres burned and structures destroyed. One number that does exist is the uninsured rate. An estimated 806,600 California homeowners, 10.5 percent, carry no homeowners insurance at all. Moody's has estimated the private-insurance coverage gap at $800 billion to $1.3 trillion. Nearly 75 percent of disaster victims are underinsured, carrying coverage limits far below the cost of rebuilding. Mortgage lenders require insurance, but homeowners who have paid off their mortgages are not required to carry it, and rising premiums are pushing more of them to drop coverage entirely.

The data also does not capture the secondary economic effects. A home that cannot be insured cannot be easily mortgaged. A home that cannot be mortgaged cannot be easily sold. Insurance-related home sale cancellations nearly doubled from 2023 to 2024. Property values in fire-prone areas of California have begun to decouple from comparable properties in low-risk areas, but the magnitude and permanence of the discount is too early to measure. Without insurance, homes become unmortgageable, creating a downward spiral: declining property values reduce the local tax base, which reduces funding for the fire mitigation that could make the properties insurable again. The insurance crisis is becoming a housing crisis within the housing crisis, compounding the state's existing affordability challenges with an availability constraint that no amount of new construction can solve.

And the data says nothing about the global reinsurance market that ultimately drives California's insurance prices. Swiss Re and Munich Re, the world's two largest reinsurers, have repriced wildfire risk globally in response to California's losses. Reinsurance costs nearly doubled after 2017, with a 35 percent spike in 2023 alone. Global insured natural catastrophe losses exceeded $100 billion for the sixth consecutive year in 2025, reaching $107 billion, with wildfire the fastest-growing risk category at approximately 12 percent annual growth in insured losses. Industry estimates that reinsurance pass-through, now permitted for the first time under the Sustainable Insurance Strategy, could add 40 to 50 percent to California premiums. The California Department of Insurance regulates primary insurers. It does not regulate the reinsurance market. The price of living in a fire-prone California neighborhood is set, in part, by underwriters in Zurich and Munich who have never visited the property.

---

Fourteen million Californians, one in three, live in the wildland-urban interface, which encompasses $1.3 trillion in real estate. The state has an insurance market that lost its three largest writers of new business in a single year. It has an insurer of last resort that now covers 661,000 households and carries $650 billion in exposure. It has a ballot measure, approved when the state's largest wildfire season had burned 290,000 acres, governing a market where a single January fire complex can produce $40 billion in insured losses. Total wildfire costs from 2017 through 2021 alone reached $117.4 billion. And it has a reform strategy that asks insurers to return to a market they have spent three years leaving, in exchange for the right to charge prices that have already risen 41 percent and may rise further. The data documents a market in structural failure. Whether the Sustainable Insurance Strategy can rebuild it depends on a question the data cannot answer: at what price does insurance stop being insurance and start being a luxury that only some Californians can afford?
