# California Counts: The Promise That Compounds
## What $267 billion in unfunded pension liabilities tells us about the cost of promises kept

In 2007, CalPERS was fully funded. One hundred and one percent. Assets exceeded liabilities. The largest public pension fund in the United States had enough money to pay every dollar it owed to every current and future retiree. Then the financial crisis erased $100 billion in asset value in 18 months, and the funded ratio fell to 61 percent. Eighteen years later, it has not recovered. As of June 30, 2024, CalPERS held $506.4 billion in assets against $685.0 billion in accrued liabilities, a funded ratio of 73.6 percent and an unfunded liability of $178.6 billion. CalSTRS, the separate pension system for California's public school teachers, carried an additional $88.7 billion in unfunded liabilities at a funded ratio of 76.7 percent.

Combined, that is $267 billion in promises California has made to its public employees and does not yet have the money to keep. The number is not a crisis in the way a bridge collapse is a crisis. Nobody is going unpaid tomorrow. The benefits are flowing. The checks clear. But the unfunded liability is the gravitational force pulling on every city budget, every school district staffing plan, and every state spending decision in California. It is the cost of a promise that compounds.

---

## The SB 400 Story: How the Hole Was Dug

The origin story of California's pension math begins on a specific date. In September 1999, Governor Gray Davis signed SB 400, a bill that retroactively increased pension benefits for state employees, including the now-infamous "3 percent at 50" formula for public safety workers. Under that formula, a police officer or firefighter who worked 30 years and retired at age 50 would receive 90 percent of final salary as a pension, for life. The comparable formula for miscellaneous state employees was "2 percent at 55," producing a 60 percent replacement ratio for a 30-year career.

CalPERS told the Legislature that SB 400 would cost nothing. The fund's own analysis projected that state contributions would remain below the FY1998-99 level of $766 million "for at least the next decade." The logic was simple: the dot-com boom had inflated the fund's assets well past its liabilities. The surplus was real. Why not share it with workers?

Actual state contributions to CalPERS hit $3.9 billion by FY2010-11. By FY2024-25, the annual bill was $8.7 billion. Over 25 years, state taxpayer contributions to CalPERS alone rose from $160 million to $8.7 billion, an increase of more than 5,000 percent. SB 400 was not the only cause. The 2001 dot-com crash, the 2008 financial crisis, and a series of actuarial assumption changes all contributed. But SB 400 locked in the benefit levels that determined the size of the liabilities that the crises exposed.

Local governments followed the state's lead. In the early 2000s, city after city adopted "3 percent at 50" for public safety and enhanced formulas for miscellaneous employees, competing with each other for workers in a tight labor market and relying on the same optimistic return assumptions that CalPERS was using. The contribution rates were low. The stock market was up. The promises were easy to make and expensive to keep.

## The Contribution Ratchet

The mechanism by which unfunded pension liabilities translate into budget pressure is the employer contribution rate, the percentage of payroll that each government employer must pay into the pension system on top of employee salaries.

For state miscellaneous employees, the CalPERS employer contribution rate was near zero during the pension holidays of the early 2000s. By FY2025-26, it had reached 30.87 percent of payroll. For state peace officers and firefighters, the rate is 46.26 percent. For every dollar of salary paid to a California Highway Patrol officer, the state pays an additional 46 cents to CalPERS.

CalSTRS tells a parallel story. The employer contribution rate for school districts was 8.25 percent of payroll from 1986 through 2013, nearly three decades of stability. After the Legislature passed AB 1469 in 2014 to address CalSTRS's growing unfunded liability, the rate was increased every year for seven consecutive years. It reached 19.10 percent in FY2020-21 and has remained there since. The rate more than doubled in a decade.

The total employer contributions flowing into CalPERS from all sources, state, local government, and school districts combined, reached $23.4 billion in 2025. State spending on CalPERS alone doubled from $4.8 billion in 2016 to $9.8 billion in FY2025-26. These are not voluntary investments. They are mandatory payments, set by CalPERS actuaries and enforced by contract law. A city that fails to pay faces legal action. A school district that falls behind has no alternative.

The ratchet works in one direction. When markets crash, unfunded liabilities grow, and contribution rates rise. When markets recover, the gains are spread over 20 years of actuarial smoothing, and rates decline slowly if at all. The asymmetry means that employer costs rise quickly in bad times and fall slowly in good ones. The trajectory since 2008 has been relentlessly upward.

## The Crowd-Out: What Pensions Displace

The most consequential number in pension data is not the size of the fund or the return on investment. It is the share of a city's general fund consumed by pension contributions, because that share determines what is left for everything else.

Statewide, average city pension costs rose from 8.3 percent of general fund expenditures in FY2006-07 to a projected 15.8 percent in FY2024-25. The share nearly doubled. In specific cities, the numbers are higher.

| City | Pension cost as share of general fund | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| Oakland | 28% of unrestricted revenues | $1.8 billion unfunded liability; $133 million in annual interest on CalPERS debt alone |
| San Jose | Over 20% of general fund | Pension costs rose from $73 million (2001) to $245 million (2012); lost 300+ police officers |
| Los Angeles | Projected 27% of general fund | Pension contributions tripled from $350 million (2005) to $1.1 billion; combined unfunded liability exceeds $10 billion |
| San Francisco | $681 million annual SFERS contribution | Only 18% of the $15.9 billion city budget is truly discretionary |

A Stanford SIEPR study examined 14 California jurisdictions from FY2003 through projected FY2030 and found that employer pension contributions grew 400 percent in nominal dollars during FY2003-2018, while general fund operating expenditures grew only 46 percent. Pension contributions as a share of operating expenditures tripled from 3.9 percent to 11.45 percent. Roughly 10 percent of all public revenue generated in California now goes to fund public employee pensions.

The crowd-out is not abstract. It is the police officers San Jose did not hire. It is the fire stations Vallejo closed. It is the road repairs Oakland deferred. It is the school enrichment programs that districts cut as CalSTRS employer rates climbed from 8.25 to 19.10 percent. Every dollar that goes to fund a pension promise already made is a dollar that does not go to a service being delivered today. The trade-off is not optional. It is arithmetic.

## Three Bankruptcies and a Lesson

Between 2008 and 2012, three California cities filed for bankruptcy in which pension costs played a central role.

Vallejo, a city of 115,000 across the Carquinez Strait from the Bay Area, filed in May 2008 with an $18 million deficit. Its police and fire pension formula was "3 percent at 50." Employer contributions consumed 37.6 percent of payroll for both police and fire. In bankruptcy, CalPERS was paid in full. Pensions were not cut. Instead, the city slashed its police force by roughly 40 percent, closed three of nine fire stations, and eliminated retiree health benefits. After exiting bankruptcy in 2011, CalPERS payments rose from $11 million, 14 percent of the general fund, to $15 million, 18 percent, within two years. Moody's warned of the risk of a second insolvency.

Stockton filed in June 2012. It had issued $125 million in pension obligation bonds in 2007, whose invested proceeds lost roughly one-third of their value in the crash. In bankruptcy, CalPERS again received 100 cents on the dollar. Bondholders received 17 cents. The bankruptcy judge ruled that CalPERS pensions could legally be reduced under Chapter 9 but the city chose not to, arguing it needed competitive benefits to retain workers.

San Bernardino filed in August 2012 and took the most adversarial stance, actually stopping CalPERS payments for a period and accumulating $13.5 million in arrears before settling. But it too declined to broadly reduce pension benefits.

The pattern across all three cases was the same: pensions survived intact while bondholders, retiree health benefits, and current services absorbed the cuts. The lesson was not lost on other California cities. Pension obligations are, in practice, the most senior claim on a city's general fund, more senior than bonds, more senior than services, more senior than the roads and fire stations that the city exists to provide. The hierarchy is not written in statute. It is the product of CalPERS's legal posture and the political reality that cutting pensions triggers employee flight in a market where public workers already face a compensation gap.

## The Discount Rate Question

Buried in the actuarial reports is a number that changes everything: the discount rate. CalPERS currently uses 6.8 percent as its assumed long-term rate of return on investments. That assumption determines how future pension liabilities are valued in today's dollars. A higher assumed return makes liabilities look smaller. A lower one makes them look larger.

At 6.8 percent, CalPERS reports a funded ratio of roughly 75 percent and an unfunded liability of $178.6 billion. Stanford's SIEPR pension researchers, led by Joshua Rauh, argue that pension liabilities should be discounted at a risk-free rate, roughly 2 to 4 percent, because the liabilities are backed by the taxing power of government and are virtually certain to be paid. At a risk-free rate, CalPERS's funded ratio drops to roughly 48 percent. The combined unfunded liability of all California public pension plans, which the systems report at roughly $267 billion, balloons to over $1 trillion under market-value accounting. Per household, the gap moves from roughly $20,000 to somewhere between $77,000 and $93,000.

CalPERS's counterargument is that the discount rate reflects expected long-term portfolio returns, not the riskiness of the liabilities. The fund's 10-year annualized return as of June 2025 was 7.1 percent, slightly above the 6.8 percent target. Its 20-year return was 6.7 percent, slightly below. The most recent fiscal year delivered 11.6 percent, well above assumption.

The debate is technical but the stakes are not. If Rauh is right that the true unfunded liability is $1 trillion rather than $267 billion, then the contribution rates required to close the gap are roughly triple what CalPERS currently charges. If CalPERS is right that its investment portfolio will earn 6.8 percent over the long run, then the current contribution schedule, while painful, will gradually close the hole. The distance between those two positions is the distance between a problem that is expensive and a problem that is existential.

## PEPRA: The Reform That Might Work

The Public Employees' Pension Reform Act of 2013 was the most significant structural change to California public pensions in a generation. For new employees hired after January 1, 2013, PEPRA reduced benefit formulas: "2 percent at 62" for miscellaneous workers instead of "2 percent at 55," and "2.7 percent at 57" for safety workers instead of "3 percent at 50." It required employees to pay at least half the normal cost of their benefits. It capped pensionable compensation and closed anti-spiking loopholes.

The savings are real but slow. CalPERS reports $5.8 billion in cumulative PEPRA savings through June 2024, with an additional $25 to $26.5 billion projected if the reforms remain intact. Normal cost reductions run 39 percent for miscellaneous members and 48 percent for safety. As of 2025, approximately 68 percent of CalPERS active members are in the PEPRA tier. That share is projected to reach 90 percent within the next decade.

The critical word is "if." AB 1383, introduced in the current legislative session, would retroactively restore "3 percent at 55" formulas for public safety employees hired after 2013, effectively gutting PEPRA for the most expensive tier of beneficiaries. The estimated impact: $338 million in new annual normal costs and $3.6 billion in present-value liability increases. The bill has the backing of public safety unions, whose members were the primary targets of PEPRA's benefit reductions.

PEPRA's savings accrue through generational turnover: each classic employee who retires and is replaced by a PEPRA employee reduces the system's long-term cost. That process takes decades. The full benefit will not arrive until the last "3 percent at 50" retiree dies, an event that, given current life expectancies, is roughly 40 to 50 years away. AB 1383 would extend that timeline further by restoring the more expensive formula for an entire generation of safety workers. The question is whether California's Legislature will allow the slow savings to compound, or whether political pressure from the unions that represent current workers will override the fiscal interests of the ratepayers and residents who pay the bills.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's pension system is a slow-moving fiscal crisis that has already begun to damage the state's capacity to govern. The $267 billion unfunded liability, which may be $1 trillion on a market-value basis, represents promises made in a different era under assumptions that proved catastrophically wrong. SB 400 was sold as a free lunch. It produced a $8.7 billion annual bill. Employer contribution rates have tripled and quadrupled. Three cities went bankrupt and pensions emerged untouched while services were gutted. The crowd-out is real and measurable: pension contributions grew 400 percent while operating expenditures grew 46 percent. Every year, more of every city's general fund goes to paying retirees and less goes to serving residents. PEPRA was a genuine reform, but it is under legislative attack before its savings have fully materialized, and the political incentives favor rolling it back. The pension promise is a ratchet that only tightens.

The generous read is that CalPERS and CalSTRS are paying 1.7 million retirees and beneficiaries a dignified retirement after careers of public service, and the system is working. The average CalPERS pension is roughly $3,500 per month, not a lavish retirement by California's cost of living. The 55 cents of every pension dollar that comes from investment earnings represents genuine wealth creation, not merely a transfer from taxpayers to retirees. PEPRA has already saved $5.8 billion and will save tens of billions more as generational turnover proceeds. CalSTRS is on track to reach full funding by 2046. CalPERS's 10-year return exceeds its discount rate. The funded ratio has improved from 61 percent post-crisis to 79 percent in 2025. The system absorbed the worst financial crisis since the Depression and continued paying every dollar it owed. The unfunded liability is large in absolute terms but manageable as a share of the California economy, which generates roughly $4 trillion in gross state product annually. Pension debt is not like household debt. It is a long-dated obligation serviced by a perpetual entity with taxing authority. The math is hard but not impossible.

Both readings draw on the same dataset. The pension system is simultaneously the most reliable vehicle for middle-class retirement security that California's public workers have, and the single largest structural constraint on the state's ability to fund the services those workers were hired to deliver. The tension does not resolve.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The actuarial reports measure funded ratios and unfunded liabilities. They do not measure the quality of retirement those liabilities purchase. The average CalPERS pension of $3,500 per month provides a different standard of living in Fresno than in San Francisco. The aggregate numbers flatten a distribution that ranges from part-time municipal workers receiving $500 per month to retired fire chiefs receiving over $200,000 per year. Roughly 5.5 percent of CalPERS retirees receive pensions exceeding $100,000 annually, accounting for 19.5 percent of total pension payouts.

The data also does not capture the counterfactual: what California would look like if it had not made these pension promises. States without defined-benefit pension systems struggle to recruit and retain public workers in a different way, through higher turnover, lower institutional knowledge, and greater reliance on contractors. California's pension system is one reason the state has historically been able to attract talented people to public service. The unfunded liability is the price of that attraction. Whether the price is worth paying depends on what you think public service is worth.

And the data says nothing about timing. A funded ratio of 75 percent is manageable if markets cooperate and contribution rates remain stable. It becomes a crisis if a recession arrives, asset values crash, and contribution rates spike at precisely the moment that tax revenues are falling. The 2008 experience demonstrated this dynamic. The next recession will test whether PEPRA and the subsequent contribution increases have built enough resilience, or whether the system remains one bad market away from another round of service cuts and municipal distress.

---

CalPERS was fully funded in 2007. It was a solved problem. Eighteen years later, the combined unfunded liability of California's two largest pension systems stands at $267 billion, employer contribution rates have tripled, three cities have gone through bankruptcy, and the reform that was supposed to bend the cost curve is under legislative attack before its savings have arrived. The pension promise is not a scandal. It is not a fraud. It is a structural feature of how California chose to compensate its public workforce, made during years of plenty and now serviced during years of constraint. Every dollar of the $267 billion will eventually be paid, because the legal and political system guarantees it. The question is what will not be paid instead.
