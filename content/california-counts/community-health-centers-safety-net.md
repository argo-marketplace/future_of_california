# California Counts: The Safety Net Clinic
## What 1,370 community health center sites, 7.5 million patients served, a 40 percent Medi-Cal caseload share, and a $340 Medi-Cal visit reimbursement that barely covers costs reveal about the federally qualified health centers that provide primary care to the patients no one else will see

California has approximately 1,370 community health center sites operated by approximately 200 organizations designated as Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs). These centers served approximately 7.5 million patients in 2023, approximately one in five Californians. They provide primary medical care, dental care, behavioral health, pharmacy, and enabling services (translation, transportation, case management) regardless of the patient's ability to pay or insurance status. Approximately 70 percent of FQHC patients are enrolled in Medi-Cal, approximately 10 percent are uninsured, and approximately 20 percent have Medicare or private insurance.

FQHCs receive a prospective payment system (PPS) rate from Medi-Cal of approximately $340 per visit (the rate varies by center based on historical costs). The PPS rate is intended to cover the full cost of providing care, including the "enabling services" (language interpretation, care coordination, outreach) that FQHCs provide and that conventional providers do not. The federal government provides supplemental funding through Section 330 grants (approximately $1 billion per year to California FQHCs collectively), which fund operations in underserved areas and for underserved populations. The combined revenue (Medi-Cal PPS, federal grants, patient fees, other insurance) produces operating margins of approximately 1 to 3 percent at most FQHCs, leaving virtually no surplus for capital investment, expansion, or salary increases.

## The Workforce Challenge

FQHCs face the same workforce challenges as the broader healthcare system, amplified by lower compensation. FQHC physicians earn approximately 15 to 25 percent less than their counterparts in private practice or hospital systems. FQHC nurses, medical assistants, and behavioral health professionals earn comparable or slightly lower wages. The compensation gap is partially offset by federal loan repayment programs (NHSC Loan Repayment provides up to $50,000 in exchange for two years of service at an FQHC), mission-driven motivation, and the professional satisfaction of serving underserved populations.

Despite these incentives, FQHC vacancy rates for primary care physicians are approximately 15 to 20 percent statewide, with higher rates in rural and inland centers. The vacancies translate directly to reduced access: patients at FQHCs with physician shortages wait 2 to 6 weeks for primary care appointments, compared to 1 to 2 weeks at adequately staffed centers. The wait time gap is most acute in behavioral health: FQHC patients seeking mental health or substance use treatment face wait times of 4 to 12 weeks, reflecting a statewide behavioral health workforce shortage that is particularly severe in safety-net settings.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's community health center system is an underfunded substitute for the universal healthcare system the state does not have. FQHCs serve 7.5 million patients because those patients have no alternative: they are too poor for private practice, too underinsured for hospital systems, and too geographically isolated for urban medical centers. The $340 PPS rate covers the cost of a visit but does not cover the cost of building the infrastructure (facilities, technology, workforce) required to serve the population well. FQHCs operate on margins so thin that a single bad quarter (a delayed Medi-Cal payment, a key physician departure, a facility repair) can threaten organizational viability. The state depends on FQHCs to provide primary care to one in five Californians and funds them at a level that requires permanent financial precarity.

The generous read is that California's FQHC network is the most extensive community health infrastructure in the nation, serving 7.5 million patients through 1,370 sites that are embedded in the communities they serve. FQHCs provide culturally competent, linguistically accessible care that conventional healthcare systems do not: 70 percent of FQHC patients are people of color, 40 percent are best served in a language other than English, and the centers' community governance model (FQHC boards must be majority-patient) ensures accountability to the population served. The PPS rate, while tight, is a guaranteed payment that commercial providers do not receive (private practice physicians must negotiate rates, manage denials, and absorb bad debt). The FQHC model works: patients at FQHCs have comparable or better primary care outcomes (diabetic control, hypertension management, immunization rates) than patients in private practice, at lower per-patient cost.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

HRSA publishes Uniform Data System (UDS) reports for all FQHCs, including patient demographics, services provided, and clinical quality measures. The data does not include patient experience (satisfaction, perceived access, cultural appropriateness), unmet demand (patients who seek care but cannot get appointments), or the health outcomes of the approximately 3 million uninsured or underinsured Californians who do not access FQHC care despite eligibility. The safety net catches many. How many fall through is not measured.
