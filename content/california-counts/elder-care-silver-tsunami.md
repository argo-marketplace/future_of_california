# California Counts: The Silver Tsunami
## What 9 million Californians over 65 by 2030, 1,100 nursing homes, $400 per day for a bed, and a workforce earning $18 per hour reveal about the elder care system California has not built for the generation about to need it

California's population aged 65 and older is approximately 6.5 million and growing rapidly. By 2030, it will exceed 9 million, roughly one in five Californians. The growth is driven by the aging of the baby boom generation (born 1946-1964), increased longevity, and the reduced in-migration of younger populations. The demographic shift will produce the largest sustained increase in demand for long-term care services in California's history, and the infrastructure to meet that demand does not exist.

The state has approximately 1,100 skilled nursing facilities (nursing homes) with roughly 100,000 licensed beds. Occupancy rates average approximately 75 to 80 percent. The cost of a semi-private room in a California nursing home averages approximately $350 to $400 per day, or $130,000 to $150,000 per year. Medi-Cal pays approximately 70 percent of nursing home residents' costs, at reimbursement rates that average approximately $280 to $320 per day, below the private-pay rate and, according to the California Association of Health Facilities, below the cost of care at many facilities. The gap between Medi-Cal reimbursement and the cost of care is subsidized by private-pay residents (who pay higher rates) and, when the gap is too large, absorbed as operating losses that produce facility closures.

## The Workforce Crisis

California's long-term care workforce, the certified nursing assistants (CNAs), home health aides, and personal care attendants who provide the hands-on care that elderly and disabled Californians depend on, is the lowest-paid healthcare workforce in the state. CNAs earn a median of approximately $18 to $20 per hour. Home health aides and personal care attendants earn approximately $16 to $18 per hour, barely above the state minimum wage. The In-Home Supportive Services (IHSS) program, which provides personal care services to approximately 700,000 low-income elderly and disabled Californians in their homes, pays providers approximately $17 to $20 per hour depending on county, with no benefits, no retirement, and no career advancement pathway.

The turnover rate for CNAs in California nursing homes exceeds 50 percent annually. The turnover rate for home health aides is similar. The workers leave for retail, food service, and other entry-level positions that pay comparable wages with less physical and emotional demand. The shortage is structural: the number of workers needed to care for 9 million seniors by 2030 is estimated at 500,000 to 600,000 direct care workers, roughly double the current workforce. No recruitment, training, or compensation pipeline exists at the scale required.

## The IHSS Colossus

The In-Home Supportive Services program is one of the largest publicly funded home care programs in the nation. It serves approximately 700,000 recipients (primarily elderly and disabled Medi-Cal beneficiaries) through approximately 500,000 providers (most of whom are family members of the recipients). The program's annual budget is approximately $16 billion, funded roughly 50/50 by state general fund and federal Medicaid. IHSS is the primary alternative to nursing home placement for low-income Californians: without it, a significant portion of the 700,000 recipients would require institutional care at costs two to three times higher per person.

The program's structure is unusual. Most IHSS providers are family members, typically adult children, spouses, or other relatives, who are paid by the state to provide care they would otherwise provide unpaid. The arrangement provides income to families (often low-income) while keeping recipients in their homes. It is simultaneously a caregiving program, a poverty alleviation program, and a labor market intervention. The workers were classified as public employees for collective bargaining purposes through legislation in the early 2000s, and SEIU represents IHSS providers in most counties. The unionization has produced wage increases but has not brought compensation to a level that attracts a professional workforce; the program remains dependent on family caregivers who accept the work because they would provide the care regardless.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California knows the demographic tidal wave is coming and has not built the care infrastructure to receive it. The state will add 2.5 million residents over 65 in the next five years while its nursing home capacity is flat, its home care workforce earns poverty wages, and its long-term care financing model depends on a Medi-Cal program that reimburses below cost. The 700,000 IHSS recipients are kept at home by 500,000 family caregivers who are paid $18 per hour to perform work that nursing homes charge $400 per day for, a cost savings that depends on the willingness of families to perform demanding care work at wages that would not attract a non-family workforce. When the baby boomers who need care outnumber the family members available to provide it, the system will face a crisis for which no contingency plan exists.

The generous read is that IHSS, for all its limitations, is a model that other states envy: a publicly funded program that keeps hundreds of thousands of people in their homes, delays or prevents institutionalization, supports family caregivers, and costs a fraction of nursing home care per recipient. CalAIM (California Advancing and Innovating Medi-Cal), the state's Medi-Cal transformation initiative, includes enhanced care management and community supports designed to address the social determinants of health (housing, nutrition, social isolation) that drive elderly Californians into institutional care. The Master Plan for Aging, released in 2021, articulates a vision for a coordinated aging services infrastructure. The vision is ambitious. The implementation funding is modest. But the institutional awareness of the problem, the demographic data, the Master Plan, the CalAIM reforms, is more developed than in most states.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Department of Health Care Services publishes IHSS enrollment and expenditure data. The Department of Public Health publishes nursing home inspection reports and staffing data. Neither publishes a comprehensive supply-demand analysis projecting the gap between the long-term care workforce California has and the workforce it will need by 2030 and 2040. The demographic projections are published by the Department of Finance. The workforce data is scattered across multiple agencies. No single state publication integrates the demand projections with the supply data to produce the gap analysis that policymakers need.
