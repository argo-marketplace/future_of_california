# California Counts: The Underfunded Defense
## What 58 county public defender offices, caseloads double the national standard, $2.4 billion in combined spending, and no statewide oversight reveal about the constitutional right California funds as an afterthought

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to counsel. In California, that right is funded and administered by 58 county governments with no statewide standards for caseloads, qualifications, supervision, or performance. Each county operates its own public defender office (or contracts with private attorneys for indigent defense), funded primarily from the county general fund with modest state and federal supplements. The combined spending on indigent criminal defense across California's 58 counties is approximately $2.4 billion per year, a figure that sounds substantial until measured against the approximately 1.5 million criminal cases filed annually in which a significant majority of defendants qualify for appointed counsel.

The American Bar Association's recommended maximum caseload for a public defender is 150 felonies per year or 400 misdemeanors. In California's largest counties, public defenders routinely carry caseloads of 300 to 500 felonies or 700 to 1,000 misdemeanors per year, double to triple the national standard. A public defender handling 400 felony cases per year has approximately 5 to 6 hours to devote to each case across the life of the case: investigation, client meetings, legal research, motion practice, plea negotiation, and, if necessary, trial. For misdemeanor cases, the time per case drops to 2 to 3 hours. The caseload numbers produce an assembly-line practice in which the vast majority of cases are resolved through plea bargaining with minimal investigation, not because the attorneys are incompetent but because the arithmetic of caseloads makes thorough case preparation physically impossible.

## The County Disparity

The quality and resources of indigent defense vary by county as dramatically as any county-administered service in California. The Los Angeles County Public Defender's Office, the nation's largest, employs approximately 700 attorneys and handles roughly 250,000 cases per year. The San Francisco Public Defender's Office, under longtime public defender Jeff Adachi and his successor Mano Raju, has been nationally recognized for its investigative capacity, trial skills, and client-centered advocacy. Rural counties may have a single public defender attorney handling every criminal case in the county, from traffic misdemeanors to murder.

The funding disparity is significant. Urban counties with progressive political cultures (San Francisco, Santa Clara, Alameda) tend to fund their public defender offices at higher per-case rates, invest in investigation and social worker staff, and provide training and resources comparable to mid-tier law firms. Rural and suburban counties may fund their offices at rates that cover salary and overhead but not investigators, experts, or mitigation specialists, the ancillary resources that effective criminal defense requires.

The Rand Corporation's 2019 study of California's indigent defense system found that per-case spending varied from approximately $500 in the lowest-funded counties to over $3,000 in the highest-funded counties. The 6-to-1 disparity means that the quality of the constitutional right to counsel in California depends on the county of arrest, a geographic lottery that the state constitution does not contemplate and no state law addresses.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California has outsourced a constitutional obligation to 58 county governments and walked away. Unlike K-12 education (where the state sets standards, provides formula funding, and enforces accountability), indigent criminal defense has no state standards for caseloads, no state funding formula, no state oversight body, and no state mechanism for ensuring that the constitutional right to counsel is meaningfully delivered in every county. The 2-to-3x caseload overloads mean that most criminal defendants in California receive representation that falls below any reasonable standard of adequacy. The system processes cases. It does not defend clients. And the defendants processed through the system are disproportionately poor, disproportionately Black and Latino, and disproportionately unable to evaluate whether the representation they received was adequate. The right to counsel is a right on paper. In practice, it is a county budget line item.

The generous read is that California's public defender offices, despite chronic underfunding and impossible caseloads, contain some of the most dedicated and skilled criminal defense attorneys in the nation. The offices attract attorneys motivated by the mission of defending the constitutional rights of people who cannot afford private counsel. The work is demanding, the pay is below private-sector equivalents, and the emotional toll is severe, but the offices produce meaningful outcomes: acquittals, dismissals, reduced charges, and sentencing advocacy that materially affects the lives of their clients. The AB 1621 (2022) initiative to develop statewide workload standards for public defenders, modeled on similar efforts in other states, represents the beginning of a state-level engagement with indigent defense quality that has been absent for decades.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

No statewide data system tracks public defender caseloads, case outcomes, or per-case spending across all 58 counties. The $2.4 billion figure and the caseload estimates are derived from county budget documents, survey data, and academic research rather than from a statewide reporting system. California is one of the few large states without a statewide indigent defense commission or oversight body that collects and publishes standardized data on the system.
