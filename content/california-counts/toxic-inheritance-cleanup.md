# California Counts: The Toxic Inheritance
## What 1,325 toxic sites, $48 billion in estimated cleanup costs, a 30-year average remediation timeline, and a 71 percent correlation with poverty reveal about the land California poisoned and forgot

California has approximately 1,325 sites on the Department of Toxic Substances Control's hazardous waste facility list and an additional 97 sites on the federal Superfund National Priorities List, the second-most of any state behind New Jersey. These are the places where the 20th century's industrial economy left its chemical residue: aerospace manufacturing plants that poured trichloroethylene into the ground, military bases that burned jet fuel in unlined pits, oil refineries that leaked benzene into aquifers, pesticide formulators that contaminated entire neighborhoods, and electronics manufacturers that dumped solvents into storm drains. The sites are not abstractions. They are neighborhoods. The communities living on and adjacent to California's toxic legacy are disproportionately low-income, Latino, Black, and Asian, a correlation so consistent that the term "environmental racism" was effectively coined to describe it. The estimated cost to remediate all identified toxic sites in California exceeds $48 billion. The average time from listing to cleanup completion at a federal Superfund site is approximately 30 years. California has been cleaning up the 20th century's industrial waste for 40 years and is, by every measure, less than halfway done.

## The Inventory: What Is Where

The geography of contamination maps the geography of California's industrial history. The San Fernando Valley, once home to aerospace manufacturers including Lockheed, Rocketdyne, and Hughes Aircraft, contains some of the largest solvent plumes in the nation. The Rocketdyne Santa Susana Field Laboratory in the Simi Hills, where rocket engines were tested and a partial nuclear meltdown occurred in 1959, has been in remediation since 1989 and is not projected to be fully cleaned until 2034 at the earliest. The contamination plume beneath the San Fernando Valley, fed by decades of TCE and PCE dumping, extends approximately 12 miles and affects the drinking water of more than 800,000 residents.

The Bay Area's industrial legacy includes the Hunters Point Naval Shipyard in San Francisco, a former nuclear decontamination site where the Navy cleaned ships exposed to atomic bomb tests at Bikini Atoll. Cleanup began in 1989. In 2018, a whistleblower revealed that the contractor performing radiological surveys had falsified data. The Navy restarted portions of the cleanup. The site is simultaneously one of San Francisco's largest planned housing developments (12,000 units) and one of its most contaminated, a contradiction that has produced litigation, community outrage, and construction delays measured in decades.

The Central Valley's contamination is agricultural and industrial: pesticide residues (including banned organochlorines like DDT and toxaphene), nitrate contamination from fertilizer and dairy operations, and industrial waste from food processing and petroleum production. The Lathrop-Sharpe Depot site in San Joaquin County, a former military storage facility, contaminated groundwater with pesticides and industrial solvents over an area of approximately 5,400 acres. The Stringfellow Acid Pits in Riverside County, a disposal site for industrial waste dumped legally into an unlined canyon between 1956 and 1972, became a federal Superfund site in 1983 and has consumed more than $200 million in cleanup costs without achieving remediation.

Southern California's legacy includes the Exide Technologies battery recycling plant in Vernon, which operated for decades while emitting lead, arsenic, and other metals that contaminated approximately 10,000 residential properties within a 1.7-mile radius. The state ordered Exide closed in 2015. The cleanup of contaminated residential yards, which involves removing and replacing topsoil, has been conducted on approximately 3,600 properties at a cost of roughly $600 million. An estimated 6,400 properties remain unclean. Exide declared bankruptcy, leaving the state and federal government to fund remediation. The residents, predominantly Latino working-class families in communities including Boyle Heights, Maywood, Commerce, and East Los Angeles, lived with lead contamination for decades before regulatory action.

## DTSC: The Agency That Can't

The Department of Toxic Substances Control, the state agency responsible for hazardous waste regulation and site cleanup, has been the subject of more scathing audits, legislative investigations, and restructuring proposals than any other California agency. A 2020 state audit found that DTSC "has not effectively managed the state's hazardous waste cleanup program," with cleanup timelines averaging 15 to 30 years for state-overseen sites and a backlog of more than 500 sites awaiting assessment. A 2014 independent review panel appointed by Governor Brown found that DTSC suffered from "a culture of complacency," inadequate staffing, insufficient enforcement, and capture by the industries it regulated.

DTSC's budget for fiscal year 2025-26 is approximately $420 million, funded primarily through fees on hazardous waste generators and handlers, federal grants, and responsible party reimbursements. The budget supports approximately 1,200 staff positions. The agency oversees roughly 1,325 cleanup sites, 900 permitted hazardous waste facilities, and thousands of annual enforcement actions. The ratio of sites to staff, approximately one cleanup site per staff member (and each staff member also handles permitting, enforcement, and emergency response), makes intensive oversight of any single site impossible.

The Responsible Party model, in which polluters are legally obligated to fund and conduct cleanup under DTSC oversight, works when responsible parties are solvent, cooperative, and identifiable. When they are not, the state pays. Many of California's worst sites involve parties that are bankrupt (Exide), defunct (numerous defunct aerospace and military contractors), or the federal government (military bases cleaned under the Department of Defense program, which operates on its own timeline). The Orphan Site Cleanup Account, which funds cleanups where no responsible party exists, is chronically underfunded, with an annual budget of approximately $20 million against an orphan site remediation need estimated at several hundred million per year.

The governance reforms have been continuous and insufficient. Governor Brown's 2015 restructuring placed DTSC under the California Environmental Protection Agency's direct oversight. Governor Newsom's 2022 executive order directed an organizational assessment. The Legislature has held multiple oversight hearings. DTSC has cycled through directors. Each reform addresses governance. None addresses the fundamental resource mismatch: the contamination is measured in billions, the budget in hundreds of millions, and the timeline in decades.

## Environmental Justice: The 71 Percent Correlation

CalEnviroScreen, the state's environmental justice mapping tool developed by the Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment, scores every census tract in California on cumulative environmental burden, combining pollution exposure (air quality, water contamination, toxic releases, hazardous waste sites, pesticide use) with population vulnerability (poverty, unemployment, educational attainment, linguistic isolation, health conditions). The tool reveals a correlation that is consistent to the point of being structural: 71 percent of census tracts in the top 10 percent of environmental burden are also in the top 25 percent of poverty. Communities with the worst pollution are overwhelmingly the poorest.

The communities adjacent to California's most contaminated sites read like a demographics textbook of disadvantage. The Exide impact zone: 95 percent Latino, median household income $42,000. Hunters Point: 88 percent Black, median household income $30,000 (among the lowest in San Francisco). The West Oakland rail yards and port corridor: 65 percent Black and Latino, median household income $38,000. Kettleman City, near a massive hazardous waste landfill in Kings County: 97 percent Latino, median household income $26,000.

The correlation is not accidental. Industrial zoning decisions made in the early and mid-20th century placed factories, rail yards, refineries, and waste facilities in areas where land was cheap and political resistance was low, which meant communities of color and immigrant communities. The contamination followed the zoning. When properties were subsequently contaminated, property values declined further, attracting lower-income residents who could afford the depressed rents. The cycle is self-reinforcing: pollution depresses property values, depressed property values attract vulnerable populations, vulnerable populations lack the political power to demand cleanup, and the contamination persists for decades.

SB 535 (2012) and AB 1550 (2016) required that 25 percent of California's cap-and-trade revenue be invested in disadvantaged communities as identified by CalEnviroScreen. The Community Air Protection Program, established by AB 617 (2017), required community-level air monitoring and emissions reduction plans in the most impacted areas. These programs represent genuine investment: billions in cap-and-trade dollars directed to environmental justice communities. They do not, however, clean up the legacy contamination. They address future pollution. The past remains in the soil.

## The Military Legacy: Bases That Won't Die

California hosts more active and former military installations than any other state. Approximately 30 former military bases are undergoing environmental remediation, including some of the largest and most complex cleanup projects in the nation. The Department of Defense's Installation Restoration Program and the Base Realignment and Closure (BRAC) process have been cleaning military contamination since the 1980s.

The former Fort Ord on the Monterey Peninsula, closed in 1994, contained unexploded ordnance, lead, TCE, and other contaminants across 28,000 acres. Cleanup has been underway for more than 30 years. Approximately 3,300 acres have been cleared for reuse. An estimated 8,000 acres will take decades more. The cost to date exceeds $1.5 billion. The former Mather Air Force Base near Sacramento, closed in 1993, contaminated groundwater with jet fuel and solvents. Cleanup continues. The former Treasure Island Naval Station in San Francisco Bay, where the Navy conducted radiological research, is being remediated for a mixed-use redevelopment of 8,000 housing units, with cleanup costs exceeding $1 billion and a timeline extending to the 2030s.

The federal government's cleanup pace is driven by Congressional appropriation cycles, DOD budget priorities (which favor readiness and weapons systems over environmental remediation), and a legal framework (CERCLA and the Federal Facility Agreement) that gives the EPA oversight authority but not enforcement power against a co-equal branch of government. States can negotiate, pressure, and publicize, but they cannot compel the federal government to clean faster. California's most contaminated sites are, in many cases, sites where the state has the least authority to accelerate remediation because the responsible party is the United States government.

## The Groundwater Problem

Many of California's most consequential contamination sites involve groundwater plumes, underground pools of contaminated water that migrate slowly through aquifers, spreading contamination over decades. Groundwater remediation is the most expensive, technically challenging, and time-consuming form of environmental cleanup. Pump-and-treat systems, which extract contaminated groundwater, treat it above ground, and reinject clean water, can operate for 30 to 50 years or more. In-situ treatment, which injects reactive chemicals underground to break down contaminants, is faster but less proven at scale.

The San Fernando Valley groundwater contamination, the largest in Southern California, involves a TCE and PCE plume that has contaminated three major drinking water wellfields. The cleanup, overseen by the EPA under the federal Superfund program, has been underway since 1986. Four decades of pump-and-treat operations have removed millions of pounds of contaminants. The plume is not yet remediated. Current projections extend the timeline to the 2050s or beyond.

The intersection with SGMA, the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act, adds a layer of urgency. As California reduces groundwater pumping to achieve basin sustainability, the contaminated aquifers that have been diluted by heavy pumping will concentrate. Contamination levels that were tolerable when mixed with large volumes of clean pumped water may become intolerable as pumping declines and the ratio shifts. SGMA and toxic site remediation are on a collision course in several Central Valley basins where agricultural contamination (nitrates, pesticides) overlaps with industrial contamination (solvents, petroleum) in aquifers that communities depend on for drinking water.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California industrialized, profited, and left the bill in the ground. The aerospace industry that won the Cold War contaminated the San Fernando Valley. The Navy that projected power across the Pacific poisoned Hunters Point. The battery recycler that served the automotive economy irradiated 10,000 homes in East Los Angeles. In each case, the profits were extracted by corporations and government agencies, and the costs were deposited in communities with the least power to object. DTSC, the agency created to manage the cleanup, has been repeatedly found inadequate by its own state's auditors. The estimated $48 billion cleanup cost has no funding source. The average remediation timeline of 30 years means that children born today in communities adjacent to Superfund sites will raise their own children in the same contamination. The 71 percent correlation between toxic exposure and poverty is not a coincidence or an unfortunate side effect. It is the system working as designed: pollution was placed where land was cheap and people were poor, and the cleanup is slowest where the political power is weakest.

The generous read is that California has the most aggressive toxic site identification, environmental justice mapping, and community investment programs in the nation. CalEnviroScreen is a global model for environmental justice analysis, adopted or imitated by the EPA and multiple states. AB 617 created the first community-level air monitoring program in the country. SB 535's requirement that cap-and-trade revenue flow to disadvantaged communities has directed billions toward environmental justice investments. The Exide cleanup, however slow, is the largest residential soil remediation in California history and demonstrates the state's willingness to spend hundreds of millions cleaning up a community that no other state would prioritize. DTSC's reforms, while insufficient, reflect a genuine institutional effort to improve an agency that operates under resource constraints no reform can overcome without additional funding. And the contamination is being cleaned, site by site, year by year, at a pace that is inadequate but not zero. The 30-year timeline is unacceptable. It is also shorter than the timeline in states that have not invested in cleanup at all.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

DTSC's EnviroStor database tracks identified cleanup sites, their status, and responsible parties. It does not track health outcomes for residents of contaminated communities. The epidemiological link between specific contamination sites and specific health effects (cancers, reproductive disorders, neurological conditions) is studied on a site-by-site basis by academic researchers and public health agencies, but no statewide database connects toxic site proximity to health outcomes. The CalEnviroScreen tool combines pollution data with health vulnerability indicators, but it measures exposure potential, not actual health impact. Whether living near a Superfund site causes cancer in a specific individual is a question that epidemiology can address at the population level but not the individual level, a gap that polluters exploit in litigation and that communities experience as a denial of their lived reality.

The data does not capture the sites that have not been identified. DTSC's inventory includes sites that have been reported, assessed, or discovered through regulatory oversight. An unknown number of contaminated sites exist that have not been identified because no one has tested the soil or groundwater. These undiscovered sites are concentrated in areas with limited environmental monitoring: rural agricultural communities, small industrial towns, and older urban neighborhoods where historical industrial activity predated environmental regulation. The contamination exists whether or not it has been catalogued. The inventory is a lower bound.

The data also does not capture the economic cost of contamination beyond cleanup. Contaminated sites depress property values, deter investment, limit development potential, and reduce the tax base of affected communities. A 2019 study by the EPA found that Superfund cleanups increase residential property values within a 3-mile radius by 18 to 24 percent upon completion. The corollary is that unremediated sites are depressing property values by a comparable amount, reducing the wealth of homeowners, most of them low-income, who cannot afford to move and cannot afford to wait 30 years for cleanup. The toxic inheritance is not just environmental. It is economic, generational, and, in the communities that bear it, permanent until someone decides it is not.

---

California's 20th-century economy built aircraft, refined petroleum, tested rockets, processed chemicals, manufactured electronics, and won two wars. It did these things on land that it treated as disposable. The waste went into the ground, the water, and the air, and it stayed there because the people who lived nearby did not have the power to make it stop. Forty years of cleanup have remediated some sites, partially cleaned others, and catalogued the rest. The inventory is 1,325 state sites and 97 federal Superfund sites. The cost is $48 billion. The timeline is 30 years per site. The communities are 71 percent low-income. And the agency responsible has been found inadequate by every audit conducted in the past decade. The toxic inheritance is California's most honest monument to its industrial past: invisible, persistent, concentrated in communities that built the state's prosperity and were repaid in contamination. The sites are not going anywhere. The question is whether the cleanup will.
