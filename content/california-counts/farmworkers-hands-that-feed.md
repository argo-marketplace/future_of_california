# California Counts: The Hands That Feed You
## What 800,000 farmworkers, $59 billion in output, a heat death every nine days, and a median wage of $19.50 reveal about the workforce that grows a third of America's vegetables and three-quarters of its fruit

California's agricultural output of approximately $59 billion in 2024 gross revenue makes it the most productive farming state in the nation by a factor of two. Iowa, the second-largest, produces roughly $36 billion. California grows more than 400 commodities. It produces virtually all of the nation's almonds, walnuts, pistachios, artichokes, dates, figs, kiwifruit, olives, clingstone peaches, persimmons, pomegranates, prunes, raisins, and table grapes. It produces more than 90 percent of the nation's strawberries, broccoli, carrots, celery, garlic, lettuce, spinach, and tomatoes. It produces more than three-quarters of the nation's citrus. Behind every one of these crops is a hand, usually brown, often undocumented, always underpaid relative to the physical demands of the work, that picked it. California's $59 billion agricultural miracle is built on a labor force of approximately 800,000 farmworkers whose median hourly wage is $19.50 and whose median annual income, reflecting the seasonal nature of most agricultural work, is approximately $25,000 to $30,000. The most productive agricultural system in human history pays the people who make it possible less than a beginning fast-food worker earns.

## The Workforce: Who They Are

California's farmworker population is approximately 800,000, a number that fluctuates seasonally and is inherently imprecise because a significant portion of the workforce is undocumented and does not appear in formal employment records. The National Agricultural Workers Survey, conducted by the U.S. Department of Labor, provides the most detailed demographic profile. Approximately 75 percent of California farmworkers were born in Mexico. Approximately 10 percent were born in Central America, primarily Guatemala and Honduras. The remainder were born in the United States or other countries.

An estimated 40 to 50 percent of California's farmworkers are undocumented, meaning they lack legal authorization to work in the United States. They are employed using fraudulent documents, through labor contractors who do not verify immigration status, or in informal arrangements that bypass the employment verification system entirely. Their undocumented status means they cannot access most public benefits, cannot travel freely, cannot complain about workplace violations without risking deportation, and cannot participate in the political process that determines their working conditions. They are essential to an industry that cannot function without them and invisible to a legal system that criminalizes their presence.

The workforce is aging. The average age of California farmworkers has risen from approximately 31 in 2000 to approximately 41 in 2024. Younger workers, including the children of farmworkers, are choosing other occupations: construction, warehousing, food service, and other physically demanding but less grueling work that pays comparable or better wages with more consistent hours. The pipeline of new workers from Mexico has slowed as Mexico's own economy has improved, its birth rate has declined, and U.S. border enforcement has made crossing more dangerous and expensive. The labor shortage is not theoretical. Farmers report difficulty hiring at current wages, particularly for the most physically demanding crops: strawberries, which require stooping; table grapes, which require reaching; and tree fruit, which requires climbing.

## The H-2A Pipeline

The H-2A temporary agricultural worker visa program has expanded rapidly in California as the undocumented workforce has aged and shrunk. H-2A visas allow employers to bring foreign workers to the United States for temporary agricultural work when they can demonstrate that insufficient domestic workers are available. Nationally, H-2A certifications grew from approximately 79,000 in 2010 to more than 370,000 in 2023. California's share has grown proportionally, with approximately 35,000 to 45,000 H-2A positions certified annually.

H-2A workers are tied to their sponsoring employer: they cannot change jobs freely, and their visa status depends on the employer's continued sponsorship. The program requires employers to provide housing (free), transportation (from housing to worksite), meals or cooking facilities, and a wage rate set by the Adverse Effect Wage Rate, which in California was approximately $19.75 per hour in 2024, slightly above the state agricultural minimum wage. Critics argue that the employer-tied structure creates a power imbalance that discourages workers from reporting safety violations, wage theft, or harassment, because losing the job means losing the visa and facing deportation. Supporters argue that H-2A provides legal, regulated employment with guaranteed housing and wages above what workers could earn in their home countries.

The H-2A program's growth represents a structural shift in California agriculture. The workforce is transitioning from a settled, family-based, predominantly undocumented resident population to a temporary, employer-controlled, rotating guest worker model. The implications for rural communities are significant: settled farmworkers, however poor, are community members who rent apartments, patronize local businesses, enroll children in schools, and sustain the social fabric of agricultural towns. H-2A workers live in employer-provided housing, often remote from towns, send remittances home, and leave at the end of the season. The economic and social footprint is different.

## The Heat Toll

Farmwork in California's Central Valley occurs in temperatures that routinely exceed 100 degrees Fahrenheit from June through September. Cal/OSHA's Heat Illness Prevention standard, first adopted in 2005 and among the most stringent in the nation, requires employers to provide water, shade, rest breaks, and training when temperatures exceed 80 degrees, with enhanced protections at 95 degrees. The standard exists because farmworkers were dying.

Between 2010 and 2024, approximately 60 farmworker deaths were attributed to heat illness in California, roughly one every nine weeks during the growing season. The actual toll is likely higher: heat-related deaths are underreported because they can be classified as cardiac events, and because workers who feel symptoms may not report them for fear of losing wages or employment. A 2023 UC Merced study found that heat-related emergency department visits in agricultural counties spike during heat waves at rates three to five times higher than in non-agricultural counties, suggesting that the death count captures only the most extreme outcomes.

Cal/OSHA's enforcement capacity is the bottleneck. The division has approximately 200 field inspectors for the entire state, covering all industries, not just agriculture. Agricultural inspections are a fraction of total activity. Fines for heat illness violations average $15,000 to $25,000 per incident, amounts that are meaningful for small operations but negligible for large agricultural corporations. When a worker dies, penalties can reach $150,000, still a modest cost relative to the revenue of a large farming operation. The deterrent effect is limited by the probability of inspection, which for any given farm in any given year is low.

Climate change is intensifying the risk. The number of days exceeding 105 degrees in the San Joaquin Valley has increased by approximately 15 to 20 percent over the past two decades. Heat waves are lasting longer, starting earlier in the season, and producing higher overnight temperatures that prevent the body's recovery. Agricultural counties in the Central Valley, already the hottest in the state, are projected to see an additional 15 to 30 extreme heat days per year by 2050. The workers most exposed to this trend are the workers least able to protect themselves from it: undocumented, non-English-speaking, employed through intermediaries, and economically unable to refuse work on dangerous days.

## The Housing Crisis Within the Crisis

California's housing crisis is a statewide emergency. For farmworkers, it is a crisis within the crisis. The USDA estimates that 46 percent of California farmworker families live in overcrowded housing, defined as more than one person per room. Many live in garages, sheds, trailers, and vehicles. In the Salinas Valley, where lettuce and strawberry production generates billions in revenue, farmworker families pay $1,500 to $2,000 per month for apartments in a market where vacancy rates hover near 1 percent. In the eastern Coachella Valley, farmworker communities live in mobile home parks with documented contamination issues, including arsenic and nitrate in well water.

Publicly subsidized farmworker housing exists but is vastly insufficient. The Joe Serna Jr. Farmworker Housing Grant Program and the USDA Section 514/516 programs have produced approximately 25,000 units of farmworker housing statewide, serving roughly 3 to 4 percent of the workforce. The Office of Migrant Services operates 24 seasonal migrant housing centers with approximately 2,000 units, available only during harvest season. Waiting lists for permanent farmworker housing are years long.

The H-2A program's employer-provided housing requirement has created a parallel system: bunkhouse-style accommodations, often on farm property or in leased motels, housing eight to twelve workers per unit. The housing meets minimum federal standards but is institutional, temporary, and isolated. Workers with families in their home countries send money home and endure the housing as a cost of the job. Workers who are settled residents of California receive no housing benefit from H-2A and compete for the same constrained rental market as everyone else in agricultural communities.

Local governments in agricultural areas have resisted farmworker housing construction for the same reasons they resist all affordable housing: neighborhood opposition, zoning restrictions, perceived impacts on property values, and the assumption that housing farmworkers is someone else's responsibility. The result is that the workers who produce the food that feeds the nation live in the worst housing conditions in the state, in communities surrounded by the most productive agricultural land on Earth.

## Mechanization: The Coming Displacement

The agricultural labor shortage is driving investment in mechanization at rates not seen since the tomato harvester transformed processing tomato production in the 1960s. Robotic strawberry harvesters, developed by companies including Agrobot and Tortuga AgTech, can identify and pick ripe berries using computer vision and robotic arms. Automated lettuce thinners reduce hand labor by 80 percent. Drone-based crop monitoring, GPS-guided tractors, and automated irrigation systems have already reduced labor requirements for field preparation and crop management.

The mechanization potential varies by crop. Commodities that are harvested mechanically (corn, wheat, cotton, processing tomatoes) have already been automated for decades. The remaining hand-harvest crops, the fruits and vegetables that make California agriculture distinctive, are the ones where mechanization is most difficult and most impactful. Strawberries require gentle handling and selective picking. Table grapes require cluster-by-cluster assessment. Tree fruit requires navigating three-dimensional canopy structures. Each crop presents distinct engineering challenges that are being solved incrementally rather than all at once.

The UC Davis Department of Biological and Agricultural Engineering estimates that mechanization could reduce hand-labor requirements in California agriculture by 30 to 50 percent over the next 20 years. The displacement would affect 250,000 to 400,000 workers, overwhelmingly in the Central Valley, Salinas Valley, and Ventura County. These workers have no obvious alternative employment in their communities. The mechanization transition will be celebrated by economists as productivity growth and experienced by farmworkers as job loss, and both descriptions will be correct simultaneously.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that California's agricultural system is a labor exploitation regime dressed in the language of food security. The state produces $59 billion in agricultural output using a workforce that earns $25,000 a year, lives in overcrowded housing, dies of heat exposure at a rate of one every nine days during summer, and is roughly half undocumented, meaning the workers cannot advocate for themselves without risking deportation. The farms that employ them lobby against immigration enforcement because their business model depends on a labor supply willing to work for $19.50 an hour in 105-degree heat. The H-2A program replaces an exploited undocumented workforce with an exploited documented workforce tied to a single employer. The housing the workers live in is a scandal in any developed country. And the coming mechanization will displace hundreds of thousands of workers from communities that have no other economic base, producing a rural unemployment crisis in the state's poorest region. California's agricultural prosperity is not shared with the people who create it. It is extracted from them.

The generous read is that California agriculture employs 800,000 people, many of them immigrants with limited education and English proficiency, at wages that exceed what they could earn in their home countries by a factor of five to ten. The $19.50 median hourly wage is above California's minimum wage, reflects significant increases driven by minimum wage legislation and labor market tightness, and does not include the in-kind benefits (housing, transportation) provided under H-2A. Cal/OSHA's heat illness prevention standard is the most protective in the nation and has demonstrably reduced heat fatalities since its adoption. The United Farm Workers, founded by Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta, won protections for agricultural laborers that did not exist before the 1960s: the right to organize, overtime pay (phased in under AB 1066), access to bathrooms and drinking water, and pesticide safety standards. California farmworkers have more legal protections than agricultural workers in any other state or country. The protections are imperfectly enforced. They are also more extensive than anything that existed a generation ago. And the food they produce, a third of America's vegetables and three-quarters of its fruit, is not a luxury. It is the national diet.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The National Agricultural Workers Survey provides the best available data on farmworker demographics, but it is conducted by the federal government through employer-facilitated interviews, which may undercount workers who avoid contact with government surveyors, particularly the undocumented. The true size of the farmworker workforce is estimated rather than counted. The true percentage that is undocumented is estimated rather than measured. The true injury, illness, and fatality rates are estimated rather than recorded, because many incidents are not reported.

The data does not capture pesticide exposure, one of the defining occupational hazards of farmwork. The California Department of Pesticide Regulation's Illness Surveillance Program recorded approximately 400 to 600 confirmed pesticide illness cases among agricultural workers per year, but epidemiologists believe the actual incidence is five to ten times higher because most cases are not reported, diagnosed, or linked to pesticide exposure. Long-term effects, including elevated rates of certain cancers, reproductive disorders, and neurological conditions among farmworkers, are documented in peer-reviewed research but not in the state's routine surveillance data.

The data also does not capture what economists call the "true cost" of California's food. The retail price of a head of lettuce reflects the cost of seed, water, fertilizer, equipment, and labor at $19.50 per hour. It does not reflect the cost of the housing subsidy the farmworker does not receive, the healthcare provided through Medi-Cal rather than employer insurance, the educational cost of the children enrolled in underfunded schools, or the long-term health cost of heat exposure and pesticide contact. If these costs were internalized in the price of the food, a head of lettuce would cost more. How much more is debatable. That it would cost more is not.

---

California asks its farmworkers to perform the most physically demanding labor in the state's economy, in the most dangerous heat, for the lowest wages, in the worst housing, with the least political power, and then argues about whether they should be allowed to live here. The $59 billion agricultural system that feeds the nation is built on a workforce that is half undocumented, aging out of the fields, and one enforcement action away from a labor crisis that would empty grocery shelves from Sacramento to New York. Every American who eats a salad, bites into a strawberry, or cracks an almond is a beneficiary of this arrangement. None of them pay the true cost. The farmworker does, in heat, in housing, in health, and in a median income of $25,000 in the most expensive state in the nation, picking the food that makes California the most productive agricultural economy in human history.
