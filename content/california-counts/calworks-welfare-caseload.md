# California Counts: The Welfare That Shrank
## What a caseload that fell from 600,000 families in 1998 to 150,000 in 2025, a maximum monthly grant of $1,107 for a family of three, a federal block grant frozen at 1997 levels, and a 60-month lifetime limit reveal about the cash assistance system California has operated since welfare reform

In 1996, Congress passed the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act and ended the federal entitlement to cash welfare that had existed since the New Deal. In its place came Temporary Assistance for Needy Families, a block grant that gave states fixed federal funds and broad latitude to design their own welfare programs. California created CalWORKs, the California Work Opportunity and Responsibility to Kids program, in 1998. At launch, the program served approximately 600,000 families, representing roughly 1 in 10 California households with children. By 2025, the CalWORKs caseload had fallen to approximately 140,000 to 160,000 families, a decline of more than 75 percent. The question the data poses but does not answer is whether this decline represents success or exclusion.

---

## The Caseload Collapse

The shape of the CalWORKs caseload over 27 years is unmistakable: a steep initial decline from 1998 to 2001, a partial recovery during the 2001 recession, a sharp drop after the 2003-04 welfare reform tightenings, a significant increase during the 2008-09 recession to approximately 580,000 cases, another decline through the 2010s as the economy recovered, and then a continued decline through 2020-25 that brought the caseload to its lowest level in the program's history. The 2020-21 period is anomalous: despite the COVID recession being severe, the caseload did not spike as it did in 2008-09, in part because expanded unemployment insurance and stimulus payments provided income support that reduced the number of families meeting CalWORKs eligibility criteria.

The peak-to-trough decline of more than 75 percent is more dramatic than the decline in California poverty over the same period. The official poverty rate for California families with children has declined, but not by 75 percent. In 1998, approximately 18 percent of California children lived in families below the federal poverty line. By the early 2020s, the Supplemental Poverty Measure, which accounts for government assistance, housing costs, and tax credits, placed the child poverty rate at approximately 11 to 13 percent. The caseload declined by more than the poverty rate declined. That gap is the interpretive puzzle at the center of CalWORKs data.

---

## The Grant: What $1,107 Buys in a High-Cost State

California's maximum CalWORKs grant for a family of three (one adult, two children) in a high-cost region is approximately $1,107 per month as of 2025. The amount varies by county, with 49 of California's 58 counties classified as high-cost and receiving the higher grant amount. The 2025 maximum represents a significant increase from historical levels: the grant was approximately $638 per month in 2012 and has been increased multiple times through the 2010s and 2020s as the Legislature responded to advocacy campaigns and documented inadequacy.

Even at $1,107 per month, the CalWORKs grant covers approximately 45 to 50 percent of the federal poverty level for a family of three, which is $2,072 per month in 2025. It does not cover rent in any major California metropolitan area, where median one-bedroom rents run $1,800 to $3,000 per month in San Francisco, Los Angeles, and San Diego. CalWORKs recipients are also eligible for CalFresh (food assistance), Medi-Cal, and subsidized childcare, and the combined package of benefits is more substantial than the cash grant alone. But the cash grant, which provides the discretionary income to cover rent, utilities, transportation, and other necessities, leaves families far below what market conditions in California's cities require for basic stability.

The grant has not kept pace with inflation over the full history of the program. The 1998 maximum grant of approximately $656 per month for a family of three would be worth approximately $1,300 in 2025 dollars. The current grant of $1,107 is roughly 15 percent below its inflation-adjusted 1998 value, despite the nominal increases of recent years.

## The Federal Block Grant: Frozen Since 1997

California's TANF block grant from the federal government is approximately $3.7 billion per year. That figure has not changed since 1997. Over 28 years of inflation, the real value of the federal TANF block grant to California has declined by approximately 40 percent from its original purchasing power. A grant that was worth $3.7 billion in 1997 dollars would be worth approximately $7.0 billion in 2025 dollars. California receives $3.3 billion less in real federal welfare funding than it did at the program's launch, even as the state's population has grown, the cost of living has increased, and the state has expanded CalWORKs benefits.

California supplements the federal block grant with approximately $1.5 to $2.0 billion per year in state General Fund dollars, making the total CalWORKs program budget roughly $5 billion to $6 billion annually. The state uses flexibility within the TANF rules to spend federal funds on a broad range of anti-poverty programs beyond cash grants, including subsidized childcare, domestic violence services, and county administrative costs for eligibility determination.

The 60-month federal lifetime limit on TANF-funded assistance is a structural feature of the program that creates a category of "safety net" cases: families who have exhausted their federal TANF eligibility but remain eligible under California's state-only Safety Net program. California funds a reduced grant for these families, approximately 55 percent of the maximum amount, using state dollars only. Approximately 30,000 to 40,000 families per year receive Safety Net grants, a population that has been on the program long enough to exhaust the federal time limit but remains in poverty without assistance.

## Work Participation and the Administrative Machine

CalWORKs requires adult recipients to participate in work, job training, or approved activities for 35 hours per week (30 hours for single parents with children under 6). The state's work participation rate, a federally required metric measuring the share of eligible adults engaged in approved activities, has been a chronic challenge. California has historically failed to meet the federal TANF work participation rate of 50 percent for two-parent families, relying instead on caseload reduction credits that reduce the required rate in proportion to caseload declines. Because California's caseload has declined significantly from the 1995 baseline used for the credit calculation, the effective required rate is lower than the nominal 50 percent.

The work participation requirements generate a significant administrative apparatus at the county level. CalWORKs is administered by California's 58 county welfare departments, each of which employs eligibility workers, employment services staff, and case managers who process applications, verify eligibility, assess participants, assign work activities, monitor participation, and impose sanctions for noncompliance. County administrative costs are approximately $700 million to $800 million per year, representing a significant share of the total program expenditure. California's county-administered welfare model means that program implementation varies across counties in ways that are not always visible in statewide data.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that a 75 percent decline in caseload while poverty declined by roughly a third is not primarily a success story. It is an exclusion story. The families who most need cash assistance have increasingly been screened out, timed out, or discouraged from applying by a program whose eligibility rules, work requirements, sanctions, and 60-month lifetime limit were designed to reduce caseloads rather than reduce poverty. The maximum grant of $1,107 per month is insufficient to cover rent in most California cities, and the families receiving it remain in deep poverty even while participating in the program. The federal block grant has lost 40 percent of its real value since 1997, and Congress has shown no interest in updating it. The $3.7 billion in federal TANF funds serves an increasingly narrow population for an increasingly limited time, while the structural poverty that created the need for welfare in the first place remains largely unaddressed. The program has not solved the problem. It has administratively reduced its own caseload.

The generous read is that CalWORKs represents a genuine compact between the state and low-income families that provides cash, childcare, employment services, and case management to families who need transitional support. California has repeatedly increased the maximum grant, from $638 in 2012 to $1,107 in 2025, recognizing that the original amounts were inadequate. The Safety Net program extends assistance beyond the 60-month federal limit for families who remain in need. The combination of CalWORKs, CalFresh, Medi-Cal, and subsidized childcare represents a multi-dimensional support system that is more comprehensive than the cash grant alone suggests. And the decline in caseload has coincided with declining poverty rates by most measures, including the Supplemental Poverty Measure, suggesting that the combination of work supports, earned income tax credits, and a sustained labor market recovery has moved families toward economic self-sufficiency. The program was designed to be temporary; a 27 percent caseload rate relative to 1998 may reflect that it is working as intended.

---

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The CalWORKs caseload data tracks the number of families receiving assistance. It does not track the number of families who are eligible for assistance but not receiving it, a figure sometimes called the "participation rate." Research has consistently found that participation rates in means-tested programs fall well below 100 percent, and that the families least likely to participate are often those with the greatest need, including those with limited English proficiency, unstable housing, and prior negative experiences with the welfare system. The data also does not track what happened to the families who left CalWORKs: whether they achieved economic stability, fell into deeper poverty, moved out of state, or became part of the unhoused population. The exit destination of 450,000 families is not visible in program data.
