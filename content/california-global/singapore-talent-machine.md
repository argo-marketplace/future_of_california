# California Global: Singapore, the Talent Machine
## What 89,000 civil servants, $700,000 ministerial salaries, a 1.7 percent corruption index, and a $382 billion sovereign fund reveal about the state that governs like a corporation and why California can't

Singapore's Public Service employs approximately 89,000 officers across 16 ministries and more than 60 statutory boards. It governs 5.9 million people on 733 square kilometers of land with no natural resources, no hinterland, and no agricultural base, and has produced a GDP per capita of approximately $87,900, the third-highest in the world. Its civil service is routinely ranked first or second globally in government effectiveness by the World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators, where it scores in the 99th percentile. Its Corruption Perceptions Index score of 83 out of 100 (fifth least corrupt in the world) reflects an anti-corruption regime so aggressive that a cabinet minister was charged and convicted in 2024 for accepting gifts worth $290,000, a sum that would not register as a scandal in most countries. California has 234,000 state employees serving 39 million people, a GDP per capita of roughly $105,000 (highest among large states), and a government effectiveness that its own residents rate as poor: a 2024 PPIC survey found that only 22 percent of Californians believe the state government does a good job. Singapore and California are roughly comparable in GDP per capita. They are not comparable in the capacity of the state to convert public resources into public outcomes. The gap is not funding. It is institutional design.

## The Talent Pipeline: Administrative Service and Shell Postings

Singapore's Administrative Service, the elite cadre within the civil service, numbers approximately 300 to 350 officers at any time. Entry is ferociously competitive: candidates are identified as early as secondary school through national exam performance, tracked through university scholarships (often to Oxford, Cambridge, Harvard, Stanford, or MIT on full government bonds), and recruited into a career structure designed to produce generalists who understand every facet of government before they lead any part of it.

An Administrative Service officer's first 15 to 20 years involve structured rotation across multiple ministries and statutory boards, typically spending two to four years in each posting. A single officer might serve in the Ministry of Trade and Industry, rotate to the Ministry of Defence, then to the Economic Development Board, then to a posting at a government-linked company, then back to a ministry as a director or deputy secretary. The rotation is not optional. It is the system. By the time an officer reaches permanent secretary rank (the highest civil service position in a ministry, equivalent to a deputy minister), they have typically served in four to six different agencies across 20 years.

The most distinctive feature is what Singapore calls "shell postings": deliberate placement of senior civil servants into government-linked companies (GLCs) and Temasek-owned enterprises for two to three years. The officer runs a commercial division, manages a P&L, hires and fires, and is evaluated on market performance. They return to government with commercial literacy that no MBA program can replicate because it was earned under real operational pressure. The reverse also occurs: private sector executives are recruited into statutory boards, often at significant pay cuts, for the prestige and policy influence that government service confers.

California's civil service has no equivalent. The state's classified civil service, governed by the State Personnel Board and civil service rules dating to 1913, uses competitive examinations, seniority-based advancement, and position classifications that make lateral movement between agencies slow and rotation between public and private sectors nearly impossible without leaving the civil service entirely. A CalTrans engineer cannot rotate to the Air Resources Board without a new examination. A Department of Water Resources manager cannot spend two years at a climate technology company and return to their position. The system was designed to prevent patronage. It has succeeded. It has also prevented the talent circulation that Singapore uses to keep its government competent.

## The Compensation Question: Paying Market Rates

Singapore's approach to civil service compensation is radical by Western standards: it pays senior officials at or near private-sector rates, benchmarked annually against the top earners in the private economy. The prime minister's salary is approximately S$2.2 million (roughly $1.6 million USD) per year, making it the highest head-of-government salary in the world. Ministers earn approximately S$935,000 to S$1.1 million ($700,000 to $825,000 USD). Permanent secretaries earn S$700,000 to S$900,000 ($525,000 to $675,000). Senior administrative officers earn S$200,000 to S$500,000 ($150,000 to $375,000).

The rationale, articulated explicitly by founding Prime Minister Lee Kuan Yew, is that government will not attract top talent if it pays half what the private sector offers. Singapore benchmarks ministerial pay to 60 percent of the median income of the top 1,000 earners in Singapore, a formula that automatically adjusts with private sector compensation. The benchmark was revised downward in 2012 after public criticism, but the principle remains: the government competes for talent on compensation, not on sacrifice.

California's compensation tells the opposite story. The governor earns $218,556 per year, governing an economy of $3.6 trillion. Cabinet secretaries earn $190,000 to $210,000. Deputy directors and senior managers earn $140,000 to $180,000. A senior software engineer at Google's Mountain View campus earns $300,000 to $500,000 in total compensation. A senior data scientist at Meta earns $350,000 to $600,000. The state's IT managers, who are responsible for systems serving 40 million residents, earn $130,000 to $170,000. The pay gap between California's public and private sectors at the senior level is approximately 60 to 70 percent, a ratio that Singapore considered unacceptable for a city-state of 5.9 million people, let alone a state economy larger than most G20 nations.

The vacancy data tells the story the compensation data implies. California's state workforce vacancy rate was approximately 21 percent as of 2024, with some departments exceeding 30 percent. The Department of Technology (CDT), responsible for the state's digital infrastructure, has chronic vacancies in engineering and cybersecurity positions. The Franchise Tax Board, which collects $100 billion in annual revenue, struggles to recruit data analysts who could earn three times more across the Bay. The civil service examination process, which can take three to six months from application to appointment, loses candidates to private sector offers made in weeks. Singapore fills Administrative Service positions within months of identification. California loses qualified candidates during the examination scoring period.

## Temasek and the GLC Model: $382 Billion in Public Equity

Temasek Holdings, founded in 1974 to manage the Singapore government's commercial assets, holds a portfolio valued at approximately S$389 billion ($382 billion USD) as of March 2024. It is wholly owned by the Singapore Ministry of Finance. It is not a sovereign wealth fund in the traditional sense (that is GIC, Singapore's other investment entity, which manages foreign reserves). Temasek is an active investor and owner of operating companies: Singapore Airlines, Singtel (telecommunications), DBS Bank (Southeast Asia's largest bank by assets), PSA International (the world's second-largest port operator), ST Engineering, and Mapletree Investments (real estate), among others.

The GLC model functions as follows: the government holds equity through Temasek, which appoints boards and sets strategic direction but does not manage day-to-day operations. The companies operate commercially, compete in markets, hire and fire based on performance, and are evaluated on return on equity. Temasek's 20-year annualized total shareholder return is approximately 7 percent, comparable to major private equity firms. The portfolio generated S$15.2 billion ($11.2 billion) in net income in fiscal year 2024.

GLCs account for approximately 37 percent of the market capitalization of the Singapore Exchange. They employ tens of thousands of Singaporeans in high-skill positions. They also serve as the "shell posting" destinations for Administrative Service officers: a permanent secretary might spend three years as a division head at Singtel or DBS, acquiring commercial experience that no government posting could provide, before returning to lead a ministry.

California has no equivalent institution. The state's investment assets are managed by CalPERS ($502 billion) and CalSTRS ($342 billion), but these are pension funds with fiduciary obligations to retirees, not development vehicles with public mission mandates. The state owns commercial assets, including port facilities, water infrastructure, and real estate, but manages them through traditional government agencies or special districts with no equity discipline, no performance benchmarking against private alternatives, and no deliberate talent circulation between the asset and the civil service.

The Port of Los Angeles is the closest California analogy. The city owns the harbor, appoints the commission, and captures the public benefit, while the port operates with commercial discipline, moving 9.9 million TEUs in 2024. But the port is a single instance, not a system. Singapore built the system: a portfolio of commercially disciplined public enterprises spanning logistics, banking, telecommunications, defense technology, and real estate, connected to the civil service through talent circulation and connected to the public interest through government ownership. California has the assets. It has not built the system.

## The Statutory Board Model: 60 Agencies That Actually Work

Singapore's statutory boards are autonomous public agencies established by act of Parliament to carry out specific government functions with more operational flexibility than ministries. There are more than 60, and they are the operational engine of the Singapore model. The Housing and Development Board (HDB) has built and manages approximately 1.1 million public housing flats housing 78 percent of Singapore's resident population. The Economic Development Board (EDB) is the investment promotion agency that has attracted more than $20 billion in annual fixed asset investment commitments in recent years. The Land Transport Authority (LTA) built and operates a 220-kilometer mass rapid transit network. The Public Utilities Board (PUB) manages the "four national taps" water strategy that has made Singapore water-independent despite having no natural freshwater sources.

Statutory boards differ from ministries in two critical ways: they have dedicated revenue streams (fees, charges, investment returns) that provide operational independence from the annual budget cycle, and they have professional boards with private-sector representation that provide governance discipline distinct from political oversight. A statutory board's CEO is typically an Administrative Service officer on rotation, bringing government perspective to a commercially structured organization. The board members include private-sector executives who bring market discipline.

The performance metrics are public and specific. HDB's resident satisfaction rate is approximately 92 percent. The MRT system operates at approximately 99.7 percent reliability (measured by mean kilometers between failures). Changi Airport, managed by Changi Airport Group (a Temasek subsidiary), has been ranked the world's best airport by Skytrax for 12 of the past 15 years. PUB has achieved a non-revenue water rate (water lost to leaks) of approximately 6 percent, compared to a global average of 30 percent and Los Angeles DWP's rate of approximately 8 percent.

California's equivalent agencies, Caltrans, the Department of Water Resources, the Employment Development Department, lack every structural feature that makes statutory boards effective: they have no dedicated revenue streams independent of the general fund, no professional boards with private-sector governance, no CEO-level Administrative Service officers on rotation, and no public performance metrics comparable to Singapore's. EDD's pandemic unemployment insurance failure, in which the department paid an estimated $31 billion in fraudulent claims, is the kind of operational catastrophe that Singapore's statutory board model is designed to prevent through professional governance and commercial accountability.

## The Constraints: What Singapore Has That California Doesn't

The comparison requires honesty about what Singapore has that California cannot replicate. Singapore is an authoritarian state with restricted press freedom (ranked 129th of 180 countries by Reporters Without Borders), limited political competition (the People's Action Party has governed continuously since 1959), and curtailed civil liberties including restrictions on assembly, speech, and judicial independence. The government's ability to implement long-range plans without political opposition, to set civil service compensation without populist backlash, and to rotate talent through government-linked companies without conflicts-of-interest accusations is inseparable from the political system that enables it.

Singapore's population of 5.9 million is one-sixth of California's. Its geographic compactness (733 square kilometers, roughly the size of San Jose) allows a centralization of governance that is impossible across California's 164,000 square miles. Its ethnic composition, while diverse (74 percent Chinese, 13 percent Malay, 9 percent Indian), exists within an institutional framework of managed multiculturalism that has no parallel in California's decentralized, litigious, democratically contested governance environment.

The tax structure is also radically different. Singapore's top personal income tax rate is 24 percent (compared to California's combined federal-state top rate of approximately 50 percent), but the government captures revenue through other mechanisms: land sales (the government owns approximately 90 percent of the land), corporate taxes at a competitive 17 percent rate that attracts global headquarters, GST (goods and services tax) at 9 percent, and Temasek's investment returns. The fiscal model is designed around asset accumulation and investment returns rather than progressive income taxation, a model that is politically and structurally impossible in California.

## The Transferable Pieces

Not everything about Singapore's model requires authoritarianism or city-state scale. Three elements are transferable, and California is uniquely positioned to adopt them.

First, talent rotation. California could create a structured fellowship program, not an internship but a genuine two-to-three-year posting, that places senior private-sector technologists, engineers, and managers into state agencies with decision-making authority, and places senior civil servants into mission-aligned companies with operational responsibility. The federal government's Presidential Innovation Fellows and the U.S. Digital Service demonstrate that this model works in democratic contexts. California's version, scaled to the state's technology talent base, could be an order of magnitude larger. The state already struggles with a 21 percent vacancy rate. Structured rotation would fill positions with people who bring skills the civil service cannot develop internally, while giving those people a career narrative that includes public service without requiring permanent sacrifice.

Second, performance-benchmarked compensation for senior roles. California does not need to pay its governor $1.6 million. But it does need to pay its chief technology officer, its chief data officer, its senior IT architects, and its agency deputy directors at rates competitive with the private sector roles those people would otherwise hold. The state could create a limited number of "strategic talent" positions, perhaps 200 to 500 across all agencies, compensated at 70 to 80 percent of private-sector equivalents and exempted from the standard civil service classification system. Singapore's Administrative Service is 300 to 350 people. California's equivalent, at proportional scale, would be 2,000 to 2,500. Even 500 would transform the senior talent pool.

Third, mission-driven public enterprises. California already has institutions that operate in the productive space between pure government and pure market: the UC system, the port authorities, certain water districts, BART. What it lacks is a deliberate framework for creating new ones where the public interest case is clear but the operational case for traditional government management is weak. Healthcare data integration, education technology infrastructure, climate monitoring systems, emergency logistics platforms: each of these is a domain where long-horizon public planning and commercial operational discipline are both necessary, and where California's existing institutional forms (state agency or private contractor) provide only one or the other.

## The California Comparison in Numbers

The statistical comparison between Singapore's government and California's crystallizes the institutional gap:

Government employees per 10,000 residents: Singapore approximately 151; California approximately 60 (state employees only; approximately 150 including local government). At the state level, California is less than half as staffed as Singapore relative to population.

Government IT spending as percentage of GDP: Singapore approximately 1.2 percent (S$4 billion of S$332 billion GDP in 2024, including Smart Nation initiatives); California approximately 0.025 percent (approximately $900 million CDT budget against $3.6 trillion GDP). Singapore spends roughly 48 times more on government technology per dollar of GDP.

Civil service vacancy rate: Singapore approximately 3 to 4 percent; California approximately 21 percent. Singapore fills positions six times more effectively.

Median time to obtain a business license: Singapore 1.5 days (ranked second globally by the World Bank's former Ease of Doing Business Index); California 30 to 90 days depending on jurisdiction and license type.

Public housing as percentage of population housed: Singapore 78 percent (1.1 million HDB flats); California approximately 2.5 percent of housing units are publicly subsidized (approximately 350,000 units for 14 million households).

Government debt as percentage of GDP: Singapore's net government debt is effectively zero or negative (sovereign wealth fund assets exceed gross debt); California's state and local government debt is approximately $510 billion, roughly 14 percent of GDP.

Corruption perception score (Transparency International, 100 = least corrupt): Singapore 83 (5th globally); United States 65 (27th globally; no state-level data is published).

## What California, Inc. Might Look Like

On one side of the Bay, engineers are redesigning how money and intelligence move through the world. On the other, state procurement offices are still faxing vendor invoices, permitting systems crash on Thursdays, and talented people leave after two years because the institutional immune system rejects anyone who moves too fast. The City of Los Angeles has effectively stopped repairing its streets. The state that invented the internet cannot fill potholes efficiently. At some point, the gap between California's civilizational ambitions and its operational reality stops being ironic and starts being inexcusable.

Singapore offers a provocation, not a blueprint. A city-state of 5.9 million people with no democratic accountability cannot be transplanted to a continental democracy of 39 million. But the mechanisms, talent rotation, competitive compensation for senior roles, and commercially disciplined public enterprises, are not inherently authoritarian. They are institutional design choices that California has the resources, the talent base, and the economic scale to implement more ambitiously than any other American state.

The state that gave the world the internet, the smartphone, the search engine, the electric vehicle, and the large language model governs itself with a civil service designed in 1913, a procurement system that takes 18 months to buy software, and a compensation structure that values a senior state IT manager at one-third of what their Stanford classmate earns across the street. Singapore's 89,000 civil servants, operating at 99th-percentile effectiveness with 3 percent vacancy rates and $700,000 ministerial salaries, did not achieve that performance through cultural superiority or authoritarian magic. They achieved it through deliberate institutional design: recruiting the best, paying them competitively, rotating them through commercial and government roles, and holding them accountable to public performance metrics. California has the talent. It has the money. It has the institutions that could serve as foundations. What it does not have, and what Singapore decided to build 60 years ago, is the political will to treat government capacity as an investment rather than an expense.
