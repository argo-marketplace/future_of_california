# California Global: London, the Transit Metropolis
## What 1.4 billion annual Tube rides, a $23 congestion charge, 86 percent farebox recovery, and a single transport authority reveal about the organizational idea Los Angeles has spent 50 years avoiding

London moves 9.2 million people per day on a unified public transit network governed by a single authority. Los Angeles moves approximately 900,000. London's population is 9.7 million. Los Angeles County's is 9.6 million. The populations are nearly identical. The transit outcomes are not. The London Underground alone, a system that opened in 1863, carries approximately 1.4 billion passenger journeys per year across 272 stations and 402 kilometers of track. LA Metro, the combined rail and bus network, carries approximately 280 million annual boardings, a number that has not recovered to its pre-pandemic level of 340 million and is roughly one-fifth of the Underground's ridership alone. Add London's buses (1.7 billion annual journeys), Overground (190 million), Docklands Light Railway (130 million), Elizabeth Line (175 million in its first full year), trams, river buses, and cycling infrastructure, and Transport for London moves approximately 3.6 billion passenger journeys per year. Los Angeles County's entire transit system, across all modes and operators, moves approximately 350 million. London carries ten times the passengers with the same population. The difference is not geography, culture, or climate. It is institutional design.

## Transport for London: One Authority, One Network

Transport for London was created in 2000 as part of the Greater London Authority Act, consolidating the management of buses, Underground, roads, cycling, river services, and traffic management under a single authority accountable to the directly elected Mayor of London. Before TfL, London's transport was fragmented across London Underground Limited, London Buses, the Docklands Light Railway, various highway authorities, and the Department for Transport. The consolidation placed network planning, fare integration, capital investment, and operational management under one roof.

TfL's annual budget is approximately 11.7 billion pounds ($14.8 billion). Its revenue comes from three primary sources: fares (approximately 5.6 billion pounds, representing 86 percent of operating costs for the Underground, the highest farebox recovery ratio of any major metro system in the world), congestion charge and road user fees (approximately 600 million pounds), and government grants and borrowing (the remainder). The farebox recovery ratio is the critical number: London's transit system covers 86 percent of its operating costs from rider fares. LA Metro's farebox recovery is approximately 18 to 22 percent, meaning taxpayer subsidies cover roughly 80 percent of operating costs.

The fare integration is seamless. A single contactless payment card (Oyster) or bank card works across every TfL mode: Tube, buses, Overground, DLR, Elizabeth Line, trams, and river buses. Daily and weekly fare caps automatically apply, so riders never pay more than the equivalent of a day pass or week pass regardless of how many trips they take. The integration means that transferring between bus and Tube is a single transaction, not two separate fares. LA Metro's TAP card works across Metro-operated services but does not integrate with the 25 other transit operators in Los Angeles County (including LADOT, Santa Monica Big Blue Bus, Foothill Transit, Long Beach Transit, and Metrolink), each of which has its own fare structure, its own governance board, and its own route planning.

The 25-operator fragmentation is the structural problem. Los Angeles County has more independent transit agencies than any comparable metropolitan area in the world. Each agency plans routes to serve its own jurisdiction, not the network. Service gaps at jurisdictional boundaries are common. Fare penalties for transfers between operators discourage multi-modal trips. Route duplication wastes resources. And no single authority has the power to redesign the network as a network rather than a collection of municipal bus companies that happen to share a county.

## The Congestion Charge: Pricing Road Space

London introduced the Congestion Charge in February 2003, charging vehicles 5 pounds (later raised to 15 pounds, currently 15 pounds, approximately $19) to enter central London between 7 a.m. and 6 p.m. on weekdays. The charge was implemented under the authority of the Mayor, using powers granted by the Greater London Authority Act, without requiring a public referendum. Mayor Ken Livingstone campaigned on the charge, won election, and implemented it within three years.

The effects were immediate and measurable. Traffic entering the congestion zone fell by approximately 18 percent in the first year. Average traffic speeds in central London increased by 30 percent. Bus ridership in the zone increased by 37 percent. Cycling increased by 43 percent over the following decade. Annual revenue from the charge, approximately 220 to 260 million pounds after operating costs, was reinvested in bus service improvements.

In 2019, London introduced the Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ), initially covering the same central area as the congestion charge, then expanded in 2021 to inner London and in 2023 to all of Greater London. ULEZ charges vehicles that do not meet emission standards (generally Euro 6 for diesel, Euro 4 for petrol) 12.50 pounds per day. The expansion to outer London was politically contentious, with protests, legal challenges, and vandalism of enforcement cameras. Mayor Sadiq Khan proceeded despite losing a suburban by-election widely attributed to ULEZ opposition. The public health data supported his position: a 2024 Imperial College London study found that ULEZ reduced nitrogen dioxide concentrations by 20 to 44 percent in the expanded zone and was associated with measurable improvements in childhood asthma outcomes.

Los Angeles has discussed congestion pricing for more than two decades. Metro's traffic reduction study, the Metro ExpressLanes program on the I-10 and I-110 freeways, charges solo drivers $1 to $14 for access to HOV lanes based on real-time congestion levels. The program generates approximately $50 to $60 million per year. But a cordon congestion charge for downtown Los Angeles, comparable to London's, has never advanced beyond the study phase. The political obstacles are formidable: Los Angeles has no unified governance authority equivalent to the Mayor of London (the city mayor has no jurisdiction over unincorporated county areas or the 87 other incorporated cities), no transit alternative comprehensive enough to absorb drivers who switch modes (London's Tube carried the displaced drivers; LA has no equivalent), and a political culture that treats road pricing as a tax on the working class rather than an efficiency measure. London implemented congestion pricing by executive authority of a mayor governing a unified metropolitan area. Los Angeles cannot implement it because no one governs Los Angeles as a unified metropolitan area.

## The Elizabeth Line: How to Deliver a Megaproject

The Elizabeth Line (Crossrail), which opened in stages between May and November 2022, is a 118-kilometer railway running east-west across London, connecting Reading and Heathrow in the west to Shenfield and Abbey Wood in the east, with 42 kilometers of new twin-bore tunnel beneath central London. The project cost approximately 18.9 billion pounds ($24 billion), roughly double its original 2010 budget of 9.8 billion pounds. It was delivered four years late (originally scheduled for December 2018).

By the standards of London megaprojects, the overruns were significant. By the standards of California megaprojects, they were modest. The Elizabeth Line was delivered at approximately $200 million per kilometer of new tunnel. California's high-speed rail Central Valley segment, which involves no tunneling, is costing approximately $275 million per kilometer. The Elizabeth Line carries 175 million passenger journeys in its first full year and has increased central London's rail capacity by 10 percent. It connects Heathrow Airport to central London in approximately 30 minutes, versus the previous 45 to 60 minutes by Underground.

The institutional structure that delivered the Elizabeth Line involved a dedicated delivery body (Crossrail Limited), a hybrid public-private governance board, a funding package combining national government grants, London business rate supplements, and TfL contributions, and a procurement model that broke the tunneling and station construction into packages awarded to experienced international contractors. The delivery body had a defined scope, a defined budget, a defined timeline, and authority to make procurement and design decisions without reverting to political bodies for approval on every change order.

LA Metro's current megaprojects, the Purple Line Extension to UCLA, the Regional Connector, the Crenshaw/LAX Line, and the East San Fernando Valley Light Rail, face a different institutional reality. Each project requires environmental review under CEQA and NEPA (the Elizabeth Line required environmental review under UK planning law, but the process took approximately two years, not the five to ten that California projects typically require). Each requires coordination with multiple jurisdictions, utilities, and stakeholders. Each is governed by the Metro Board of Directors, a 14-member body composed of Los Angeles County supervisors, the mayor of Los Angeles, and city appointees whose political priorities may not align with project delivery timelines. The Regional Connector, a 1.9-mile downtown tunnel, cost $1.76 billion and took nine years to build. The Elizabeth Line built 42 kilometers of tunnel beneath one of the most densely built and historically sensitive cities in the world for 18.9 billion pounds. The per-mile cost comparison favors London by a significant margin.

## Cycling and the Reallocation of Road Space

London has tripled cycling trips since 2000, from approximately 300,000 to over 1 million daily cycling journeys, through a combination of dedicated infrastructure investment, road space reallocation, and demand management (the congestion charge made driving more expensive, making cycling relatively more attractive). The Santander Cycles bike-share system, launched in 2010, has approximately 12,000 bikes at 800 docking stations. Cycling infrastructure spending increased from approximately 10 million pounds per year in 2010 to over 260 million per year by 2024, reflecting a commitment by successive mayors to make cycling a viable commuting mode.

The physical infrastructure is visible: separated cycle lanes (called "Cycleways") on major routes, low-traffic neighborhoods that restrict through-traffic on residential streets, and cycle superhighways connecting outer boroughs to central London. The Embankment Cycleway, opened in 2016, carries approximately 6,500 cyclists per day on a segregated lane along the Thames that replaced a lane of motor vehicle traffic. The political cost of reallocating road space from cars to bicycles was borne by elected mayors who judged that the long-term benefits (reduced congestion, improved air quality, public health) outweighed the short-term opposition from motorists.

Los Angeles has approximately 500 miles of bike lanes, of which roughly 40 to 50 miles are physically protected (separated from traffic by barriers rather than painted lines). The city's 2010 Bicycle Plan called for 1,684 miles of bikeways by 2035. Progress has been slow: approximately 200 miles were added between 2010 and 2024. The cycling mode share in Los Angeles is approximately 1 percent of commute trips, compared to London's approximately 7 percent and Amsterdam's 36 percent. The gap reflects both infrastructure deficiency and the sprawling geography that makes cycling impractical for many Los Angeles commuters, but the infrastructure deficiency is the addressable variable. London is not compact. Its outer boroughs are suburban in character, with commute distances comparable to many Los Angeles neighborhoods. The difference is that London invested in cycling infrastructure as part of a unified transport strategy. Los Angeles treats cycling infrastructure as an afterthought appended to road projects.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that Los Angeles had every opportunity to build a transit metropolis and chose the freeway instead, a decision that is now irreversible at any affordable cost. The county's 25-operator fragmentation makes network planning impossible. The absence of congestion pricing means that driving remains artificially cheap relative to transit, suppressing ridership on a system that needs riders to justify further investment. Metro's $52 billion Measure M capital program is building rail lines that will carry a fraction of the riders that comparable London investments carry, because the land use patterns around Los Angeles stations are too low-density to generate walk-up ridership, and the bus network that should feed the rail stations is operated by separate agencies with no coordination mandate. London moved 3.6 billion passengers last year because it built a system. Los Angeles moved 350 million because it built a collection of projects.

The generous read is that Los Angeles is attempting, in a single generation, what London built over 160 years, and the trajectory is positive. The combined Metro rail network, which did not exist before 1990, now spans 105 miles and is expanding rapidly toward a 2028 Olympics opening deadline. Measure R (2008) and Measure M (2016) represent the largest local transit investment in American history, backed by voter-approved sales tax increases that demonstrate genuine public demand for alternatives to driving. The Regional Connector, completed in 2023, created the first through-running rail service in Los Angeles, eliminating the downtown transfer penalty that suppressed ridership. The NextGen Bus Plan, adopted in 2020, represented Metro's first attempt to redesign the bus network as a network rather than a collection of inherited routes. Los Angeles will never be London: its geography, density, and development history preclude it. But a Los Angeles with 200 miles of rail, a redesigned bus network, congestion pricing on its freeways, and a unified fare structure across operators would be a fundamentally different city than the one that exists today. Most of those elements are funded, under construction, or under active development. The transformation is underway. It is just 100 years behind London's.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

TfL publishes ridership data, financial reports, service performance metrics, and journey time data at a granularity that has no equivalent in Los Angeles. Metro publishes ridership and financial data but does not routinely publish door-to-door journey time comparisons against driving for specific origin-destination pairs, the metric that matters most to potential riders deciding between modes. The data that would reveal whether LA Metro is competitive with driving for any given commute, accounting for walk time, wait time, transfer time, and travel time, is not systematically collected or published.

The data does not capture the equity dimensions of transit access in Los Angeles. Metro's rail investments have disproportionately served the Westside (the Purple Line Extension to Beverly Hills and UCLA) and downtown (the Regional Connector), while bus service, which is the primary mode for low-income riders and riders of color, has been subject to service cuts during budget constraints. The Bus Riders Union successfully sued Metro in 1996 (Labor/Community Strategy Center v. MTA) for discriminatory service reductions, resulting in a consent decree that required minimum bus service levels. The decree expired in 2006. Bus service hours have fluctuated since. The riders who depend most on transit, the riders who have no alternative, are the riders whose service is most vulnerable to budget pressure.

The data also does not capture what London's model costs in housing. London's transit investment has been capitalized into property values: homes within walking distance of Tube stations command premiums of 10 to 20 percent over otherwise comparable properties. The Elizabeth Line increased property values along its corridor by an estimated 10.5 billion pounds before it opened. Transit-accessible neighborhoods in London are expensive precisely because transit access is valuable, a dynamic that pushes lower-income residents to outer boroughs with longer commutes. Los Angeles faces the same risk: the rail-adjacent neighborhoods that transit investment is intended to serve may become unaffordable to the residents who need transit most. London has not solved this problem. Neither will Los Angeles, unless it couples transit investment with housing policy that preserves affordability near stations.

---

London is not a model Los Angeles can replicate. It is a model Los Angeles can study for the institutional insight that matters most: a metropolitan transit system works when a single authority governs the network, fares are integrated, road pricing makes driving reflect its true cost, and capital investment is sustained over decades rather than lurching between ballot measures. London built the Underground over 160 years, the bus network over a century, and TfL over 25 years. Los Angeles is attempting all three simultaneously, with 25 independent operators, no congestion pricing, and a governance structure that substitutes county supervisors for a transit-empowered mayor. The engineering is hard. The engineering is also solvable. What is harder, and what London solved by creating TfL, is the institutional question: who governs the network? In London, the answer is one authority accountable to one elected official. In Los Angeles, the answer is everyone and no one, which in practice means the car.
