# California Global: Scotland, the Devolution Dividend
## What 25 years of devolved power, a $250 billion GDP, free university tuition, and the Barnett Formula reveal about what happens when a rich region inside a larger union decides to govern itself

Scotland has 5.4 million people, a GDP of approximately $250 billion, and since 1999 has operated its own parliament with devolved authority over health, education, justice, housing, transport, agriculture, environment, and most domestic policy. It remains part of the United Kingdom for defense, foreign affairs, immigration, monetary policy, and the core fiscal framework. The devolution arrangement, established by the Scotland Act 1998 and expanded by the Scotland Acts 2012 and 2016, gives Scotland more domestic policy autonomy than any U.S. state has relative to Washington, while keeping it inside a fiscal and monetary union it did not design. California has 39 million people, a GDP of approximately $3.6 trillion, and domestic policy authority that is in many areas narrower than Scotland's, despite having an economy 14 times larger and a population seven times greater. California cannot set its own immigration policy, has limited flexibility on healthcare design, operates within a federal tax system that transfers approximately $12 billion per year more to Washington than the state receives back, and faces federal preemption on issues from drug policy to environmental regulation. The Scottish experiment in devolution offers a provocation: what would California look like if it governed with the domestic autonomy that a nation of 5.4 million already exercises?

---

## The Devolution Settlement: What Scotland Controls

The Scotland Act 1998 established the Scottish Parliament at Holyrood with legislative competence over all matters not explicitly "reserved" to the Westminster Parliament. Reserved matters include the constitution, defense, foreign affairs, immigration, the currency, social security (partially devolved since 2016), and broadcasting. Everything else is devolved by default, a structural principle called "the reserved powers model" that gives Scotland authority over any new policy area that is not specifically claimed by Westminster.

The practical scope is vast. Scotland sets its own policy on:

Education: Scotland operates a separate education system with its own curriculum (Curriculum for Excellence), its own examination body (the Scottish Qualifications Authority), its own teaching standards, and, most consequentially, its own university tuition policy. Scottish students attending Scottish universities pay no tuition. English students attending Scottish universities pay up to 9,250 pounds per year. The policy costs approximately 2 billion pounds annually in foregone revenue and represents the single most visible policy divergence from England.

Health: NHS Scotland is a separate institution from NHS England, with its own governance, its own budget (approximately 19 billion pounds per year, roughly 42 percent of the Scottish Government's total budget), and its own policy decisions. Scotland abolished prescription charges entirely in 2011 (England charges 9.90 pounds per item). Scotland has separate policies on alcohol pricing (minimum unit pricing of 65 pence per unit, the first such policy in the UK), drug treatment, mental health, and social care integration.

Justice: Scotland has its own legal system, distinct from England and Wales, with its own courts, its own criminal law, its own prosecution service (the Crown Office and Procurator Fiscal Service), and its own police force (Police Scotland, created in 2013 by merging eight regional forces into one). Scots law is a hybrid system combining elements of civil law (Roman-derived, similar to continental European systems) and common law, distinct from the purely common law system of England.

Taxation: The Scotland Acts 2012 and 2016 devolved significant tax powers. The Scottish Parliament sets its own income tax rates and bands for earned income (though not savings or dividend income). Scotland currently operates a five-band system with rates of 19 percent (starter), 20 percent (basic), 21 percent (intermediate), 42 percent (higher), and 47 percent (top rate on income above 125,140 pounds). The top rate is three percentage points higher than England's 45 percent, generating approximately 80 million pounds in additional revenue. Land and Buildings Transaction Tax (Scotland's equivalent of stamp duty) is devolved. Air Departure Tax is devolved but not yet implemented. Approximately 40 percent of Scottish Government revenue is now raised in Scotland, up from near zero before 2016.

## The Barnett Formula: Fiscal Transfers Without Representation

Scotland's fiscal relationship with the UK is governed by the Barnett Formula, a population-based allocation mechanism created in 1978 by Chief Secretary to the Treasury Joel Barnett as a temporary expedient. It has never been revised. The formula allocates changes in UK government spending to Scotland (and Wales and Northern Ireland) based on population share: if England's health budget increases by 1 billion pounds, Scotland receives approximately 175 million pounds (its roughly 8.1 percent population share, adjusted for which powers are devolved). The formula does not consider need, cost of delivery, or regional economic conditions. It is a mechanical calculation that has, over 47 years, produced per-capita public spending in Scotland approximately 20 to 25 percent higher than the English average: roughly 14,100 pounds per person in Scotland versus 11,500 in England (2024-25 figures).

The higher spending is politically contentious. English politicians periodically argue that Scotland is subsidized. Scottish politicians counter that Scotland contributes significant tax revenue (including North Sea oil revenue, which peaked at approximately 12 billion pounds per year in the early 2000s but has declined to approximately 3 to 5 billion as fields mature) and that the higher spending reflects higher costs of service delivery in a geographically dispersed, partially rural, partially remote population. The Government Expenditure and Revenue Scotland (GERS) report, published annually, shows Scotland running a notional fiscal deficit of approximately 8 to 12 percent of GDP in recent years, significantly higher than the UK as a whole (approximately 4 to 5 percent). This deficit is notional because Scotland does not borrow independently; the spending is funded through the Barnett Formula and devolved taxation, with the deficit effectively absorbed by the UK fiscal framework.

California's fiscal relationship with the federal government operates in reverse. California is a net contributor to the federal treasury, sending approximately $12 billion more per year in federal taxes than it receives in federal spending. New York, New Jersey, Connecticut, and Massachusetts are in similar positions. The transfers flow from high-income, high-cost states to lower-income states through Medicare, Medicaid, military spending, agricultural subsidies, and federal retirement benefits. Unlike Scotland, which receives a fiscal premium for its membership in the union, California pays a fiscal penalty for its membership. The state receives approximately $0.96 in federal spending for every $1.00 it sends to Washington, a ratio that has been negative for decades.

## Policy Divergence: What Devolution Produced

Twenty-five years of devolved government have produced measurable policy divergence between Scotland and England, providing natural experiments in domestic policy that a unitary state cannot.

Tuition: Scotland's free tuition policy, maintained by every Scottish Government since devolution, has produced a university participation rate of approximately 55 percent (among the highest in Europe) and eliminated tuition debt for Scottish-domiciled students. The policy has not, however, closed the attainment gap between advantaged and disadvantaged students as much as proponents hoped: students from the most deprived areas are still roughly half as likely to attend university as those from the least deprived. The policy's primary beneficiary is the broad middle class, which in England accumulates 30,000 to 50,000 pounds in student debt.

Healthcare outcomes: NHS Scotland's performance on waiting times, a key political metric, has been mixed. The target of 95 percent of patients seen within four hours at emergency departments was met consistently before the pandemic but has deteriorated to approximately 65 to 70 percent post-pandemic, comparable to England's similar decline. Cancer treatment waiting times, mental health access, and GP availability show similar patterns: Scotland performs comparably to England on most health metrics, slightly better on some (drug-related death response, alcohol minimum pricing outcomes), and slightly worse on others (dental access, rural service delivery).

Minimum unit pricing: Scotland's 2018 introduction of minimum unit pricing for alcohol (initially 50 pence per unit, raised to 65 pence in 2024) has been evaluated by Public Health Scotland and the University of Sheffield. The evaluation found a 13 percent reduction in alcohol-related deaths in the first three years compared to the counterfactual, and a 7 percent reduction in alcohol-related hospitalizations. The policy disproportionately reduced consumption among the heaviest drinkers, who purchase the cheapest alcohol most affected by the minimum price. England has not adopted minimum unit pricing despite the Scottish evidence. Wales has indicated interest. The Scottish experiment provided evidence that would not exist without devolution.

Childcare: Scotland implemented a policy of 1,140 hours per year of funded early learning and childcare for all three- and four-year-olds, approximately 30 hours per week during term time, significantly more generous than England's 30 hours (available only to working parents of three- and four-year-olds) or the 15 hours available universally. The cost is approximately 1 billion pounds per year. California's Transitional Kindergarten expansion, which aims to provide universal pre-K for four-year-olds by 2025-26, is the American policy closest to Scotland's model but is funded through K-12 education budgets rather than a dedicated childcare entitlement.

## The Independence Question: What Devolution Didn't Resolve

Scotland held an independence referendum on September 18, 2014. The question was: "Should Scotland be an independent country?" The result was 55.3 percent No, 44.7 percent Yes, on a turnout of 84.6 percent, the highest for any UK electoral event in modern history.

The referendum was held under an agreement between the Scottish Government and the UK Government (the Edinburgh Agreement), which established the terms, the question, and the legal framework for a binding vote. The UK Government agreed that it would respect the result. Had Scotland voted Yes, it would have become an independent state, left the UK, and applied for EU membership (a process that the 2016 Brexit vote subsequently complicated, since Scotland voted 62 percent to remain in the EU while the UK as a whole voted to leave).

The independence movement did not end with the 2014 vote. The SNP (Scottish National Party), which governed Scotland from 2007 to 2024, maintained that Brexit constituted a material change in circumstances that justified a second referendum. The UK Government refused to grant one. The UK Supreme Court ruled in November 2022 that the Scottish Parliament could not unilaterally legislate for a referendum without Westminster's consent. The SNP's political fortunes subsequently declined: it lost its overall majority in the Scottish Parliament and experienced leadership turmoil, and the question of a second referendum has receded from immediate political viability.

The independence debate illuminates the subsidiarity question for California. Scotland's devolution was achieved through a constitutional settlement that required the consent of the central government. California's policy autonomy exists within a federal framework that is simultaneously more permissive (states have broader inherent powers under the Tenth Amendment than Scotland had before 1998) and more restrictive (federal preemption, the Supremacy Clause, and the practical dominance of federal spending in healthcare, defense, and social security constrain state policy in ways that devolution does not constrain Scotland). California cannot hold a binding independence referendum, and essentially no serious political movement advocates for one. But the subsidiarity question, how much domestic policy should a large, wealthy region within a larger union control, is identical.

## The California Subsidiarity Case

California's policy autonomy, while substantial by American standards, is narrower than Scotland's devolution settlement in several critical domains.

Immigration: Scotland cannot set its own immigration policy (immigration is reserved to Westminster), but has lobbied for and partially achieved a distinct visa route (the former Fresh Talent scheme, now replaced by UK-wide visa categories). California has no immigration authority whatsoever. It cannot issue work permits, set visa categories, or adjust the flow of labor to meet economic needs. The state's agricultural, technology, and healthcare sectors all depend on immigration flows that California has no power to influence. A devolution framework that gave California input into immigration quotas, comparable to Canada's Provincial Nominee Program, would allow the state to address labor shortages that federal immigration policy does not.

Healthcare: Scotland runs its own NHS. California operates Medi-Cal within federal Medicaid rules, administers the ACA marketplace (Covered California), and has policy flexibility on eligibility, benefits, and delivery models, but cannot deviate from the fundamental federal framework. California's recent expansions of Medi-Cal to undocumented adults and the CalAIM reform required federal waivers, a process that involves lengthy negotiation with CMS and can be reversed by a change in presidential administration. Scotland implements health policy changes through its own parliament without requiring approval from London.

Fiscal policy: Scotland now raises approximately 40 percent of its own revenue and has latitude to set income tax rates. California raises approximately 65 percent of its spending from own-source revenue (state taxes and fees) but has no influence over federal tax rates that apply to its residents. California cannot offer a lower federal tax rate to attract or retain workers. It cannot adjust the SALT deduction cap (imposed by the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act) that disproportionately affects high-income California taxpayers. Scotland's fiscal devolution, while incomplete, gives it tools to differentiate its tax environment. California's fiscal relationship with Washington is fixed by federal law.

Drug policy: Scotland has among the highest drug-related death rates in Europe and has pursued distinctive approaches including supervised drug consumption facilities (approved by the Scottish Government in 2024 after years of legal debate with Westminster over reserved drug classification powers). California has pioneered cannabis legalization but cannot reschedule controlled substances, authorize safe consumption sites without federal approval (San Francisco obtained a limited exemption), or implement the drug policy approaches that its public health professionals recommend without navigating federal preemption.

## The Interpretive Tension

The critical read is that devolution gave Scotland the apparatus of self-government without the fiscal foundation to sustain it. Scotland runs a notional deficit of 8 to 12 percent of GDP. Its policy divergences, while real, are funded by the Barnett Formula premium that English taxpayers increasingly resent. Free tuition, free prescriptions, and generous childcare are politically popular and fiscally dependent on transfers from a larger economy. The independence referendum revealed that a majority of Scots, when confronted with the fiscal reality of self-governance, chose the security of the union. Devolution is governance without consequences: the Scottish Parliament can spend more generously than England because it does not bear the full fiscal cost of its choices. California already bears more than the full cost of its choices (it is a net federal donor) and has less domestic policy autonomy than Scotland. The devolution model offers California a mirror, not a blueprint: a picture of what asymmetric autonomy looks like when funded by someone else, and a reminder that California's version would have to be self-funded.

The generous read is that Scotland's devolution has produced 25 years of democratic experimentation, policy innovation, and evidence generation that would not exist without it. Minimum unit pricing, which reduces alcohol-related deaths by 13 percent, would never have been tested at UK level. Free tuition, whatever its distributional limitations, eliminated a major source of household debt and anxiety for an entire generation of Scottish students. NHS Scotland's separate governance allowed it to pursue integration of health and social care (through Integration Joint Boards, established in 2014) five years before England attempted similar reforms. The devolution model demonstrates that policy diversity within a larger union produces better outcomes than uniformity, because it allows smaller jurisdictions to test approaches that risk-averse central governments will not. California, with its $3.6 trillion economy and its tradition of policy innovation (vehicle emissions standards, cannabis legalization, CEQA, cap-and-trade), is already the American analog of Scotland's policy laboratory. The difference is that Scotland's laboratory has formal autonomy and can implement without federal permission. California's laboratory operates on federal sufferance, one Supreme Court decision or presidential executive order away from having its experiments overridden.

## What the Data Doesn't Show

The Government Expenditure and Revenue Scotland (GERS) report, the primary fiscal document, attributes revenue and spending to Scotland using a combination of direct allocation and population-share estimation. The methodology is contested: independence supporters argue that GERS understates Scottish revenue (particularly from North Sea oil and economic activity attributed to UK-wide entities headquartered in London) and overstates spending (by allocating Scotland a population share of UK-wide costs like defense that Scotland might not incur as an independent state). Unionists argue that GERS is the best available data and that its conclusions, a persistent fiscal deficit, are robust.

The data does not capture the counterfactual: what Scotland's economy and public finances would look like with full independence, full EU membership, full immigration control, and full fiscal sovereignty. The 2014 independence white paper projected that an independent Scotland would achieve fiscal balance through oil revenue, economic growth, and policy choices not available within the UK. The subsequent collapse in oil prices undermined those projections. Whether Scotland would thrive or struggle as an independent small European state, comparable to Denmark (population 5.9 million, GDP $400 billion) or Norway (population 5.4 million, GDP $580 billion), is unknowable because the experiment has not been run.

The data also does not capture what California would gain or lose from a devolution-style arrangement within the American federal system. A formal subsidiarity framework, giving California control over immigration policy, healthcare design, drug policy, and a larger share of federal tax revenue in exchange for reduced federal transfers, would represent the most significant restructuring of American federalism since the New Deal. The political feasibility is near zero. The intellectual case is strong. And the Scottish example demonstrates that devolution, once granted, produces democratic engagement (Scotland's turnout in devolved elections averages 55 to 60 percent, higher than California's midterm average), policy innovation, and institutional capacity that the pre-devolution arrangement suppressed. Whether California needs devolution or simply needs to use the autonomy it already has more aggressively is the question the data cannot answer, because it is a question about political imagination, not fiscal arithmetic.

---

Scotland asked a question in 1997 that California has never asked: should this region govern its own domestic affairs, even if it remains part of a larger union? The Scottish answer, delivered by a 74 percent vote in the devolution referendum, was yes. The subsequent 25 years produced a parliament, a policy laboratory, a fiscal reckoning, and an independence movement that came within 11 percentage points of dissolving a 300-year-old union. California has not asked the question because the American constitutional framework does not invite it: states have inherent powers, but those powers are increasingly constrained by federal preemption, unfunded mandates, and a fiscal transfer system that taxes California's prosperity and redistributes it to states that critique California's governance. Scotland's devolution is imperfect, fiscally dependent, and politically contentious. It is also the closest existing model for what a large, wealthy, culturally distinctive region can achieve within a larger union when it decides to take domestic governance seriously. California already has the economy, the population, and the policy ambition. What it lacks is the formal autonomy to match, and the political framework to demand it.
